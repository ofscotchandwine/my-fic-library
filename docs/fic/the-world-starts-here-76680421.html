    “Hate this dish? Not likely. Taste is both psychological and physiological. We’ve experienced liking everything, and even if Zosia had grown tired of this, we’d still find something to love about it.” She said this with devotion.

    “Hm,” Carol said, thinking down her rolodex of questions. “What about the whole ‘respecting a body’s preference’ thing?” Carol heard the words as she spoke, but was nevertheless helpless to stop them.

    “This body is all we have.”

    Carol cleared her throat, cleared away the heft of that comment, a brick falling to earth.

    Small talk, it seemed, was not flowing as easy as the wine, so Carol topped off her glass. Scales recalibrated.

    “We should go somewhere,” said Carol through breath after having taken several healthy gulps.

    Zosia dabbed sweat away from her brow with a forearm. The gołąbki were steaming, and air turned sticky around them. Zosia said, “Go where?”

    “Anywhere. Anywhere that’s not here. Anywhere you want.” She waited a moment, using the pause to fish for opinions, ideas, proposals. When none came, she added, chuckling, “There’s nowhere you wanna go?”

    “We like it here.”

    “Why?”

    “You’re here.”

    “I’d go with you, obviously.”

    “It’s nice here.”

    Carol’s eyebrows went up and then down. With her tongue, she felt molars, cheek. No words came now. She drank more wine.

    It was unyielding, the reminders of all that was to come, of all that was already here, fucking things every which way.

    “Let me know when dinner’s done. I’ll wait in the living room.”

    Then, she swiped the bottle from the counter with a severity that made Zosia’s hands still, and left to go wait in the living room.

     

     

    —

     

     

    A lightness returned to Carol’s mood the longer she drank and stared at walls: Blank things that held inside them so much misery and love alike, and yet would turn to rubble and ash and soil sometime soon. Maybe in five-hundred years, maybe in five days, but they’d turn to something that wasn’t what they were right now as Carol saw them. Existence, the tremendous state of it, would persist, past the point in which a person could experience it.

    For one reason or another (perhaps the wine or the inherent hilarity of being fragile in an endlessly punishing world, probably both), Carol felt better knowing this. It was a welcome shift to be certain of something, for once. The comfort of which brought Carol upstairs, to her office, her computer.

    She opened the thing, filled with drafts and decent ideas and scrapped ideas, and ran her fingers over the worn keys. The keys seemed to caress her right back. They called her to sit, so she sat, and opened a blank document, which she titled after a second’s thought, An Unremarkable Account of the World.

     

     

    —

     

     

    Zosia called her down to eat after Carol had already been writing unhindered for several pages. Words and story fell through her fingertips from her head, inevitable, like sand in an hourglass. Sentences populated the screen, then paragraphs, then pages and pages and pages. She drank and wrote and became a sort of machine, efficient at performing exactly those two tasks. And so, it had to be the third time Zosia called that Carol actually heard her.

    “Coming!” said Carol to air at her right, eyes still on the work.

    She felt warmth touch her shoulders, and it softened her but only just.

    “What are you writing?” said Zosia, and she kissed Carol’s head like she’d done this before.

    “I’ll be down in a minute.”

    “You can take it with you.” Zosia rubbed her thumbs into Carol’s tense neck. She made her voice smooth. “Can we read it?”

    “Yeah, yes, one sec, I’ll be down.” Then, lucidity approaching with lag, she looked up to Zosia, hearing her, and couldn’t quite help the smile. “And you fucking better.”

    After dinner, which Carol enjoyed too much having asked for a second and third serving, they sat in the living room, thighs touching and careless on the couch.

    Carol kept writing and sipped more conservatively her wine. Zosia had her nose in Encyclopedia D. From time to time, Zosia would say out loud a fun fact.

    “Have you ever heard about Diplozoon paradoxum?” Zosia said. “It’s a parasitic flatworm that lives inside some freshwater fish, right on their gills. But, they must physically fuse with a mate in order to reach adulthood. They stay connected like this for life.”

    Only half-listening, Carol said, “No kidding.”

    “Yes. If a diporpa fails to pair, they die. Says so right here, see?” Zosia indicated to the page and kept her finger there, nudging Carol with a shoulder.

    Carol pulled her focus from the screen and followed Zosia’s pointing. “Ah. That’s… unsettling.”

    “Or romantic.”

    “Hah.”

    They resumed their respective writing and reading and occasional sipping, though Zosia shook her head no every time Carol proffered more wine.

    The living room was in quiet that felt, to Carol, good and full. If Carol were paying attention, she might have noticed that for Zosia, the quiet was bloated with unease, with something that ached.

    Still in her book, Zosia said, timid, “Can we ask you a question?”

    “‘Course. Go ahead.” Carol continued her typing. Click click click, clack clack clack.

    “If you had to choose between death or joining, which would you pick?”

    Click click—Carol stopped. There was a thundering in the room now. Zosia had gathered her discomfort into something big and solid and dropped it like a bomb, right in the living room, right on Carol. “I take it we’re not talking worms anymore?”

    “We suspect we know the answer. But then you’ve asked us what it’s like. We’re just curious about your thinking. If it’s changed at all.”

    Too quickly, too forcefully, Carol answered, “It hasn’t.” The echo of it soured in her mouth. She sighed and swallowed more wine and then looked at Zosia, whose face was unmoving and careful.

    With another swig, Carol began the delicate process of defusing. “I’m glad I asked about it. Really. And thank you for sharing all that you could remember. And I’ll admit, it doesn’t sound as mephistophelean as I pictured in the beginning. But it’s not… The question’s sorta redundant, no? I mean, I’d be gone either way.”

    “So death, then.”

    Carol shrugged, noncommittal. “Sure.”

    “We just think that’s unwise.”

    “Unwise?”

    “It doesn’t make sense to choose that.”

    “It doesn’t make sense to willingly starve to death because you can’t shuck a goddamn ear of corn. So. Birds of a feather, I guess.”

    For a second, that seemed to be the end of it. Carol looked to her screen, though her fingers typed nothing. Zosia’s focus was back inside her book. “We can shuck corn,” she said, matter-of-factly. And then, “But if you die, as you are, then that’s that. You’ll be gone forever. Every part of you on the outside and every part of you on the inside. You can’t be known again.”

    Carol put aside her laptop and adjusted her sitting on the couch so that she could better see Zosia, whose face was now a stone. “Why are you saying any of this?”

    “Because it’s true. Because we’re worried.”

    “Worried about what, Zosia? Do you think I’m choosing any of this? Do you think any of this is going my way?”

    “You are choosing. All of you. You’re choosing wrong.”

    “And you’re choosing nothing,” Carol said. The walls were thundering again. Why was it that the good things never could just stay fixed where they were? Why did they slip, with certainty, into despair?

    It stewed between them for a bit, the bitter exchange, and then Zosia said, “We only want the best things for you, Carol. You have to know that by now. We know you see it. I know you see it.”

    “Well aren’t you just employee of the goddamn month. You know, they ought to promote you. And, here,” Carol raised a hand, scout’s honor, “if they get to me before I kill myself, I’ll be sure to put in a good word.”

    Carol went to stand and leave, thinking it’d be an effective way to billboard her displeasure, but got annoyed again replaying the conversation in her head, enough that it brought her back down, rooted her to the spot and stirred more thoughts, new arguments. Zosia watched her do this.

    “Have you thought about it?” Carol asked, leveling her tone because this was serious now. “Seeing me as them?”

    “Of course,” and there was bonafide hunger in Zosia’s smile. Her teeth shone, her eyes alight.

    “No, I mean. Really thought about it? With your fresh perspective.” Carol gestured to Zosia’s lone body.

    This made Zosia buffer. “We have, yes.”

    “Well?”

    “We’d be happy, of course. Like we said.”

    Carol’s eyes rolled skyward. “Okay, but. All those new feelings in this one person, right? That’s gotta be different. You out here, all by yourself, and me in there. You’ve thought about that?”

    “Carol,” she reached for Carol’s hand, “our singular, fleeting feelings don’t change the facts.”

    “But what are they? The feelings. Humor me.”

    Zosia’s features held an edge. There was stern disappointment coming off of her. “Carol,” she said, like a warning.

    Carol only chuckled darkly, slack-jawed and shaking her head. She took her hand back from Zosia’s. “What? You started with the third-degree. I mean, Jesus. Death or joining? This is peanuts in comparison.”

    “This is not productive.”

    “I don’t care,” said Carol in a slow, hot whisper. Wine was on her breath. “It’d please me. So, tell me.”

    There was only silence, but not really, because there was still the thundering. It was too short a respite, the ease of things.

    Finally, with effort, Zosia said, “No. It’s not practical information.”

    “No?” Carol repeated, and couldn’t help the satisfaction. “So then. ‘Honesty’… no longer the best policy?”

    “We’re honest.”

    “Ha.”

    “And we would like it if you were, too.”

    “Me?”

    “Yes.”

    Before Carol could grow more offended, Zosia sighed, heavy, eyes closed like this was very difficult, this ‘tough love,’ this ‘real love’ that Carol just couldn’t understand, and said, “We noticed the tamper seal on the shipping container was missing. When we were outside.”

    Images flashed before Carol in quick succession: Atom bombs and Manousos and his room and peaches and his packing and his leaving and the HOA that’s moved into her street. Confirmation of all that was done without her say so, without collaboration, without any stock placed in her opinion.

    It wasn’t a surprise, not really, but Carol’s body reacted anyhow. The words rolled out in a mess. It made Carol look and feel and sound stupid. “The—The seal..?”

    “We’re very concerned. And we don’t want you or anyone else to get hurt. Because they would never harm you, Carol. Any of you. Not for anything. But these are serious weapons.”

    It hit Carol all at once, and without pause she spat, “Yeah, well you fucking gave them to me.”

    Zosia was wringing her hands. She looked on the verge of tears.

    Carol took a second to compose herself, then said, “Sorry.”

    “Whatever you’re planning, please reconsider,” Zosia begged.

    No part of this interaction was crystal clear for Carol. In fact, at this point in the evening, with all that’s been said and revealed, she was rather woolly-headed. She was, in a total sense, defeated.

    A word-salad ensued. It was breathless and helpless and exhausted in delivery. “I’m not planning, okay? I’m not. Because I don’t know what to do, or what not to do. I don’t know anything. I can’t tell my ass from a fucking hole in the ground. But what I can say, is that people,” she pointed to outside, “will fight like hell to stay alive. Their version of alive. They’re desperate to get their families back, to get their lives back, and they’ll do anything to try, and I can’t blame them! But I—” The rest of the sentence went strangled. “I don’t have anything like that to fight for anymore.”

    A swell of pain and dread overwhelmed her, and Carol let herself cry but then promptly coughed it away. She continued, quieter, more controlled, though her eyes and nose still leaked. “I’m tired. But I can’t blame them, and I can’t stop them either, because people, Zosia, have something called agency, and they’re going to use the shit out of it. So there. That honest enough for you?”

    Zosia’s hands were loose fists on her lap and she nodded a few times, like all of what was said was sinking into her understanding. She still looked on the verge of tears, this fragile beast.

    At last, the quiet broke. “It would feel lonely,” said Zosia, shattered. “If you were them, and we weren’t.”

    This made Carol laugh, though there was hardly humor in the sound. “Well, that answers that then.”

    She reached for her wine glass again, and looked inside before drinking. A funny thought struck her. “If you’re lucky,” said Carol, smiling against the rim, “maybe you’ll get me as your chaperone.”

     

     

    —

     

     

    Some point in the long, long night, Zosia had gone upstairs to bed. Carol couldn’t remember exactly when that was, because she kept drinking, down to the second bottle, until there was only damp wine-air to suckle from the bottle’s mouth.

    She was laying atop the dining table, her shins dangling and swinging like vertical blinds on a breezy, summer afternoon. In her other hand, was a respirator, the ones Manousos insisted they’d need. It hung from her index finger over the edge of the table, at risk of slipping right off onto the floor where it could be stepped on and forgotten.

    Everywhere was dark except above her, where there was the big and round and bright lighting fixture. It looked alive with light. It looked hot and vicious, and it made Carol’s eyes go slack. She wasn’t squinting, but she didn’t bother with the energy it’d take to focus, and without this specific mindfulness, Carol could imagine the light was something else, something important, like a star in a galaxy. Around this star could be many planets, maybe populated with people, or something close to people.

    The front door opened, and Carol heard someone walk in. She didn’t turn away from her star.

    “Carol?” said Manousos.

    “Polo!” she called back.

    Footsteps approached the dining room, and they stopped like they had nowhere else to go.

    To the star, Carol said, “Your friends are here, by the by.”

    Manousos didn’t respond.

    “You gonna go welcome them to the neighborhood?” Carol turned now to face him, her head drooped to the side, squishing her cheek against wood. It made her mouth pucker and her words lazy. “See, the others all left them, too. Got suspicious and just up and abandoned them like they did to me and you.”

    Carol tasted bile in the back of her throat. Otherwise, she did not feel much of anything. “But you already knew that, yeah?” she continued. She sucked more air from the bottle and then pulled her ramshackled body up to sit. The motion made her burp.

    “You should go say hi,” Carol said, gesturing to the door with the mask. In doing so, it flung right off and landed somewhere in the dark. “They came aaaaall this way for you. They made weenies. Hah.” She tried for more wine and pouted at the reminder of its lacking. “I wasn’t invited, but, I’m more of a burger gal myself.”

    Still, Manousos said nothing, and this made Carol tilt her head thoughtfully. She observed him, this puzzle of a man. “It’s funny,” she mused. “They followed the north star all the way to Albuquerque and you weren’t even in the manger when they got here..” She trailed off, her gaze going wide and nonspecific. “Hm. Anyway. Least you could do is a pull off a miracle or two.”

    “You drink,” said Manousos, finally, the sound of which made Carol irate.

    “Yeah I fucking drink.” She hopped off the table and shambled toward him. “That’s what I do. I drink. And actually, give me the fucking key to my fucking alcohol cabinet. It’s mine. My house. My things.”

    He did so without protest.

    Carol swiped the key and went for her cabinet, all the while keeping her eyes on Manousos. She fumbled with the lock, putting full stock in her tactile abilities, but it refused her. Begrudgingly, she gave the cabinet and its locked door her undivided attention, and called it names under her breath until it clicked open. From inside, she dragged out a beautiful, beautiful bottle of Belvedere.

    In her head, she recited righty-tighty lefty-loosey and wrenched the thing open. It smelled like freedom and salvation. It tasted even better.

    “Ahh,” she said, smacking her lips. “Hallelujah. And I mean that.”

    Manousos observed all this with a critical, unimpressed gaze. Though, it’d be hard to hazard a guess as to what, in fact, could impress someone like Manousos. He seemed too busy a man to worry himself with something so trivial as amusement. Carol pictured him on a rollercoaster with one of those colorful propeller hats, arms crossed and scowling.

    “So,” said Carol. “What’s the plan? Are we moving everyone into the bunker then? It’ll be a tight squeeze but I guess we can all just sorta…” she tucked the bottle under an arm and slapped her hands together sloppily, “…pack it in like sardines. Which, speaking of. Did you know there’s this fish parasite—Doopy-gizmos, something or other. Yeah. They like, join, like literally fuse together to survive. Idn’t that crazy?”

    To Manousos, this was not crazy. He turned around and went upstairs. Carol followed him.

    “Where’re you going? Hm? Oh Captain, my Captain!”

    In his room, or at this point, just a room that he was vacating, Manousos pulled out another one of those plastic garbage bags from his satchel and started filling it up with his things.

    Carol leaned against the doorframe and watched on.

    “Did you tell your buddies about the,” she whispered the next part, “atom bombs? What’d they say? Did they like it this time, coming from you? Your brilliant idea? That’s where you were, right? Snuck those fuckers in the ambulance and planted them all over New Mexico?”

    This made Manousos stop, but only for a second. He resumed with the packing. Carol resumed with the monologuing.

    “I figured if they knew, they’d have a lot to say about it. Can’t imagine Laxmi’s onboard with you blasting her baby boy sky high.” Carol’s nails tapped against the bottle of Belvedere. Entertained with herself, she made a popping noise with her lips. “Well don’t worry. Your secret’s safe with me. Oh, and it’s safe with Zosia. Because she knows. And! Before you blame me and say I’m too sweet on her or whatever, she found out because of the fucking shipping seal. Yeah. I didn’t even think to check. Bested the both of us, that one. Maybe next time you could be a little more careful? You’ve got a reputation to uphold, yunno.”

    Without having missed a beat, Manousos shuffled about the room and said, “Bunker is for you. If you stay.”

    “If I stay? And you’ll be where exactly?”

    “Roswell.”

    Carol let that bit of information turn into something comprehensible in her ears. “Er… the fuck’s in Roswell?”

    “Silo. For everyone,” said Manousos, in the same instance that Carol made a joke about aliens and motherships.

    “Wha—When did you have time to find a silo?” she asked.

    He hesitated. “Margaux.”

    “Ah. Margaux. Of course. You lucky bastards got the working version, eh?”

    Post-it notes were balled up and crumpled and set aside on his desk.

    "So what," said Carol. "You're just shepherding them underground into some fabulous tin can and then crossing your fingers they'll get over it when you kill their families?"

    “Come if you want. Stay if you want. Not matter to me.”

    “Well geez. Don’t smother me with all the enthusiasm.”

    Journals and books went into the bag.

    Carol said, “And if I say no?”

    “Then do not come.”

    “No. Not to that. To this bullshit plan. Because I’m saying no.”

    Manousos appeared tired and worn in his frame. The hero’s burden, Carol supposed.

    He said, “Too late.”

    “I never agreed to it.”

    “I do not ask permission.”

    Alcohol often caused Carol to grow more buoyant in her snark, like a scrappy little imp. But there were limits to her good-humoredness, and Manousos hardly appreciated the decorum necessary to maintain it.

    A silent rage unfurled inside Carol. She got in his face, her left fist balled and her right clutched around the bottle’s neck. Her mouth was a tight line and her voice was serious and, she thought, threatening. “Where’s the firing console, Manousos.”

    He did not flinch. “No.”

    “Where.”

    “No.”

    “I swear to God—”

    “Cállate! No more. You want this life?” He pointed to the hall, towards Carol’s room where Zosia was sleeping soundly, probably dreaming of bugs and volcanoes, of a world before and after Carol Sturka. “You want her? You want this?” From her hands, he snatched the vodka and waved it in her face before shoving it back. “Then have it.”

    There were those eyes of his again, her mother’s, so full of regret.

    “Go fuck yourself,” Carol said to him.

    “Gah!” Manousos rolled his wrist, waved her off, a gnat, and cursed in Spanish as he moved around her, back toward the stairs.

    To his back, and uncaring of her volume, Carol called, “Yeah, go ahead. Scoff, laugh, walk away. Blow some shit up while you’re at it! Kill a few million people! Go nuts, Batman!” She followed him all the way down. “But gosh, pray tell what happens after that, hm? What does your world look like after you’ve ‘saved’ it?”

    In a flash, they were nose to nose. Carol smelled his hot breath. She could almost feel the tickle of movement from his sneering. “The world, Carolsturka, is over.”

    Their exciting standoff was intruded upon by five knocks at the front door.

    “Must be the wise men bearing gifts,” muttered Carol, and she took another drink.

    She left Manousos near the landing and went to answer.

    It took her a second to understand what she was looking at.

    On the other side of the threshold was a big, yellow marshmallow peep. Well, nearly. It was leathery and shiny, and it was waving, and it said, through a heavy respirator, “Hi, Carol.”

    Carol’s body reacted late, and it reacted wrong, by dropping the Belvedere to the ground, rather, smashing it, like maybe it’d work as a smoke bomb in a pinch.

    This all happened in a matter of moments, not in slow motion like it had unfolded for Carol.

    “Oh, Jesus!” Carol cried and Manousos was beside her, slamming the door shut with his body weight.

    “Masks,” he barked.

    “Masks?” Carol, at this particular juncture, was only capable of experiencing stress, and simple words, like ‘masks’, did not make a lick of sense.

    Manousos was already halfway up the stairs in a sprint. He said, “Your mask. Put it on! Block the door.” He pointed to something and then he was out of sight, rummaging on the second floor.

    Mask, Carol thought, and then she thought, yes, and then she ran back to the dining room to look for it, the one she’d dropped by the table. In the dining room, she saw chairs, and she thought of blocking the door, and she thought, yes, the door should be blocked first, but then she thought no, the mask should be found first and this revolving thought kept her from making any one decision and she stood there like one of those vibrating wind-up toys that waddle around, the ones she’d sometimes hold in place as a child so they’d have nowhere to go, so they’d look stupid, and Carol stood there just like that, stupid.

    The knock came again, rhythmic and nice, like a Mormon or a girl-scout. The voice said, friendly, “Carol? Please don’t be alarmed. We just want to talk. It’s only one of us!”

    Carol made a shrieking noise to force herself into any sort of action. She found the mask, slapped it on. It made her hair stick up and curve strikingly on the sides. To test its efficacy, Carol took several breaths that came out harsh and creaky, and then, deciding it must be working, scanned for a chair. With jittering arms, she grabbed hold of one and started dragging it toward the foyer, saying into her mask, “fuckfuckfuckfuckfuck,” to keep herself company in these trying times.

    “We’re sorry if we frightened you!” said the voice.

    Carol made unintelligible sounds and wedged the chair under the knob.

    “Could you please open the door? It’s a little difficult to make out what you’re saying.”

    “Why are you here?” Carol yelled at the door. Her body was shaking all over.

    “We only want to talk! We think it’s something you’ll appreciate. And so will Zosia!”

    Zosia. At the mention of her name, Carol’s head perked up. Her hand wrapped tactfully around the handle and the chair. “What? What about Zosia?”

    A heavy click from behind caused Carol to jump. There was Manousos, mask on, shotgun in hand. He trained it on the door and nodded for Carol to move.

    “We’ll explain. Could you open the door please? No surprises here. We promise!”

    Manousos shook his head no. Carol motioned aggressively for him to wait, and then cleared the chair away and twisted the knob, slow and cautious.

    Through the open slit, Carol saw, as they’d promised, just one of them. They were dressed from head to toe in a bright yellow hazmat suit. Atop their head was a face shield, tinted and bug-eyed.

    “Get back,” Carol commanded.

    They waddled backwards a few steps, hands raised at their sides.

    “Wow,” they said, exhaling. “It’s so good to see you, Carol.”

    Carol grimaced, looked back at Manousos, who appeared ready to shoot, then faced once more their visitor.

    The visitor cleared its throat. “We considered calling, of course, but we were concerned there wouldn’t be a willingness to hear us out. We know we left off on a bit of a sour note. Plus,” they said, their voice bright and excited, “we were really hoping to see Zosia and Margaux. We figured face-to-face would be more personable.”

    “I can’t see your face.”

    They laughed. “Oh, well yes. This was a precaution. In case…” Their head tilted stiffly in the direction of the now empty shipping container. A fat, rubbery finger pointed to Carol’s mask and then their own. “Great minds, right?”

    “Why are you here. You said ‘Zosia’. What about Zosia.”

    “May we come in?”

    “You may not.”

    From somewhere inside the house came a soft, “Carol?”

    Carol whipped her head back, opening the door wider in the process. Zosia was hugging herself in a robe up on the staircase landing. Her eyes were blown wide, stupefied.

    Everyone looked to her, though Manousos only spared a cursory glance before steering his attention and the nose of his shotgun back on their house guest.

    “Zosia,” Carol said, weakly. “Go back to the room.”

    Zosia’s fingers flew to her lips, whether in awe or fear Carol couldn't quite discern. Zosia stayed fixed where she was.

    “Zosia,” said both Carol and the visitor, dissonant in their respective inflections.

    Carol burned red with anxiety. “Don’t speak to her,” she demanded of them.

    It was then, in this triangular stare down, that Manousos walked without resistance toward the open mouth of the doorframe and nudged his gun tip into the visitor’s belly. He did this with one hand, the other in his pocket.

    “Manousos! Fuck!” cried Carol, startled by his sudden proximity and apparent intent to kill. She cowered away and up toward where Zosia still stood, frozen on the landing. Carol hugged her with a protective violence, forcing Zosia’s face down into her neck, shielding her eyes.

    The visitor raised their big, yellow hands. They appeared like a rubber toy, a puppet. They said, calm, “Lo sentimos mucho, Manousos. Ya nos vamos. No quisimos causar m—”

    Carol winced and shrank around Zosia, expecting to have heard a bang, but it was altogether gravely quiet. Timid, she opened one eye, and the scene before her gave the illusion of a painting: mankind at the mercy of man.

    Carol thought them all dead.

    Then the rubber suit began to shake, and it fell sideways, sliding down, slow and jagged against the frame with a comical squeak.

    Manousos put down the shotgun and from his pocket, retrieved a rectangle. It was, Carol realized, the transceiver.
