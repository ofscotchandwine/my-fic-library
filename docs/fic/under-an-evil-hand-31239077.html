It was freezing. It had been a hard winter so far, and Kara could see her breath pluming before her in great white clouds as she picked her way through the snow. She couldn’t decide what was the more pressing priority: gathering her thick cape closer around her to keep her warm, or lifting her skirts a little to avoid soaking them in the snow. The last thing she wanted to do was sit in a meeting house that was scarcely warmer than it was outside with wet shoes and frozen toes.

“Hurry up, Kara,” Alex grumbled over her shoulder. “The sooner we get inside and out of this snow, the better. My feet feel like they’re going to fall off and we’re only halfway there.”

“They’re not going to feel much better in the meeting house,” Kara sniped back, brushing the still falling snow impatiently out of her face. “There’s barely been any wood in there for a fire for ages and I’ll wager that there still isn’t any.”

“All the more reason for you to hurry then, isn’t it?” Alex replied exasperatedly.

“I’ll have to take the same number of footsteps between here and there regardless of how fast I walk—”

“Girls,” Eliza said firmly, the reprimand clear in her voice. “Keep your bickering to yourself, or if you must bicker, keep it within our own home. The tongue breaketh the bone.”

“Yes, mother,” Kara and Alex chorused, used to hearing the saying, and Alex shot Kara a long-suffering smile. Neither of them meant anything by their comments, and for the last twelve years, had been as close as sisters after the Danvers family had taken Kara in after her own parents had died of the pox when she was thirteen. Kara thought of Alex, Eliza, and Jeremiah as her family now. They’d moved to Salem Village from Andover only a month before, so that Jeremiah could be closer to Salem Town, and therefore to the sea and the trade it brought. Land was cheaper in the Village, but considering how harsh October and November had been, settling in hadn’t been very enjoyable.

They trudged through the thick snow, the only sounds around them the call of birdsong and the wind through the trees. Ahead of them, on the other side of the road, was the Grant family, hardly moving any faster than the Danvers were. Kara grew concerned that the heavy snow might mean some of the other families who attended church in the meeting house may not be present; she hoped that if anyone was stuck at home, that they had enough firewood to see them through until this snow melted.

The meeting house was just as cold as Kara had feared, and when she sat down in the pew, she drummed her feet as quietly as she could against the floor in an attempt to warm them up. They’d joined the church as soon as they’d arrived in the Village, and now that this was their fifth Sabbath, she had got to know (or at least recognize) some of the faces she saw every Sunday morning. There were the Matthews, Rhea and Lar, and their adult son Michael. Benjamin and Lydia Lockwood, and their young son George. Winslow Schott, his wife Mary, and their son Winslow, known as Winn. There was the kind widow Samantha Arias and her daughter Ruby; Samantha’s husband had been a soldier who had been killed in a frontier fight, and Samantha was raising her daughter to be a midwife like she was. There were more families: the Nurses, the Proctors, and the Putnams among the most prominent, but Kara hadn’t yet had the occasion to meet any of them properly yet thanks to the apparently worse than usual early winter weather.

Kara cast her eyes around as the meeting house filled with more people, and she wrapped her hands in the edges of her woolen shawl that she’d worn under her cape to try to keep them warm. Kara didn’t enjoy church here as much as she had in Andover; the reverend was more fiery than she liked, not that she would ever say that out loud. The Andover preacher had been much more sedate, something she preferred on Sunday mornings when, if she was honest, she’d rather be asleep. Her eyes widened at the thought, and she sent a quick sorry to the man upstairs for such reckless ideas in church.

Alex shifted uncomfortably on the hard seat beside her as the sermon began, the reverend’s deep voice ringing out through the building. Kara had to admit that he was a captivating speaker. If only he weren’t so— Kara cut her thoughts off before they could continue down that route, and forced herself to pay attention.

“Christ hath placed his church in this world, as in a sea, and suffereth many storms and tempests to threaten its shipwreck …” the reverend preached, and Kara resisted the urge to smile. Storms, indeed, and not just the snowstorm that had been raging outside since the night before. The very fabric of the stifling Puritan society seemed to be threatened by outside influences and times were changing; try as she might, Kara couldn’t bring herself to feel too sorry for its apparent passing. She realized, though, that this manner of thinking brought her into direct conflict with the society that surrounded her, and so it was not a thought that she often voiced, even in her own head.

For right now, however, she was in the meeting house and she should at least look like she was paying attention.

It worked, for a while, but her freezing feet were getting more distressing as time passed, and she pressed them alternately into the floor. Her movements caused the wooden seat she was sitting on to creak, and Eliza subtly cleared her throat. Kara stopped her movements.

The reverend was talking now of the Devil who, as everyone knew, was hostile to the church. “We know that we are of God, and the whole world lieth in wickedness,” he insisted, his face caught somewhere between anguish and anger, and all around her heads nodded in agreement.

To Kara’s delight, probably owing to the fact that the meeting house was so cold, the sermon didn’t last much longer. Before long, they were filing out of the building and back outside, the weak sunlight doing its best to melt the snow that was still falling.

As Eliza and Jeremiah talked to Rhea and Lar Matthews, Kara’s attention was caught by a striking woman she’d never seen before. She was richly dressed, though the designs themselves were simple and understated, but the quality of her clothes wasn’t what had caught Kara’s attention. It was the vivid green of her eyes and the way she smiled at the Widow Arias and her daughter. She was absolutely beautiful, and Kara felt her heart racing in her chest as the woman crouched down to tighten the laces on Ruby’s boots. For no reason Kara could name, she was captivated by the woman’s bright smile.

Somehow, the woman seemed to feel Kara’s eyes on her, and she looked briefly away from Ruby and over at Kara. She felt undone as the woman noticed her looking, raised an eyebrow at her and—was it Kara’s imagination?—her smile widened before she turned back to Ruby. Even though the woman’s attention was no longer on her, Kara couldn’t keep from watching the way she fussed over Ruby, couldn’t help but fixate on the lock of dark hair she could see beneath the woman’s cap and the dimples etched into the corners of her smile.

The stranger glanced once more at Kara, who felt herself blush from head to toe at being caught still staring at her. Rather than finding Kara’s attention unwelcome, though, her eyes crinkled with amusement and curiosity, like she was trying to work out whether she knew Kara or not.

Kara felt like she’d tripped over a branch: off-balance and full of energy. The woman smiled at her once more, then stood up and brushed her hand over Ruby’s shoulder. She said a few words to Samantha before looking intently back at Kara, and Kara’s breath caught in her throat. Unfortunately, Eliza pulled her into the conversation between herself and Rhea, and by the time she was able to pull herself back out of it, the woman was already walking away from the church, alone, heading towards the western side of the Village. Kara felt a little deflated as she watched her leave, as if she’d missed a chance at making a new acquaintance.

“Who’s that woman?” she asked Eliza once Rhea had left, nodding in the woman’s direction.

“Who?”

Kara turned Eliza in the right direction. “The one walking alone. She has green eyes and seems to know the Widow Arias.”

To Kara’s surprise, Eliza’s face darkened. “That’s Lena Spheer, although I’m told that she prefers to be called Lena Luthor, her name before marriage. Why she doesn’t want to keep her husband’s name, I don’t know.”

“Don’t you like her?” Kara asked, watching as Lena turned a corner and passed out of sight. “She looked kind.”

“She’s pleasant enough, very polite and an excellent seamstress. She owns a store in the Town. But her husband died in mysterious circumstances and she speaks far too openly, too … contrarily. Sometimes, she doesn’t attend church. She does not act like a good Christian woman, and I have heard … things. As well, she should be mourning and honoring her husband and instead she reads and speaks too plainly when she should not speak at all. She has ideas about society that will cause talk.”

“That does not sound to me like a bad thing.”

“Kara,” Eliza said firmly, a note of worry in her voice and looked around to see who might overhear them talking. There were no other people nearby except Alex and Jeremiah, everyone else having left already, but she lowered her voice anyway. “I know that you have … ideas, too. That your parents taught you differently. But some of the things that you think are dangerous in this society, and I would advise you to hold your tongue in company other than ours.”

The warning in her last words were plain, and the words that Eliza did not say were just as clear as if she had: Lena Spheer will lead you astray.

Eliza chucked Kara under the chin when she did not respond, and Kara nodded, taking note of the apprehension in Eliza’s eyes.

But just because she took a note of it, didn’t mean that she intended to heed her warning if she had the chance to disobey it.

All Kara could think about as they walked home was Lena Spheer—Luthor, Kara corrected herself. Why had she not seen her at the meeting house before now? What had happened to her husband? And what were her ideas that Eliza had mentioned?

All Eliza had really done was pique Kara’s interest in the stranger who had looked so kindly at Ruby, and so curiously at Kara. And despite Eliza’s warning, she hoped that if she saw Lena again, that she would be able to talk to her. The people in the Village were so pious, so staid. Lena Luthor held more interest for Kara than her whole life in the Village so far, and she was determined to speak with her, if she could.

By the time they reached their home, a large white clapboard house, Kara’s feet were both frozen and soaking wet. The snow continued falling, and all the walkways that they’d trodden on their way to the meeting house had been partially filled in by the time they’d made their way back. She was very happy to be able to finally kick the snow off her shoes at the door and come inside into the front room. Perching herself on one of the chairs, she watched while Jeremiah built up the fire once more. Usually they would leave the embers burning while they were at church, but they’d gone out this morning and they hadn’t had time to rebuild it; she wished that they’d made time, so that they could’ve come home to a nice warm house. The Danvers’ had neither an indentured servant nor a slave, feeling like if they couldn’t do all their work themselves, then the work wasn’t worth doing. More privately, they felt that both institutions were morally wrong, but were in no position to argue the fact in such a strict community.

So, the house was cold upon their return. There was no point complaining about it, so Kara pulled off her shoes and then her woolen stockings. The Danvers were a relaxed family, and she thought nothing of laying her stockings on the stone floor of the hearth to be warmed and dried by the flames.

There wasn’t much to do on the Sabbath. Nominally the Danvers were Puritans, but they were more relaxed on the Sabbath than Kara thought the rest of the Village were, at home at least. While Eliza didn’t do any cooking, as the smoke would be visible from outside, they did dispense with the constant reading of the Bible. Both Alex and Kara were allowed to play simple games of cards, as well as work on their own personal quilts or other mending. They listened to Jeremiah tell stories, and if they weren’t too busy with other tasks, Jeremiah continued teaching both of them to read and write. Eliza could already read a little, at least enough to sound her way through her more well-known parts of the Bible, but Jeremiah wanted Alex and Kara to be able to read at a much higher level, and he considered the ability to write essential for everyone.

The Danvers home was a good size, large enough for two upstairs rooms. Eliza and Jeremiah shared the smaller room with a full bed, and Kara and Alex shared the larger room, which also held all of their textile equipment for the three women to make quilts to be sold in the Danvers store in the Town. Eliza was well-known in the area from the time when she was a midwife. When she was brought low by a broken leg which had taken a very long time to heal, she had taken up sewing and quilting again. It was something she’d done as a child, and now all three of them spent their time making high quality pieces to sell in the store. The store was prosperous, and Jeremiah managed it well.

By nightfall, they were all tired. The snow continued to fall all afternoon and well into the evening, but inside the house, they had plenty of wood and candles to keep their rooms warm and well-lit. Alex and Kara both had their reading and writing lessons from Jeremiah, scratching out tiny letters on worn paper with quills, and reading out loud from the Bible and one of the few other books that they owned. Alex was better at reading, and Kara better at writing and drawing, and Eliza joked that between the two of them they had at least one educated person. Kara also liked to draw with ink on parchment, and Jeremiah brought home small supplies for her. The house was decorated with inked drawings that Kara had done.

Nothing they did that day left Kara with a lot of time to think about the woman she’d seen earlier in the day, and she decided not to ask Eliza any more about her, at least until some time had passed. It wasn’t often that Eliza passed judgement on someone, even if it had been fairly mild. Kara wanted to give her time to forget the topic of Lena Luthor before she asked about her again.

In bed later that night, Alex shuffled over to her.

“Have you thought any more about Michael Matthews?” she asked in a whisper followed by a strange sound, as if she’d started to laugh then swallowed the sound before any more of it could escape.

Kara twisted over and looked at her sister in the dim light of the fire. “What about him?”

“You know he’s got his eye on you.”

Kara sighed and lay on her back. “I know he does.”

Alex propped herself up on her elbow and pushed her hair out of her face. Kara could just about see her, though the fire was behind her now, and she looked concerned. “Don’t you like him?”

Kara shrugged. “Does it really matter what I think of him? I have to get married sometime, and he does run a successful tavern in Town.”

“You wouldn’t have to worry about money or about him going out to sea or to the frontier, at least. He stays where he is. And at least he’s handsome. You’re very pretty. Your children wouldn’t look like potatoes.”

Kara snorted and shoved at Alex, who squealed and nearly fell completely out of the bed. She pulled herself back into bed and under the covers once more.

“I suppose it could be worse,” Kara commented once she’d recovered from her giggling. “At least it’s not Morgan Edge who’s being considered as a husband for me.”

“Ugh,” Alex replied with a shiver that wasn’t all fake. “He’s awful. He reminds me of that slime you see on ponds in the summer. I’m sure he uses it to make his hair lie the way it does.”

“Let’s hope Eliza doesn’t start thinking of him as a husband for you,” Kara teased, “or you’ll have to start putting that slime in your own hair.”

“I think I’d push him in the pond before he could come anywhere near me with his slime. Or come near me at all,” she added in a quieter voice, and Kara frowned. It wasn’t the first time that Alex had mentioned something like that. There had been a time when, a few years ago and just between the two of them, Alex had mentioned that she didn’t want to get married to a man at all. The curious thing for Kara was that Alex immediately clarified her point by saying that she did want to get married, but that she didn’t want to marry a man, and that was the problem. She’d sworn Kara to secrecy, and Kara had in turn sworn to take that to her grave. Alex not wanting to get married? Maybe in a big city like Boston she wouldn’t have to, if she managed to earn enough money in the Danvers store. Kara was sure that Jeremiah could eventually open another store in Boston that Alex could work in; in a big city like Boston it wouldn’t be as obvious that she wasn’t married, but it would be difficult … in Salem Village, it would be impossible not to notice that Alex didn’t have a husband.

But if Alex didn’t want to marry a man, then who could she marry?

Ever since that conversation over three years ago, Kara had been thinking about it. They’d never talked about it again, but the question wouldn’t leave her mind. A year ago, in a moment of confusion, she had thought that maybe Alex meant that she wanted to be married to a woman, but surely that couldn’t be. That kind of thing didn’t happen. How would they have children?

And yet …

And yet, ever since that conversation and her realization that maybe Alex meant a woman, Kara had been having strange thoughts. She found herself noticing things about women that she should only be noticing about men. She noticed how pretty they were, wondered how soft their skin would feel under her fingers, what they might be like to dance with.

Late at night when she slept, sometimes she dreamed about a person whose face was indistinct. She saw glimpses of this person, no more. Just their hands, smaller than a man’s, and pale. Their hair was long and dark. And when she and this person were in the woods, she could always see them moving just out of sight, their dress, not their trousers, becoming hidden behind trees. They always called her forward in a voice she could never remember, but she knew it was gentle, that it was not harsh and deep, but inviting and warm.

When she woke in the mornings, she could never remember anything but their long dark hair and the faint knowledge that it was not her future husband she was dreaming of.

2. Chapter 2

A few days later the snow ceased and the sun came back out, chasing away the heavy grey skies and shining down on the countryside. Kara was glad to see it, for it meant a little more freedom to go for walks. It also meant that she wouldn’t have to dry her wet stockings on the hearth after every trip outside.

Or so she thought.

The thawing snow turned the roads and the walkways into rivers of mud, and Kara’s dress and her petticoats were splattered with mud. She almost couldn’t believe herself when she wished for the snow to return; at least the snow was just cold and wet. The mud required scrubbing at the river to remove the stains of it, and she knew that she would have to spend a long time at the banks cleaning her clothes.

Nevertheless, it was a fine morning in Salem Town, and Kara was happy to be out of the Village and amongst people once again after the snowbound nature of the last few days. She had come with Jeremiah to help organize the shop. They had set off early in the morning with their cart full of quilts and linen that the Danvers women had been working on recently. She’d sat in the cart, wrapped up warm against the cold that stung her face despite the weak warmth of the sun. Jeremiah drove slowly, not wishing to jolt the cart too much and risk mud splashing up onto the material, even though they had carefully wrapped them before leaving.

It took a couple of hours to cover the seven miles or so into the Town, since they were going so slowly, and they ate their breakfast and talked while they travelled. Kara didn’t get to spend a lot of time with Jeremiah as he was in the Town so often, usually staying overnight in a small room above the shop, so she enjoyed his company now. He would soon need to do his night watch duty again; Kara still didn’t think it was fair that the men of the Village had to do night watch duty for the Town, but none of the Town men did night watch duty for the Village. It was a point of contention, but she had been advised to hold her tongue about it. The men of the Village were working on gaining independence from the Town, and would be sending another petition very soon.

In the last few years, Salem had been designated a port town for the colony, and there had been a huge upswing of business, commerce, and travelers. It was the reason why they had moved to the area, after all. The Danvers store sold all sorts of goods: utensils, pins, buttons, linen, the quilts, baskets, and any other manner of things that might be needed. Eliza spent time picking fruit in the right seasons and making jams, and since their move to Salem, they sold well in the store too. The quilts gave their store an extra edge over the competition, and the money they made meant that the Danvers family was prospering because of their hard work. They had enough food and enough firewood, and enough money to buy a lot of materials to make their products.

By the time the cart rattled into the Town, Kara was feeling good about the day ahead. It would be diverting to spend some time in the store before they made their way back to the Village, and she would get to meet new people and see the ships in the port. It was exciting; sailors strode up and down the walkways, calling loudly to their friends and to strangers, getting their land legs back before heading straight to the taverns. The fresh smell of the sea air, carried high on the breeze, seemed even more potent in this weather, and it put her in mind of adventure. As she climbed down from the cart to the ground outside the store, she could hear the clanging of the ship bells in the harbor; suddenly she pictured herself on a ship, sailing across the sea to a new destination and new shores. Kara liked to watch the sea from the edge of the Town, and once she’d even taken her shoes off and stood still as the cold water washed over her feet. She had instantly felt connected to all the places that this ocean visited, and her world had, for a moment, expanded past the current confines of the Village and the Town.

She shook the thoughts out of her head for now, for there was work to be done. Jeremiah unlocked the door to the store and stepped inside. It was early morning yet, and the place still smelled of new wood. The floor creaked as Kara made her way across the large room and around the end of the counter, ducking under the rope so that she could get behind it and reach her cleaning cloths. While Jeremiah brought their produce in from the cart, she took their bucket and fetched some water from the well around the corner, bringing it carefully back and wiping her shoes of the mud from the road before entering.

Before the store opened, Kara cleaned the counter and the surfaces, rolling up her sleeves and removing the dust before rearranging the jams and little boxes of pins and buttons to make everything look more enticing. Soon, the whole room looked bright and clean, and Kara set her hands on her hips, pleased with her work.

“It looks great in here,” Jeremiah commented from his position leaning against the doorframe. He smiled when Kara grinned at him. “Are you ready for me to bring the new quilts in?”

“Yes,” she replied, stepping to the side and indicating the racks on which they’d be displayed for potential buyers. “I’m going to put them up over here.” Their quilts never lasted very long in the store, usually selling within a day or two of the ships coming in. She had even once heard that one of the very quilts she’d created had made a journey all the way to England! It gave her a little thrill to think of it so far away.

Jeremiah carried the three quilts into the store, laying them on the clean counter for Kara to spread over the racks. Eliza’s recent creation was light blue and featured fine detailing of some of the small flowers that had usually sprung up around their old home in springtime. Alex’s was bolder in color, with large yellow and green triangles and squares on a white background in patterns that resembled sunflowers; it was quite clever, Kara thought, and she wished she’d thought of it herself.

Kara’s own quilt didn’t feature shapes at all, but a dark forest scene. She’d never attempted anything like it before, and so it had represented quite a challenge. Each square had part of the scene on it, like a puzzle piece, and eventually it had all come together to show a path, lit by a single blazing torch, winding through the woods at night. It was a little haunting, a little eerie, but to her it represented the unknown and the desire for adventure and discovery. It also represented her own desire to walk down that path and see who she was when she reached the end of it. For the first time, she wasn’t sure whether a quilt she’d made would sell quickly or not, and if she was honest with herself, she wasn’t sure if she wanted it to. It would be like letting a hidden piece of herself be sold right along with it. She held it closer to her heart than she did any other quilt she’d made, but there was no room to keep it at home.

She smoothed a reverent hand over the soft fibers of the quilt as she lay it over the rack for display. She set it behind the others, because the brighter colors of the other two would draw customers towards them. She thought that her own might be off-putting.

The day passed quickly when the store was open, the fire in the back of the room inviting people in, and Kara was able to speak with many of the customers. She enjoyed the clamor of the Town, the raised voices outside shouting about the wares in their stores or trying to sell meat pies. Kara and Jeremiah spent a few coins and bought a hot meat pie each, enjoying them as the sun reached its peak in the sky before starting its journey back down.

Alex’s quilt was the first to be sold. A wealthy merchant had come through on his way to Boston, wanting something bright for one of the walls in his home. He was rude and loud, and ignored Kara to speak only with Jeremiah. Kara smothered a smile when the merchant had offered a price higher than the one Jeremiah was attempting to tell him, but neither of them were going to turn down additional profit.

A little while later, Kara was wrapping up a box of needles for a customer when a large man stepped suddenly into the shop and bellowed, “Jeremiah?!”

Kara winced at the volume; he could’ve been heard from Andover. Thankfully, before the man could shout again, Jeremiah came through from the room at the back of the store where he had been sorting out some of their less valuable stock.

“Thomas?” Jeremiah asked. “Is everything alright?”

“No, I’m afraid that Michael Jones is unwell,” the man, Thomas, boomed. Apparently, that was just the volume of his natural voice.

“Alright, let us go and talk out the back so that Kara can deal with this customer,” Jeremiah replied, drawing the large man through the shop and out of the door, closing it tightly behind him.

As he did so, the front door of the store opened a second time, but at the moment Kara was too busy taking payment to be able to take much notice of the new customer. Once the needle lady had taken her leave, though, Kara turned her attention to the customer, a woman, who was examining the boxes of buttons.

“Welcome to Danvers Store,” Kara said, brushing her hands on her apron and hoping that the woman was ignoring the obvious sound of the two men talking behind the door. She heard the back door close a moment later as they went outside, and it was blessedly quiet once more.

The customer started to turn toward her when Kara spoke, and straight away, with a jolt of excitement, Kara recognized her.

“Oh!” she exclaimed. “It’s you.”

Lena Luthor stood before her, and now that she was standing this close to her, Lena was even more beautiful than Kara remembered. Kara almost felt a little faint when Lena’s eyes lit up as if she’d been given the answer to a puzzling question. “It’s me,” she replied with a bright, pleased smile, “and you’re Kara Danvers.”

“You know my name?”

“Of course I do. Well, I knew your first name already, and the rest is above the door outside. I’ve been hoping to make your acquaintance,” Lena said, stepping forward and holding her hand out. Kara took it in her own, and instantly her daydream of holding a woman’s hand jumped to the forefront of her mind. Lena’s hand was warm despite the freezing weather, and her hand felt soft as Kara held it in hers. Her heart began to beat faster when Lena met her eyes.

A muffled silence seemed to settle over them, so that all Kara could see and hear was Lena. Her eyes were greener than she recalled, seeming even more vibrant out of the direct light of the sun; they reminded her of forests that she felt she could get happily lost in. There was a scar above one of them, as though she’d got caught by an unlucky branch while on a walk somewhere. Time seemed to slow down as they stood there together in the store, hands clasped, and Kara had the sense that she was meeting someone who was important to her, although she couldn’t say why or how. It turned out that Lena only lived a fifteen-minute walk away, since the Danvers lived near enough in the middle of the Village, but because of the frontier attacks in the colony that sometimes ranged towards the coast, Jeremiah would not let her go alone. Of course, there was the night watch, but Kara was happier with Jeremiah.

Kara wasn’t sure why she was nervous. She wanted to get to know Lena better, and logically, the best way to do that was to spend time with her. She hadn’t gone into Town this week, so they hadn’t bumped into each other at all since Sunday morning.

She wasn’t normally nervous when meeting new people, but somehow, Lena was different. The very thought of Lena sent her heart fluttering strangely in her chest, and Kara didn’t know what it meant. Instinctively, though, she knew not to ask Eliza what this feeling might be; Eliza hadn’t been too happy when Kara had announced that she was going over to Lena’s house for dinner, although she hadn’t given voice to any objections. Kara hoped, if she and Lena became good friends, that Lena and Eliza would meet each other properly. She was sure that they would like each other. Eliza usually liked everyone, and it was true that she’d had good things to say about Lena when Kara had first asked about her, so her tight-lipped silence ever since Lena had invited her over had been a little disconcerting.

Kara straightened her collar in her spotted looking glass, trying to make it lie flat. There was a wrinkle in it which would not straighten out, and it was bothering her. She pushed it down with her fingers and frowned at it, hoping that if she held it flat long enough, it would stay flat. After a moment she released it; it wrinkled once more, she sighed. It was as good as it was going to get, she figured. Lifting her hands to her face next, she tucked wisps of her golden hair behind her ear and pulled her coif cap over her head, tying it neatly beneath her chin. Thankfully, it was neither raining nor windy outside today, so she wouldn’t look bedraggled when she reached Lena’s house.

She wanted to look nice when she got there.

She wondered what they might do tonight once they had eaten whatever dinner Lena (or maybe Sam?) had prepared. Eliza had mentioned that Lena read. And then she remembered, suddenly, the warning that she’d imagined Eliza giving her once before: Lena Spheer will lead you astray. Perhaps Eliza truly did think that, although Kara couldn’t imagine in what way Lena could do that. What was she going to do, read books?

Kara shrugged the feeling off. She didn’t care to think too much about it right now, not when she was so excited to be going over for dinner with Lena. Except for walks with her family and trips to the store and the meeting house, she hadn’t had any semblance of a social life since they’d lived in Andover. Tonight, she was going to have dinner with a new friend, and she couldn’t wait.

Jeremiah knocked on the frame of the bedroom door; Kara saw him in the looking glass, and she turned around.

“You look nice tonight,” he commented, gesturing at her dress, and Kara looked down at it. It was one of her newer ones, and not one Lena had seen her wear before. It was a deep green, the color of nature and renewal, and she knew it suited her well.

“Thank you,” she replied, pleased with the compliment, then fetched her warmest cape from the cupboard and wrapped it around herself. It may not be raining or windy, but it was still December and bitterly cold outside.

“Are you ready?”

“I am. I just need to pick up the dessert.”

Eliza had very kindly baked cornbread for her to take with her, along with a jar of jam she’d made back in Andover. Together they would make a good dessert. Eliza’s disapproval of Lena did not extend to discourtesy. Jeremiah lit a torch from the fireplace and soon, they were leaving the house.

The winter air was cold but refreshing, and Kara thought that it felt like snow would come back soon enough. They walked in relative silence towards Lena’s house, listening to the sounds of the Village at night. They could hear cows lowing in the barns and fields they passed, and owls hooting in the trees while they searched for mice. Kara didn’t often get a chance to walk outside at night, and she marveled at how many stars she could see, since the night was so clear.

When she did speak to Jeremiah, she kept her voice to a whisper, unwilling to jar the silence that hung all around them. There was almost no wind, and the walk was a pleasant one as they made their way to the road Lena lived on.

Lena’s house stood at the end of the road, where the walkway started to taper off into a track that led into the woods just beyond the building. Unless someone lived in one of the few houses, there would be no reason to ever come so far out — the road Lena lived on was one that led to the wilderness.

The house was very much like the Danvers’ home: a clapboard main building with an outhouse, and there was a smaller, fully enclosed lean-to on one side that Kara imagined was used to store firewood. The house was larger than the Danvers’, reflecting Lena’s relative wealth, and cleaner. In the darkness the moonlight made the white-painted wood shine in a welcoming way, and the smell of roasting meat made her stomach grumble in anticipation.

“You’re going to have a good meal tonight,” Jeremiah said as he sniffed the air.

“I hope she’ll like the cornbread and the jam,” Kara replied as they walked up the path towards the front door. The lawn in front of the house was well-kept and slightly sloping, ensuring that water ran away from the house rather than towards it.

Kara had barely to knock on the door before Lena was opening it with a radiant smile. “Kara! I’m so happy that you could come. And Mr Danvers. Thank you so much for escorting her here,” she added respectfully. “Will you stop for a refreshment?”

“Thank you, ma’am, but no, I’ll be heading back for my own dinner. It was very kind of you to offer. Kara, I’ll return in three hours?”

Kara nodded, and with a last smile at the two women, Jeremiah tipped his hat and went on his way. Kara turned back to Lena, who stepped aside with her arm outstretched for Kara to enter the house.

“Please come in, Kara, and welcome.”

The second that Kara stepped over the threshold and into the front room, she was enveloped with warmth. There was a large fire heating the room, and candles in sconces with metal plates made the room feel bright and homely. Kara saw at once that Lena was far wealthier than she had imagined, for everything in the room was of excellent quality. The walls were plastered white and even, with picture frames hanging here and there showing scenes of Boston, and a large, patterned tapestry hanging in one corner to the side of the fire. Next to the tapestry and in front of the fire were two comfortable chairs and a table with a book on it; the chairs, especially one, looked like they were well-used, and Kara wondered what it would be like to sit in one on a quiet evening and read or think. She imagined that it would be very peaceful.

Lena stepped up beside her and Kara turned to look at her host. She was dressed simply once more, in the same manner as Kara, but once again she could tell that the material was of a higher quality. Her dress was a lovely dark russet that suited her pale skin, with embroidered white sleeves and collar. Her hair was covered by her coif cap, but Kara could see a few strands of dark hair which had worked loose of the pins that held it in place.

The corners of Lena’s mouth curved up into a smile, and Kara once more found herself a little overwhelmed with nerves. She’d looked forward to this all day, all week really, and now that she was here, she didn’t know what to do with herself except to gaze at Lena; she couldn’t have torn her eyes away from her for all the gold in Spain.

“I’m so glad you could come to visit me tonight,” Lena said, her pretty green eyes sparkling with delight. Her voice was warm, and for some reason Kara felt her breath all stopped up in her throat as Lena came toward her. When she felt Lena’s lips on her cheek, she suddenly felt quivery and her palms started to sweat around the box she was holding in her hands.

“Thank you for asking me to come,” she replied, and even to herself, her voice sounded tremulous.

“Are you alright?” Lena asked. She was slightly shorter than Kara, and had to look up a little to meet her eyes, especially from the close distance at which she was standing.

“I’m— I’m fine,” she replied, wishing she could shake her hands free of the nervous energy that she was filled with. She wanted Lena to like her, for them to know each other, and she decided to be truthful. “I feel a little … shy all of a sudden, I suppose, but I really am happy to be here.”

Lena smiled kindly and stepped backwards to give Kara a little more space. “And I’m happy to have you here. If it makes you feel better, I feel a little shy, too.”

Kara was surprised. Lena didn’t look shy, but maybe she was just better at hiding it than she was. But the admission that she wasn’t alone did help to relax her.

“I’m always shy when meeting new people,” Lena continued, and then smiled cheekily, “but I also feel like that is what’s expected of us in this society. We’re supposed to be reticent. But now that your father’s gone … I don’t know about you, but when I’m in my own home I don’t like to wear this thing,” she finished, indicating the coif cap she was wearing. She reached up and untied the strings of her cap and pulled it off, revealing her dark hair tied up in pins. She lay the cap on a cabinet and then pulled the pins out of her hair, sending it tumbling down in long, thick dark waves over her shoulders.

Standing with her hair loose in that russet dress with her green eyes focused on Kara, Lena no longer looked like a Puritan woman, but something altogether wilder. She was the kind of beautiful woman that inspired stories and poems, and Kara felt, deep down inside her, that Lena was not made for staid Puritan love but for wild and untamed passion.

Kara couldn’t say where that thought came from, only that it came from her, and she knew that she would come back to that thought, over and over again in her mind late at night.

“Would you like to take yours off too?” Lena asked quietly, breaking through Kara’s thoughts and yanking her back to the present. There was a pretty pink blush high on her cheeks, and Kara felt herself turn red. Was I gazing too obviously at her? “You don’t have to, of course. I just prefer not to wear mine, and it’s just you and me tonight. Sam and Ruby are staying with Ruby’s grandmother, and Eve finishes work early on a Wednesday so that she can go and work with the apothecary in Town.”

“Who is Eve?” Kara asked curiously, glad for something to latch her mind to.

“She’s my servant,” Lena replied. “She used to work for my family in Boston as an indentured servant, but after she completed her period, she wanted to keep working for me. When I decided to move here, she came too, and she has her own room next to Sam’s. She spends all her money on paints. She’s very good actually, and she’s helping to illustrate a physick book.”

“Oh,” Kara said in surprise. This certainly was a house full of interesting women. Lena the businesswoman, Sam the midwife, and Eve the painter. She wondered whether she’d get a chance to meet Eve as well one day.

Remembering the food in her hands, she lifted the items up to Lena. “These are for you, by the way. Cornbread and a jar of Eliza’s jam to go with it. She thought we could have it for dessert.”

Lena reached out and took them from Kara, holding the jam up to the light and examining it interestedly. The candlelight brought out the deep red, almost purple color of the jam.

“It is fox grape,” Kara said, answering Lena’s unasked question. “Eliza made it a few months before we moved here.”

“I have never tasted it like this,” Lena replied, lowering the jar and holding it carefully. “I love its color, and now I’m looking forward to dessert more than the meal I cooked! This was very kind, and please thank Eliza for me. We’ll put these in the kitchen and I’ll give you a tour of the house. There isn’t much to see though, I’ll warn you.”

“Wait,” Kara said, holding her hand out and touching Lena’s forearm, and Lena stopped before she’d moved half a step. “Before you do, could I … I’d like to take my cap off, too.” Kara blushed a little. It was considered unseemly for women to reveal their hair in front of people, and Kara hadn’t done so in front of anyone except her family for a long time. But Lena wasn’t just anybody, she was … she was Lena. She was kind, and Kara liked her. And Lena had neither asked her to remove her cap, nor expected her to. She had also not judged her for not removing it when she’d done hers.

“You don’t have to, just because I did,” Lena replied quickly, looking a little dismayed, as if she thought she’d somehow forced Kara’s hand. “I wouldn’t want you to feel uncomfortable—”

“I don’t,” Kara said gently, brushing her thumb over Lena’s sleeve reassuringly and stopping Lena’s rambling. “I want to.”

Lena gave her a soft smile, and she watched silently while Kara untied the strings of her cap then pulled it off. She lay the cap next to Lena’s, pulled the pins out of her hair, and loosened it with her hand until it lay over her shoulders.

She had revealed more than just her hair to Lena. In that one gesture, she’d said more than her words could have. She’d indicated the same level of comfort with her, of familiarity, that Lena had already displayed to her.

Kara was surprised to notice that she didn’t feel the least bit vulnerable with her hair down in front of Lena. Her shyness had dissipated, leaving her feeling a little daring instead. Eliza would never approve, but the way Lena was looking at her hair, then at Kara, made her feel good.

“Your hair is a beautiful color,” Lena said quietly, the pink blush she’d worn earlier a little deeper now. She looked gratified, her eyes trailing up the waves of Kara’s hair until their gazes met. Kara had the sense that they were having a silent conversation, one where they crossed the threshold from being mere acquaintances to something … something more. There was no acknowledgement of it other than Lena’s comment about her hair color. The change was simply accepted by both of them, and Kara felt at ease and happy at feeling so at home in front of Lena.

After a long moment, Lena tilted her head towards the side of the room. “Shall we?”

Lena led them out of the front room and through a low door into the kitchen. Bunches of herbs were strung from the wooden beams in the ceiling, and in here too, a fire was going. It was larger than the one at home, capable of roasting a whole small animal over it. On the table in the middle of the room were bowls of vegetables, a loaf of bread, and a plate with a cooked chicken on it, the meat resting after being roasted over the fire. It smelled divine, and Kara’s mouth watered.

Lena lay the jam and the box with the cornbread on the table, and indicated another door. “Come. I’ll give you the tour.”

She led Kara into a room with a large window which seemed to exist solely for eating. There was no fire burning in here, but there was a long table which was surrounded by six ornate wooden chairs with soft lining on the seats. As she led Kara up the creaking stairs, she pointed out the childlike drawings on the walls, which had apparently been done by Ruby.

Upstairs there were two rooms with their doors closed, which Lena explained belonged to Sam and Ruby, and Eve; out of respect for their privacy, Lena left those doors shut. Next to them was a large room, brightly lit with candles, which contained items for making quilts: a spinning wheel, and a large table which was covered with various pieces of fabric and several squares of cloth. There was a pin cushion with pins stuck in odd angles, some with long pieces of thread still tied to them. It was obvious that Lena spent most of her time here, and on one of the walls was a huge rack with various half-finished quilts and what looked like blankets hanging from it.

Kara stepped closer to one of the quilts, a deep blue one made of silk with green plants and little creatures sewn along the bottom third of it. It reminded Kara of the sea, of a deeper version of the rock pools along various beaches she’d visited. It made her wonder what the ocean floor might look like, if only she were able to see it. Crouching down to see it better, she ran her fingers along the image of a crab, delicately picked out in threads of various colors, darting behind a waving green frond of some kind of water plant. The detailing was exquisite, and Kara understood at once why Eliza had told her that Lena was an excellent seamstress. All along the bottom of this quilt were other tiny, meticulous details: small shoals of silver fish, shells of many shapes, rocks of different shades with anemones on them. Kara felt like she could get lost in the details of this quilt, in the tiny little world Lena had created.

“What do you think of my work?”

Kara stood up and turned around to find Lena watching her, her hip leaning against the table. She was fiddling with her fingers in front of her stomach, betraying her nerves, but Kara knew that Lena had nothing to be nervous about.

“It’s … it’s remarkable,” Kara replied, gesturing to the ocean scene behind her. “I’ve never seen anything like this. It’s beautiful.”

Lena seemed to stand up straighter under the praise, and she dropped her hands to her sides. “Really?”

“Yes!” Kara said emphatically, wondering why Lena couldn’t see her skill in her own work. “Don’t you think so?”

“Well, my mother never seemed to think much of it. She didn’t think it was worth wasting my time on when I should be settling down and having lots of babies,” Lena replied a little bitterly, before shaking her head as if clearing the unpleasant thoughts away.

Kara bit her lip. “I don’t wish to be rude about your mother—”

Lena rolled her eyes and smirked. “Oh, be as rude as you like. My mother makes even the most pious person look like a non-believer.”

“Well then. I believe that … I believe that there are more important things in life than finding a husband. Your mother is blind if she thinks that something as beautiful as this is a waste of time. It will endure long after you and I are gone from this Earth, and people will still be talking about how exceptional the detailing is.”

The smile on Lena’s face deepened the dimples in her cheeks. “I’m really happy that you like it, especially because I think so highly of your work. That reminds me … would you like to see your quilt?”

“You have already hung it up?” Kara asked with delight.

“Of course! I hung it as soon as I came home with it. Come, I’ll show you.”

Oh, Kara thought as she followed Lena out of the sewing room and across the hall to a closed door, it means we’ll be going into her bedchamber. She’d been in women’s bedchambers before of course, especially during childbirth and the lying-in; but somehow, going into Lena’s, for no real reason, at all felt far more intimate to Kara.

Lena pushed the door open. It was a medium-sized room with a bed surrounded by curtains, and a fire burning low in the grate. It wasn’t a bright room; there was only one small window, and aside from the fire, there was only a single candle burning in a sconce.

“Oh, I’m sorry,” Lena said quickly. “I prefer my chamber to be quite dark these days unless I’m reading in here, but I’ll just …” Lena left for a moment and came back with a candle from the sewing room, and with the increased light, Kara could see better. It was a normal chamber with wonderfully carved furniture, that much Kara could tell, but there were two things in it that set the room apart from others she’d been in.

One was that next to the bed, there was a shelf that was absolutely groaning under the weight of books that Lena had piled up onto it; there were more books than Kara had ever seen in her life before now.

And the other was that her tapestry was hanging on the wall that faced the foot of the bed.

She crossed the room toward it, eager to see it again, and the low light from the fire and candle Lena held in her hand seemed to make the torch on the quilt flicker as though it were real. It seemed to almost come alive in the darkened room, far more alive than she’d ever seen it. She half fancied that she saw creatures moving between the shadows of the trees, and the path looked even more enticing than it did in daylight.

Kara gasped and tentatively touched the quilt, as if to prove to herself that she wouldn’t sink through it and into another world.

“It seems almost magical to me by candlelight,” Lena said quietly, standing beside Kara. “Not malefic, but … the way the torch seems to move, the way the path seems to beg to be walked? It’s magnificent, Kara. You have such a wonderful talent for making it feel alive, for making me feel when I look at it.”

“Thank you,” Kara replied, just as quietly.

They stood before the tapestry and the silence felt almost sacred. She moved closer to Lena, wanting to be nearer to her, and felt the skin of their fingers brush against each other. And without saying a word, Lena took Kara’s hand in hers and for one long, blissful moment they stood in silence, linked together.

“Come on,” Lena said eventually, squeezing Kara’s hand once before letting go. “I didn’t slave over this chicken all afternoon just to have it freeze solid in the kitchen.”

They made their way back downstairs, a new openness between them as though viewing the tapestry together had helped move them past the shyness at the beginning of this new friendship. In the kitchen, Kara helped Lena cut pieces of meat from the chicken and parceled it out onto the two pewter plates that Lena fetched from a cabinet, then added the vegetables. Lena pulled the table in the front room between the two chairs, and Kara got her wish to sit down in one of them. Lena set the book from the table on top of the mantelpiece and Kara put the plates on the table, while Lena brought through the knives, spoons, and the bread.

She’d never had such an informal meal in her life, but quickly she found that it didn’t matter whether she was sitting formally at a table or slouched in an armchair. Nobody was there to see them, anyway. Lena was an excellent cook, using the herbs she’d grown in her own herb garden to season the chicken, and Kara happily tore pieces of bread from the loaf to mop up every last morsel of the meal. They drank ale with their dinner, and then Lena brought out a bottle of brandy.

Lena was delightful company, and the time passed quickly. Kara peppered her with questions about what it was like to live in the Village and about the people who lived there. Lena shared stories about the people they both knew, filling Kara in on the people she would be spending the foreseeable future with. Lena also talked about how exciting it was to run a business in the Town, since for the last few years the port had become very busy. There were always new people to meet and to trade with, and supplies came in much more frequently than they’d ever done before. Lena shared with Kara her hopes for building her business even further, perhaps even returning to Boston one day and opening a second store there; her Salem store was so busy that she’d had to hire people to run it for her.

“Shall we have some of Eliza’s cornbread and jam?” Lena asked, setting her glass down on the table and standing up. She stretched luxuriously, then shook herself out like a cat, and Kara laughed. Lena brushed her hair behind her ears and smiled at Kara.

“Yes, definitely. Shall I come to help?”

“No, no, I’ll manage. Thank you, though.”

Lena took their dinner plates and was back in a few moments with cornbread on them, and a new spoon for the jam. Kara waited for Lena to spread some jam on her cornbread and try it before she helped herself. Lena let out an appreciative hum as she took her first bite.

“This is delicious,” Lena said thickly, before following her mouthful with a small drink of brandy. “Forgive me if this is an impertinent question Kara, but I was under the impression that Eliza Danvers didn’t like me very much.”

“She thinks you are … what did she say?” Kara tapped her lips with her fingers and sipped her own brandy while Lena waited for her answer. “I asked her about you the first time that I saw you, outside the meetinghouse, do you remember?”

Lena nodded. “You looked at me so intensely. I thought that we must have known each other from somewhere, although I was certain that I’d never seen you before.”

“I hadn’t seen you there before,” Kara commented, picking up another piece of cornbread. “I was interested in you.”

Lena raised her eyebrows, then brought her legs up and tucked them into the side of the chair. “You were interested in me? Why?”

“I— I thought you were pretty,” Kara said honestly, the alcohol loosening her tongue. “And then I realized that I didn’t know your face. We’d been going to sermons there for a few weeks, and I didn’t know you. I suppose I thought it was strange. Your non-attendance was something Eliza commented on when I asked her about you.”

“Was it now?” Lena said, smiling wryly.

“Do you not always attend church?”

“No,” Lena said frankly, and Kara was surprised to hear her be so plain about it. Her surprise must have shown in her face because Lena let out a low chuckle that Kara felt in her chest. “I wonder why you are surprised, since neither of us enjoyed the sermon that we heard this past Sabbath. I admit that I have little time for being preached to by a reverend when there are things I would rather be doing elsewhere. I only went on Sunday because I— I hoped to see you. If I may be honest, I believe that we should be able to read the Bible in our own homes, and not have to go to church every Sunday. Why must we prove that we are good Puritans by being seen in church? And then, we waste a valuable day doing nothing when we could be working, or doing something fun. Sometimes, I am simply too busy to go, and I’m sure that invites … talk.”

“And do you work when you don’t attend?” Kara asked, leaning forward a little in her seat.

“Yes, sometimes I work on my sewing, or I go for walks in the woods to look for new herbs. Or, I just walk for the sake of walking, because I like it. Sometimes I read. You don’t seem particularly shocked at my non-attendance.”

Kara wasn’t. “Before I came to live with the Danvers, when I lived with my parents, sometimes we didn’t go to church every week either. They were a bit more … free-thinking than the Danvers. They were not Puritans.”

“They weren’t?” Lena asked. Kara shook her head, and Lena propped her head up with her elbow on the armchair, her hand in her hair. “When I was in your store, you told me that you moved in with the Danvers when your parents passed away. Suddenly having to live in Puritan society must have been difficult for you.”

“It was. I suddenly had less dreams for myself. Well, I suppose that I still have the dreams, but I can no longer act upon them.”

“What kind of dreams did you— do you have?”

Kara swirled her brandy around in her glass, looking down into it. How much should she say? She liked Lena very much, and it was freeing to talk to someone who thought in a similar way as she did, at least regarding church attendance. Maybe they would have similar ideas about other things, too, but unless Kara gave voice to them, she would never know.

“You are safe with me,” Lena said softly, and Kara looked up at her in surprise. “I can see the hesitation on your face,” Lena explained, leaning forward to pick her brandy glass back up off the table. “You do not need to tell me if you’d rather keep your dreams to yourself. We do not know each other very well, after all, although … although, I would like to know you better.”

“I dream that I can marry someone that I choose for myself,” Kara blurted out, and Lena’s brandy glass froze halfway to her lips.

“You have someone in mind to marry?” she asked carefully. “You are betrothed?”

“No, not yet anyway. I imagine that I will be soon, because the Danvers think I ought to marry Michael Matthews. And he would be fine, but I do not feel anything for him. Maybe in time, I could come to like him, but at the moment I really do not feel anything for him. I don’t think that I could love him.”

“And … is there someone else that you wish you could marry instead?”

“No. It is just that when I was young, my parents always told me that I should marry the man that makes me smile unbidden. I took that to mean that I should marry the person who makes me happy, rather than someone who Eliza and Jeremiah think will be a good match for prosperity and for children.”

“There, we are in complete agreement,” Lena said, sitting back and looking satisfied. “I think that people should marry for love, not money. People should marry the people who make them happy, who they at least like enough to want to spend their time with. And love … love is a dream we all search for.”

“Sometimes,” Kara started, her voice rough, “sometimes, I feel like I would prefer not to marry any man at all. I have never met a man that I thought I could love.”

At that, Lena’s eyes darkened, or perhaps Kara only imagined they did due to the lowering of the fire beside them. “Then you should not marry them, Kara.”

She stared into the fire for a few minutes, brooding over what she’d said. It was true. She never had met a man that she wanted to marry. Maybe in some way her mind was rebelling against marriage somehow because she’d been brought up to believe that she could choose her husband, not have him chosen for her because they’d be a good match. Oh, she believed in hard work and that prosperity could bring happiness, because prosperity could take away hardship. But she also thought that marrying for love brought its own happiness that was separate from financial security. Maybe it was happiness just for the sake of happiness, waking up next to someone who made you smile every day, and who was happy to see you all the time.

“No amount of money can make one person love another,” Lena said quietly. Kara looked up to find Lena looking at her seriously. “I believe that it is love that makes a marriage happy, not money. And if you do not love this man, then you should not marry him.”

“But our parents are discussing it. Arranging it, even. I don’t think that I can get out of it now, even if he isn’t who I’d choose for myself.”

“Life is too short to be so unhappy,” Lena commented. “Anything could carry us off to the next world at any moment. There could be another attack, or another pox outbreak. I could be trampled by a horse in Town tomorrow. I don’t want to die thinking that I haven’t lived, even if sometimes, what I think of as happiness doesn’t fit what society thinks it should be. Do you know what I mean?”

Kara’s mind flashed to Alex, and then to her own dreams late at night. “Yes.”

There was a beat of contemplative silence between them, the fire crackling.

After a moment, Lena took another piece of cornbread and spread some jam on it. “Enough solemnity for now,” Lena said, moving their conversation into safer waters, “or you’ll never come back here! How many games of cards do you know how to play? I have a deck. We could play, if you like?” There was still a remnant of seriousness in Lena’s face, but it was chased away when Kara smiled wickedly.

“I can beat you at anything,” she said confidently.

“We’ll see about that!”

And with that, the conversation turned light again. They played cards, each of them winning even amounts of games of chance. Lena played similarly to Alex, but she was shrewder, probably thanks to her head for business, and she was often able to guess Kara’s next move. Each time she did, she laughed with girlish delight, and Kara was having more fun than she’d had in a long time. Had she really only met Lena a few times? Was this truly the only time they’d spent any real time together? Kara almost couldn’t believe it.

5. Chapter 5

The next couple of weeks were a flurry of activity. Kara had to go into Town with Jeremiah several times to assist with the winter stocking he wanted to do. Ships arrived daily and dropped off supplies in the port, and the Town was bustling. Kara had never seen it so busy, and it was loud. There was a continual racket with dock workers rolling barrels of goods off the ships and toward their next destinations, often passing the Danvers’ store with the wood and metal clattering down the street. Horses and carts, laden with goods produced in the colony to go back to England on the ships’ return voyages, filled the streets.

Sailors weaved their way down the street, in and out of the taverns and brothels, enjoying their shore leave before they had to go back to sea for another few weeks. She heard that crossing at this time of year was rough, and although she’d never experienced sea sickness, she couldn’t imagine being so ill for a prolonged period; she likened it to the sickness many women got at the beginnings of their pregnancies.

Ships sailed most frequently between other port cities on the coast. Boston, New York, Philadelphia; there were even a few ships from Charleston. The cosmopolitan mix of people, accents, and food that moved around Salem was thrilling for Kara, and she loved the days when she was able to go for a walk down to the port to watch the goings-on.

Jeremiah and Kara were kept busy in their store, and this Saturday was no different. This morning they had already purchased a vast amount of material: the three Danvers women would be kept busy all winter making quilts and blankets out of it all. They had also sold a lot of woolen blankets and candles today, for the almanacs predicted that this winter was going to be a harsh one, and the Danvers were not the only family stocking up.

The almanacs were right: so far, it was one of the harshest winters that Kara had ever lived through. The trips between the Town and the Village were so cold that tears often sprung from her eyes and froze on her cheeks, and she had to sit on her hands to try to keep them warm. Jeremiah’s employee, Samuel Smith, had broken his leg at the start of December and been unable to work, which was why they made so many trips into the Town. They hoped that he would be better soon so that the reins could be handed back, but until then, Kara kept helping out. She was good with the customers, friendly and polite, and it varied her days.

Besides, with Lena as her friend now, time passed more quickly in the Village as well as the Town. They enjoyed each other’s company, and it gave Kara something to look forward to. They’d had dinner together thrice more since the first time, and each time had been just as wonderful as the first. Lena was an engaging companion: thoughtful and respectful, learned, clever, and full of opinions. She was also quick to laugh, charming, and playful. They were, as Lena thought, remarkably similar in many ways. Neither of them were particularly Puritan in their ideals; when they were alone in Lena’s home, Kara had quickly learnt that she could throw aside social convention. In fact, Lena actively encouraged her to do it. It was so much more relaxing being around Lena than around anyone else, and Kara had also learnt that she could speak her mind in a way that she couldn’t at home.

The moment they sat down together in Lena’s home, just the two of them talking by the fire, Kara felt a peace at her core such as she had never known. She felt … settled, complete, like she had lost something precious and then found it in an unexpected place. When Kara came to Lena’s house and they closed the door, took off their caps and their shoes, and sat next to the fire together or up in the sewing room looking at Lena’s progress, she felt at home. At peace. Happy, like she’d found where she was supposed to be.

Being around Lena felt good to Kara.

They spent hours talking about all sorts of things. She thought that she and Lena had spent more hours together in four meetings than she and her friends in Andover had spent together over periods of months. They were better, fuller hours, too; challenging Kara’s mind as well as filling it with laughter and growing affection. It was as though they were fitting months’ worth of friendship into weeks.

It was strange, though. Unlike with her other friends, Kara found an inexplicable urge to be near Lena all the time if they were together. It was if she was being drawn towards her, and she didn’t know why. With her other friends, she had never been moved to touch their hands, or felt breathless when they kissed her cheek, or held them in an embrace. And she felt breathless often because, after their first meeting, she and Lena frequently reached out to hold the other in some way. Lena often slipped her hand into Kara’s when they sat together at the fire. And Lena thought nothing of brushing her hands though Kara’s hair when it became time to put their caps back on before Kara went home; Lena’s touch in her hair was often slow, lingering, and it sent shivers down Kara’s spine. In turn, Kara became bolder with Lena. She gave in to her desire to touch Lena’s hair, plaiting and re-plaiting her soft tresses while Lena read to her from one of her many books. If she had to move around Lena, she would brush her hand over Lena’s arms, shoulders, or her waist. It was easy, tactile affection, given and received freely by each of them, and Kara loved it.

But it was also unusual, and more intense in her friendship with Lena than it had ever been with anyone before. She did not tell Alex or anyone else about it. It felt private, and intimate, and Kara kept it close to her heart.

The fluttery feeling that she’d had in her chest when she’d first seen Lena outside the meetinghouse had become so familiar to her that she came to expect it whenever she was around Lena.

As their meetings grew longer, they sank into an honesty and a trust that Kara had never had with anyone else. With society, even with her family, she held her tongue. The tongue breaketh the bone. But she never worried about that with Lena; if she voiced an opinion, Lena would ask her questions about it and why she felt that way, not tell her to hide herself. The result was that Kara felt as if she’d known her all her life, and so she was as free and honest with her as she was with herself.

And when they said goodbye at Lena’s door … Lena would hold her tightly, as if she did not want to let her go. And Kara held Lena just as tightly, trying to soak up just one more moment with her, just one more second with Lena in her arms.

Sometimes it almost felt as though they were dancing in the dying light of the fire.

Kara blew out a breath in the empty store and stood up straight, stretching her back out from where she’d been bent over polishing the counter for the last while. It couldn’t possibly get any shinier, and so she tossed the cloth into one of the cubbyholes and fetched the broom from the corner, dreading this one last task she’d been asked to perform. She groaned inwardly at the thought of the hot pies that Jeremiah had recently gone out to fetch them for lunch, and gripped her broom tighter in her hands.

She stood by the door and counted down from five. When she reached zero, she pulled the door open and slipped swiftly outside. The freezing wind whipped around her instantly, and her breath caught in her throat. She gasped to catch her breath, the frigid air creeping through her clothes and under her cap. As quickly as she could, Kara vigorously began brushing the front step of the store to rid it of the accumulated mud from the boots of the customers and the dirty snow. Within minutes, her hands were red and her teeth were chattering; she wished she’d thought to put her shawl on before she came outside, but she knew that if she went back inside to fetch it now, she’d never finish this task.

It was amazing how dirty the step got, she thought as she brushed frantically at the last bits of mud that clung to it. She was concentrating so hard on her task that she didn’t hear someone calling her name until they touched her on the shoulder.

Kara startled and dropped the broom against the door with a clatter. Straight away, the smell of a hot turkey pie and the sound of soft laughter overwhelmed her, and she looked up to find—

“Lena!” she burst out gaily, snatching her broom back up and smiling from ear to ear at the unexpected sight of her friend.

“I thought you’d heard me,” Lena replied with a crooked smile. She was standing on the walkway just to the side of the door, holding a basket over one arm. Her face was pink from the cold, and snow was dusted on the thick shawl that covered her shoulders. The snow was coming down hard and fast now, and the street had mostly emptied of people. Kara reached up with her spare hand to brush the snow from Lena’s shoulder, but Lena caught it in hers before she could. “Oh, you’re freezing! Are you finished out here? You need to warm up!”

“I’m finished, I think,” Kara said, looking down at her feet and nudging some of the freshly fallen snow off the step and into the street. A thankless task, if I ever saw one!

Lena moved past her and pushed the door open, and Kara caught the warm scent of lavender from the soap that Lena was sometimes able to purchase if ships brought it from England. It was an expensive luxury, and Kara didn’t know anyone else who used it. But now, whenever she smelled the lavender sachets around her own home, she was reminded of Lena.

She followed her into the shop and let out a sigh of relief as the heat from the small fire in the grate seemed to envelop her. Lena lay her basket on the table closest to her and held her arms out; Kara walked gladly into them, wrapping her arms around her friend. She squeezed Lena gently and heard her giggle in her ear.

“I wasn’t expecting to see you today,” Kara said when she let Lena go, but she didn’t move far.

“Ah, well, I walked past earlier and saw you with a customer. I thought— I had hoped that I could steal you away for lunch, maybe show you my store, but I see that Jeremiah isn’t here right now. Another time perhaps, but we could still eat lunch, if you like?”

“I would love to! What a wonderful surprise,” Kara replied, and Lena’s face broke out into a warm smile that had Kara’s stomach doing that fluttery thing again. “You and Jeremiah think alike. He has gone to get pies too, but it’s such a cold day and I’ve worked so hard that I could probably eat five anyway.”

Lena chuckled lowly and unwrapped her shawl from her shoulders. She folded it over the counter and then bent over to lift the pies from the basket. They were wrapped in a cloth, and when she removed it the whole store was filled with the delicious smell of roasted turkey and various herbs. Kara’s mouth watered. “Ooooh! Are they from Smith’s?”

“They are,” Lena confirmed, picking up a pie with another small cloth and holding it out to Kara. “They’re my favorite.”

Kara bit into the pie with gusto. “Mine too,” she mumbled around her mouthful.

Lena laughed before she took a daintier bite of her own.

A moment later, Jeremiah loomed in the doorway and then burst through it behind Lena, stomping loudly and shaking snow all over the place. Lena squeaked in surprise and moved towards Kara, then started to cough violently on the pie she’d been eating. Kara hesitated before clapping her on the back, and Lena’s eyes watered as she fought to get her breath under control once more.

Jeremiah apologized profusely and pulled out a jar of beer he’d bought with the pies. Kara rushed upstairs and brought down a cup, poured some beer into it, and offered it to Lena, who spluttered but drank gratefully.

Once Lena had recovered, rubbing her hand on her chest, she smiled weakly at Jeremiah.

“I’m sorry about that, Mrs Spheer,” he said. “I would’ve come through a bit more carefully had I known you were eating.”

“No harm done, Mr Danvers,” Lena replied, her voice a little rough from her coughing fit. “I just came by to see whether I could have lunch with Kara. I brought a pie for you, too, but as Kara told me, we both had the same idea.”

Jeremiah chuckled. “It seems we did, and thank you very much! You two can go upstairs if you like, and eat up there? Take another one of these pies with you to share if you’re still hungry. This is one of the coldest winters I can remember, and these pies are still nice and hot.”

Kara took the spare pie and balanced both in her hands, then led Lena through the back of the store and up the creaky, narrow stairs to the upper floor. It was almost as large as the shop floor downstairs, with a storage cupboard along one wall. There was a large table in front of the window, upon which was spread two of the quilts that Kara worked on sometimes while she was in Town with Jeremiah. She set the pies down on the corner of the table and folded the quilts up, leaving them next to her boxes of sewing supplies on the low shelf that ran the length of the other wall.

The fire in here was burning low in the grate, so Kara stoked it a little and added a few pieces of kindling and a log on top. The room wasn’t as warm as downstairs, but it wasn’t cold either, and she pulled out chairs for them to sit down at by the table.

While they finished their own pies and then split the one Jeremiah had given them, they watched the snow fall outside on the street. It was one of the swiftest snowfalls Kara had seen, quickly covering everything in white and giving the world a muffled, peaceful quality. Occasionally someone would hurry past, their hat pulled down low over their eyes and their hands jammed in their pockets or into their shawls. They exchanged news about what they’d both been doing since the last time they’d seen each other earlier in the week, and Kara told Lena about the busy morning she’d had in the store before the snow had come.

“My day has also been busy,” Lena said, wiping a crumb of pastry from the corner of her mouth with her thumb. She licked at it before continuing, and Kara suppressed a shiver. “A shipment came through on a ship from England that I’ve been waiting for for quite some time. A large amount of cloth, silk, and lace. It was supposed to be here a month ago, but the crossing was delayed.”

“Silk!” Kara exclaimed. Silk was not commonly worn in the Village; it was considered ostentatious.

“Yes,” Lena replied. “I intend to add some to some more of my quilts, and the rest I will sell to merchants trading up the coast. There isn’t much place for anything else made of silk in this society. Although …”

Kara looked up from her pie at Lena’s hesitation. “Although?”

Lena cleared her throat lightly. “I do own a— a gown made of silk, for sleeping in.”

“Oh.” Kara felt the blush creeping up her face. She probably wore it with her husband, she thought a little bitterly, and she shoved the thought as far away from her mind as she could. “I see.”

Lena raised her eyebrows, catching the change in Kara’s tone. “It’s warmer than my normal shift,” she explained, and then her eyes turned playful. “You may have noticed that it’s quite cold here in the winter.”

“Oh, really? You know, I hadn’t noticed.” Lena slapped her lightly on the arm and Kara laughed. “Oh, alright. It’s the most frigid place I’ve ever lived. Do you often order a lot of material from England?”

Lena looked down at the table and cleaned her hands with her cloth, now that she’d finished her pie. She pursed her lips together a little before she let out a little sigh. “Yes. My … um, my husband’s family live and trade there.”

Kara’s stomach twisted uncomfortably. This was the first time that Lena had mentioned her husband, the one that Eliza had said had died in mysterious circumstances. Because of that, Kara had not yet asked Lena about her husband, or about what had happened to him, not wanting to bring up bad or painful memories. She was a little surprised that Lena had taken until now to mention him, but she hadn’t wanted to push Lena, either. And so, Lena’s husband was the one major topic they had not yet discussed.

“Oh?” she asked dumbly, not knowing what else to say.

Lena fiddled with her fingers in her lap, then rested her forearms on the table and looked out of the window. Her face was troubled, and immediately Kara wanted to take the frown away somehow, to smooth it gently away from her face with her fingertips. The thought of Lena being troubled made Kara’s heart feel heavy.

“You don’t need to tell me about him if you don’t want to,” Kara said gently, sliding her hand across the counter to rest next to Lena’s.

“His name was Jack,” she said, her bottom lip trembling a little. “He was a good man, a good friend, and I loved him dearly. He died two years ago, in one of his crossings from Boston to England. Apparently, he fell overboard during a storm. They never recovered his body.”

“Oh Lena, I’m sorry,” Kara said instantly, moving her whole hand to cover Lena’s in comfort. If there was one thing in Kara’s life that she understood, it was the deaths of people she loved. Nothing ever truly took that sting away. Lena turned to look at her then, her face lined with pain, and Kara’s heart ached for her.

“I miss him. He— I just miss him. I told you that I was somewhat of an orphan?” Kara nodded. “I’m sure that someone must have told you by now that I’m a bastard. My father had relations with another woman, my mother, when he was already married with a son. When my mother died, I was brought into the Luthor family. My father, Lionel, knew the Spheer family and respected them greatly. He and Jack’s father were trading partners across the ocean, for all that the rest of Boston thought it was strange because of … they did not approve of the Spheers.”

I am … he was the only one in my life who ever understood me in a way that … in a way that nobody else could.” Lena turned her hand over so that Kara’s palm rested against hers, and for a minute neither of them spoke. Lena ran her fingertips over Kara’s knuckles, the touch gentle but deliberate, as if it were helping to ground her in the present, rather than in the painful memories of her past.

Kara was stunned. Marrying against the wishes of half of the family would have been a very difficult thing to do.

“It was nice, being married to Jack. He didn’t want, or expect, much from me other than my company and my friendship, both of which I gave to him freely. We rebelled against much of society. They were more of a family to me than the Luthors were.”

“I’m glad that you had them, and that you have them still,” Kara said sincerely.

“Thank you,” Lena said, reaching for Kara’s hand once more. “I’ve wanted to tell you about him, but I wasn’t sure how you’d react. Most people think that my marriage was … very strange. And I— didn’t want to think that you might somehow think less of me for it. That would make me very … sad.”

“It means a great deal to me that you think so,” Lena said in a watery voice. “And I am glad to know that you understand, even if you don’t know everything.” She leaned a little closer, and Kara wrapped her arm around her shoulder. Lena burrowed into her embrace, sliding her own arm around Kara’s waist, and Kara held Lena tighter.

People drowned at sea all the time, tossed overboard by storms, or falling due to simple accidents. Kara wondered where Eliza had got the idea from.

She hadn’t anticipated an emotional conversation when Lena had come over for lunch, but she was glad to have been let into Lena’s confidence about Jack. Jack, her husband, who’d loved her and then died at sea, leaving Lena alone. How awful it was that there was no grave for Lena to visit. There was a strange twisting in her stomach when she thought about how Lena had loved someone, but she was also happy to know that Lena had felt such a deep love in her heart. It was a strange contradiction of feelings, but she shoved them aside and concentrated on Lena instead.

“I don’t often get to talk about him. Our marriage … it wasn’t entirely normal, and people don’t like that. But he was my best friend, and I miss him dreadfully.”

“Well,” Kara said, drawing out the word in a drawl, “if it would make you feel better, please talk about him with me whenever you’d like to.”

“It would. You remind me of him, in a way,” Lena said. Her smile was brighter now, and it gladdened Kara’s heart. “You make me feel safe, just as he did.”

Her words warmed Kara from the inside out with their affection, and she pressed a kiss to Lena’s cheek. “You make me feel safe, too,” she replied, running her hand gently up and down Lena’s back.

By the time they made their way back downstairs, the snow had started easing off, and Jeremiah had a customer in the shop. People were passing by on the road outside again, the horse and wagon tracks leaving dirty smears in the white snow. It looked like they’d had at least a foot of snow in the last few hours, and Kara didn’t look forward to the journey home later that evening. The sky looked like it was heavy with more snow, and she almost wished that there was a bed upstairs she could sleep in instead of having to travel all the way back home once they’d closed the store.

Lena was just wrapping herself in her shawl once more when the customer left, and Jeremiah beckoned her over.

“Mrs Spheer? I would like to ask a favor of you, if I may.”

“Of course,” Lena replied, stepping up to the counter in front of Jeremiah. “Whatever I can do.”

“I know that Kara will be spending the evening at your house again on Tuesday, but while you were both upstairs, Thomas came in to tell me that Michael Jones— you know, Michael Jones? The tailor. He has been unwell for some time, but he has worsened. Thomas wants me to cover his night watch duty on Tuesday, which means that I must leave for Town earlier, and I will not be able to come to fetch Kara later in the evening.”

“Oh, I am sorry to hear about Mr Jones,” Lena said sincerely. “He is such a kind man, and I hope that he recovers quickly. But, what is this favor?”

Jeremiah grimaced. “I am sorry, for I know you both enjoy your evenings, but would it be possible for Kara to come another day?”

Kara’s heart sank, but Lena smiled quickly. “I have another suggestion. Could Kara not come to my home earlier, and perhaps work on her quilt at my house? She may also stay the night, so that she doesn’t have to worry about getting home.”

Kara’s heart jumped in her chest at the thought of having an afternoon as well as an evening with Lena, and then the night and the following morning. She was not sure where she would sleep, but it wouldn’t matter. She could not contain the smile she felt growing on her face as she turned to Jeremiah in excitement.

Jeremiah chuckled when he looked at Kara. “I take it that this is something that pleases you?” he asked, and laughed when Kara nodded. “As long as it does not inconvenience you, Mrs Spheer, then it would be a nice solution to the problem. I’m sure Eliza will bake some cornbread for your trouble. Kara told me how you enjoyed the fox grape jam.”

After they made arrangements for Tuesday, Lena gave Kara a quick hug goodbye and hastened back to her own store, saying that she had neglected it long enough.

On the way home later that evening with Jeremiah, even the heavy snow falling all around her could not dampen her spirits. She would get a whole afternoon and evening with Lena, and more besides. Almost a whole day, all told! Her whole body felt warm with excitement. They could work on their quilts together in Lena’s sewing room and talk, and then have dinner together, and maybe they could play some games in the evening. The possibilities spread out before her, and Kara smiled all the way home.

A moment later, she heard the front door close; Jeremiah was going back to the meetinghouse to find out what had happened with Lena, and whether she had pled guilty to witchcraft or not.

Sat by the low, crackling fire in the bedchamber, she thought about how Lena must be feeling. Scared, alone. They would have done the examination by now; they would’ve found the mole under her breast that Kara loved so much. Kara ached to think of it; that little mole in that secret, intimate space known only to Kara and Lena, would prove to be her undoing. Lena would have to decide whether to say she was guilty or not, and Kara had no idea what she was going to say. It hurt like a physical pain in her heart that she wouldn’t even be there when Lena had to make her announcement. Kara knew that she had been the only person in that meetinghouse besides Sam that was on Lena’s side.

She was reasonably sure that Sam knew something of Kara and Lena’s relationship, but she’d still never said anything to Kara about it. She’d caught Sam looking between them sometimes, especially in the last few months, and smiling to herself. It just seemed that Sam tacitly accepted what was going on in her home without any fuss, which amazed Kara. If Sam did know, and she didn’t mind, then in this society she was a real rarity.

Unlike the Danvers.

On some level Kara knew that they had no idea what her real relationship with Lena was like, and so could not understand her feelings of grief and worry, but she also knew that they would never accept it if they did find out. They thought that her actions were born of witchcraft, not of love. Kara wanted to kiss Lena, love Lena, every day for the rest of her life, and that had nothing to do with witchcraft. The Danvers would never understand that.

As she thought of Lena again, she felt a surge of energy; she jumped up from her seat and started pacing the room. Back and forth, back and forth between the fire and the door. There was nobody in the room with her, so she went to her side of the bed and carefully took the little square of blue silk out of the tear in the mattress.

This little blue ocean scene that Lena had made for her, knowing how much she loved the larger quilt. Kara felt her heart overflow with love as she thought about Lena had sat in her sewing room, taking the time to make this little piece just for Kara. She imagined Lena sitting there at her table with the sun shining through the window, smiling to herself as she embroidered a tiny golden fish into the silk. Kara ran her thumb gently across the fish and thought about Lena, how much she adored her.

She’d never expected to fall in love like this. There was a peace and a comfort in loving Lena, and being loved by her in turn, that she never thought she’d find. And loving a woman? Kara understood now why she’d never felt any desire to marry a man, why she’d been reluctant to court a man, and why feigning excitement about the very idea of marriage to a man had been so difficult. She wasn’t sure whether it was all men, or whether it was all women, but one thing she was sure of.

The only person she wanted was Lena. She wanted to wake up with her every morning and kiss her. She wanted to spend her life finding new ways to make Lena smile and laugh, to laugh right along with her. She wanted to run her hands through Lena’s soft hair, and hold her in her arms, and be held by Lena in return. The mornings where they’d woken up together with soft kisses and gentle smiles, Lena’s fingertips trailing down Kara’s face, her arms, her side, were the happiest of Kara’s life. Lena was the companion to her soul. It wasn’t just that she found Lena beautiful, although she did. It wasn’t just that she wanted to always kiss Lena, be holding her hand, although she did. Lena was her favorite person, her best friend, her confidante, and her lover. She wanted to spend whole days and evenings and nights just talking with Lena, learning the ways she saw the world, finding out which trees she liked the best, or what she thought about each Shakespeare play.

Kara didn’t want to imagine going through the world without Lena by her side. Such a life would always be missing something vital, would always be grey, would never be complete. Now that she knew what loving Lena was like, she only wanted to love her more. She wanted to know what their love could grow to be.

And she wasn’t going to be able to do that without Lena. Her heart already felt as though it was ripped in half with Lena in such danger. How could she live a whole life without her?

Kara felt determination run through her, spreading through her and giving strength to her mind, her heart, and her body.

If she wanted a life with Lena, then she needed Lena to be free. How could she get her free of the watch house? What would she do if she managed to get Lena out? Where would they go, and how would they manage?

As she brooded, staring at the fire, Kara realized that if she were to somehow break Lena free, they couldn’t stay in the Village. They wouldn’t even be able to stay in the Town, because they would re-arrest Lena, and no doubt arrest Kara too. For Lena’s safety, and for Kara’s, they’d have to flee. Kara’s hands shook and her breath came quickly to her at the thought of having to leave the Village, leave her family, her home. It was a frightening thought, but the alternative was Lena being found guilty, being executed. And she knew that she could never bear the idea of that, not when Lena was only guilty of loving her. Not when Kara could do something about it. Not when she could save Lena from hanging.

Not only would they have to leave, they’d have to leave very quickly. But how could she do it? Breaking Lena out of a watch house to save her life, somehow getting them both far away, leaving behind everyone she’d ever known … it was daunting. Kara came up with idea after idea, discarding most of them. Nobody came to disturb her, and she was grateful for the silence so that she could think.

After the fire had burned low and she added more logs, the front door opened and closed once more. She heard Jeremiah’s voice, but then someone else’s. A woman’s voice! Kara stood up and strained her ears, then recognized the voice of Sam.

Sam!

Kara crept across to the door and cracked it open a fraction so that she could hear better.

“I’m afraid it cannot wait,” Sam was saying.

“But she’s not been well. It would be better to leave her alone to rest,” Eliza murmured. “Under the circumstances … the news will just upset her.”

“Whether it does or doesn’t, I’m afraid I must see Kara,” Sam replied, a note of authority in her voice that Kara had never heard before.

She closed the door quietly, then quickly made the bed and tucked the ocean silk square into the rip once more. She smoothed her hands over her wrinkly dress, trying to flatten it, as two sets of footsteps came up the creaky stairs. She wiped her face with her sleeve and sat by the fire just in time for the door to open and Eliza to come in, followed by a grave-looking Sam.

“Widow Arias?” Kara asked politely, nodding at her while desperately trying to ignore the feeling of dread sitting low in her stomach at the expression on Sam’s face. She looked like someone had died.

“Kara,” Sam replied, before turning to Eliza. “Thank you, Mrs Danvers. I’ll just talk to Kara for a few minutes and then I’ll take my leave. I would be grateful if you could give us some privacy.”

Eliza’s eyes darted between Kara and Sam. She looked torn, like she wanted to insist that she stayed in the room, but Sam drew herself up to her full height and her stern face brooked no argument. Eliza’s silent protest buckled under the weight of Sam’s assertive expression, and she left the room, closing the door behind her.

With an unspoken agreement, both women listened carefully until they heard Eliza’s footsteps on the stairs once more, then Sam rushed across to Kara. She drew her into the furthest corner of the room, as far away from the door, and any listening ears, as they could get.

“What’s happened?” Kara asked frantically. “Lena? Is she—”

“She’s refusing to plead one way or the other,” Sam said quickly. “Neither guilty nor not guilty.”

“But …” Kara said, her mind reeling with that information. “But what does that mean?”

“It means that they’ll try to force a plea from her,” Sam replied, her eyes flashing with anger and worry. “And they do that by— by crushing.”

“Crushing?!” Kara cried, aghast at the sound of it, at the idea of it. She swayed on her feet and groped for words. “They’ll— they’ll put her on the ground with a board over her and—”

“And add rocks to it, crushing her under the weight of it, until she gives her answer one way or the other,” Sam said grimly, looking sick.

“But she could be killed!”

“Yes,” Sam said, her voice heavy. “Yes, she’ll bear the weight until it kills her, knowing Lena. She’s far too noble. She won’t want to make things worse for you by pleading not guilty to witchcraft, and she won’t give them the satisfaction of winning by pleading guilty either, especially when she hasn’t done anything wrong and there’s nothing to please guilty for.”

Tears welled in Kara’s eyes, spilling down her cheeks at the thought of Lena just … just … having that kind of end. She couldn’t stomach it. Being hung was one thing, but crushed to death? The room swam in front of her, and the next thing she knew, Sam was sitting her down on the seat in front of the fire.

“I know this is hard,” Sam said, “I know how hard this is. Listen, I know about you and Lena. I know about the relationship that you two have.”

Kara’s eyes snapped up to Sam’s. So it was true: Sam did know. Kara wiped the tears from her face. “You don’t mind it?”

“Of course I don’t mind it,” Sam replied, crouching down beside Kara and shaking Kara’s knee. “Of course not. Lena is one of the best people I’ve ever known, and Kara, you make her so happy. And she loves you. She’d do anything for you. She’d die to keep you safe.” Sam’s eyes were swimming with tears too, and she sniffled quietly. “I can’t bear the thought of her dying, but I don’t know what to do. I don’t … but I had to come and tell you. Someone told me after her examination finished that she hadn’t pled one way or the other. And that means the— the crushing,” she finished in a broken whisper.

“I can’t let her do that, I can’t,” Kara choked out. “Sam, I want to free her somehow. I can’t let this stand. We have— I have to do something.”

“We,” Sam stated quietly, putting her hand over Kara’s. “If you’re going to do something, then I’m going to help you. Lena’s like my sister, and I’d do anything I could to help her. I just don’t know what we could do.”

They sat in contemplative silence for a few minutes, Kara in the chair, and Sam adjusting herself to kneel on the floor next to Kara. It must have hurt her knees, but Sam didn’t complain. How could they rescue Lena?

And like the dim light of the morning that promised another day, an idea slowly grew in Kara’s mind.

“Sam, where would they do the cr— you know …”

“In the Town,” Sam replied, “so that if she does confess, she can be taken straight to the jail. And if she—”

Kara held up a hand, not wanting to hear the end of that sentence. “So they’re going to have to move her from the watch house to the Town.”

“Yes.”

“And when are they going to do that?”

“Sunrise tomorrow,” Sam replied quietly.

“Tomorrow,” Kara murmured. “Tomorrow…”

Kara thought about the ideas she’d had, the vague ideas that had been running through her mind ever since Lena had been arrested. She sifted through them, rejecting any ideas that needed time to plan because there was no time. Anything preparations that had to be made, anything that had to be done, had to be completed by tonight or at least ready to go by the morning.

Panic rushed through her at the thought that this could very well be her last day in the Village, her last day with the Danvers, but she roughly shoved that painful thought aside. If she didn’t act, Lena could very well die tomorrow. And even if she didn’t die, she would be thrown in jail until they’d hang her, and she’d die anyway. She could always try to fix it with the Danvers later when Lena’s life didn’t hang in the balance. Abandoning the Danvers after everything they’d done for her made her feel sick, but the thought of Lena’s death made her feel sicker.

They couldn’t break Lena out of the watch house. It was guarded, for one thing, and anything they could do would make noise and attract attention. They’d have to somehow rescue Lena when she was being taken to the Town, as well as overpower the guards. And Sam had Ruby …

“Sam, you have Ruby. You can’t be involved, what if they arrest you? What will happen to Ruby?”

Sam looked up at her from where she was sat on the floor. “We’ll leave Salem. I’m a midwife, Ruby’s in training to be one. We could find work anywhere. Boston, maybe. Lena has a house in Boston. I’m sure we could stay there with her. I know that a friend of hers is looking after it for her, and her store there too.”

Kara thought about it. If they were willing, yes, Sam and Ruby could live in Boston … but Lena’s family lived in Boston. Kara felt like bees were buzzing in her brain, like she was on the brink of thinking something important. Lena’s family … suddenly, Kara felt sick as some of the pieces clicked into place.

“The judge today in the examination … he knew far too much about Lena. About her marriage, about her mother,” she said, reaching out for Sam’s shoulder. “How did he know so much? It wasn’t just rumor. He knew that she was the sole heir in Jack’s will before she told him. He asked her about her birth mother. Sam, when did Eve start working for Lena? Did she ever mention it?”

“Eve started working for her after she married Jack,” Sam said, looking at Kara curiously. “And when Lena moved here four years ago, Eve came with her.”

“Eve came to spy on her for whatever reason,” Kara said angrily, “because Lena’s— Lillian and Lex didn’t want her to marry him. I bet that they sent her here so keep an eye on her. And they were furious about being left out of Jack’s will. How else would the judge know what to ask? Lillian and Lex arrived so quickly after Lena’s arrest. How would they know to come unless Eve somehow got word to them?”

Sam’s face blanched. “She’s the one who told them about … she must have known about you two somehow. And all these awful witchcraft accusations at the moment … she must have seen that as an opportunity to get rid of Lena. With her gone, if she hasn’t left a will behind, everything of hers would pass to her nearest male relative. To Lex.”

“Has she got a will?” Kara asked quickly.

“Not that I’m aware of,” Sam replied, still pale.

“Then there’s nothing we can do about that,” Kara said, feeling sick that Lex would throw his own sister to the wolves just to get his hands on a little more wealth. “Not for now. Forget that part. Does Lillian know you? Or Lex? Have you ever met?”

Sam shook her head, and Kara was relieved. “No, I’ve never met either of them. They’ve never visited, and I’ve lived here all my life.”

“So if we were to all somehow get to Boston,” Kara said, “if you were to live in Lena’s house there, they would not know you.”

“Only Eve would know,” Sam said, and Kara’s blood boiled in anger at the thought of the depth of Eve’s betrayal. “But from what Lena has told me, Eve has no right to enter the house in Boston. She might know where it is, but Lena’s friends won’t let her in. Lillian and Lex are likewise unwelcome.”

Kara thought quickly. “Then we have somewhere to go. All we need to do is get ourselves there, and we can figure the rest out later. Sam. When you go downstairs, ask Eliza and Jeremiah whether you can borrow the horse and the cart. Give them any reason you like, they’ll lend them to you. I’ll have to pay them back, somehow, but I’ll think of it later. When you get them home, pack up …” Kara leaned forward and took Sam’s hands in hers. “Pack up Lena’s things, anything that she values. Any important documents, Jack’s will if it’s here, letters between Lena and his parents, physick herbs, and anything that she could sell for money. But also … the forest quilt on the wall. And there is another one—”

“I know the one you mean,” Sam said quietly, squeezing Kara’s hands. “The one of yours and Lena’s hands, joined by the fire. I saw her making it. It was when I finally asked her if there was something between the two of you.”

In spite of the urgency of their conversation, Kara couldn’t help but give in to the warmth that Sam’s words brought her and ask, “what did she say?”

Sam smiled. “She said that you made her feel like she wanted to walk down the path in your forest quilt,” she said, and Kara’s eyes brimmed with tears once more. “She said that you gave her hope, that she cared for you very much. She was … she was happy, Kara. And you’ve only made her happier.”

“She makes me happy too,” Kara whispered.

Sam squeezed her hands again. “I know. So I’ll take the quilt and the other gifts you’ve given each other, Lena’s valuables, the—”

“The ocean quilt,” Kara interrupted.

“I’ll pack that too,” Sam agreed with a nod. “I’ll pack anything I can fit. The rifle from the kitchen, too, if Eve hasn’t taken it. But what then?”

Kara leaned forward and explained the rest of her plan to Sam, and she was grateful when Sam did nothing but listen and nod, and ask questions when she needed to. She didn’t question any part of the idea that Kara had come up with, instead just quietly accepting it. They had very little time to put into action anything more elaborate, and they talked for a further ten minutes to make sure they both understood everything.

When they were finished, Sam stood up. “I must go,” she said, pulling Kara to her feet too. They regarded each other silently for a moment, allies in the fight to save Lena’s life. “I am glad beyond anything that Lena has you in her life,” Sam said, her voice soft. “I don’t know many people who would be willing to give up so much for someone they loved, especially when society … would not be kind to the kind of relationship that you two share.”

“Thank you, Sam,” Kara said sincerely, feeling a little choked with emotions the day had brought her. “Thank you for everything you’re willing to risk for Lena. I know she will be grateful forever. As I am and will always be.”

Sam smiled at her once more. “I’ll go and start the preparations. I’ll set Ruby to baking.”

“What are we going to do about the guards?” Kara asked abruptly. She and Sam couldn’t fight the guards that would be transporting Lena to the Town. What if they were armed?!

Sam’s eyes flashed mischievously, and despite the worry flooding her veins, Kara couldn’t help but smile in response. “Leave the guards to me,” Sam said. “I have an idea. I’ll see you tomorrow morning, Kara. Be ready.”

Trust in Sam helped to alleviate some of the panic that had enveloped Kara. “I will.”

And with that, Sam surprised Kara by wrapping her in a hug, before giving her a solemn nod and leaving the room. Kara listened to her make her way downstairs, then heard the rumble of voices in the front room. Not long after that, Kara heard the rattle of the horse and cart, and looked out of the window briefly to see Sam leading the horse down the street and towards Lena’s house.

Now alone again, Kara looked around the room and tried to think about what she could take with her. Truth be told, there wasn’t much here that was hers that she wanted to take. Just the blue silk square and some of her clothes, that was all. And she’d have to prepare it all and hide it before she left in the morning. She supposed that she wouldn’t even be able to sleep if she wanted to be awake in time.

Kara shifted from foot to foot in the room, wondering what she should do first. Most of the plan wouldn’t be able to be put into action until the morning, but there were a few things she could do. She was still alone in the room, and maybe she should take advantage of that. Listening carefully by the door for a moment, to make certain that nobody was coming, she turned to face the room. She was thankful that she and Alex did their sewing work in their bed chamber; she fetched an unused piece of linen and spread it over the bed. From the chest where her clothing was stored, to keep away moths, she pulled out two of her dresses, a spare shift, her thickest shawls, a cape for bad weather, three pairs of long woolen stockings, petticoats, and her second pair of leather shoes. She threw them all onto the linen on the bed, flattened everything as much as she could, then bundled it together with a piece of string. It wasn’t a very large bundle once she was done, since she didn’t own very much.

She stared at the bundle, then looked around the room. She really didn’t own much, but this was still her home. The Danvers were still her family, and what she was about to do tomorrow would devastate them. She would be breaking the law whether the plan was successful or not, and the consequences of her actions would be felt across the whole Village. Everyone would know what she had done, whether they thought it was because of witchcraft or not.

Lena. Their being discovered in the woods, and Lena’s arrest. Her examination, and her refusal to plead. Sam’s visit. Leaving the Danvers. Leaving her home. It all whirled around in Kara’s head, and she felt herself become breathless. She felt hot, and when she wiped the back of her shaking hand across her forehead, it came away damp. All of a sudden the room suddenly felt very close and airless, and she crossed the floor and fumbled with the window lock until she pushed the window open. She let the breeze blow into her face and took deep breaths of cooler air, her hand on her forehead, until she felt calmer.

A few moments later, she let out a sigh of relief, and turned to face the room once more with her hands on her hips. Even doing just some packing, putting some kind of plan into motion, helped to settle her jangling nerves and sick stomach.

What she was about to do, what she and Sam were about to do, was illegal, outrageously dangerous, and something she could never come back from. She couldn’t think of the consequences of it, because if she did, she’d never manage to get started, and Lena would die. Once more she tamped down the thought of abandoning the Danvers after all they’d done for her. But she couldn’t just do nothing when Lena was in such danger, not when her life hung in the balance. Her sadness at leaving the Danvers, if this plan came off, would have to be something she dealt with later. She only had room for one catastrophe at a time.

And yet, her mind shot between the two notions: thinking about it, and trying not to think about it. She felt sicker and sicker as the sun moved across the sky, counting down the minutes until she had to act. Kara put her face in her hands.

Just then, she heard someone on the stairs, and in a panic, she pulled the window closed, then put the bundle of clothes on the floor and shoved it as far under the bed as she could. No sooner had Kara stood up straight once more than the door creaked open, and to her relief, only Alex came in.

Alex shut the door quickly behind her, then rushed over to Kara, throwing her arms around her.

“Kara! I’ve been so worried. I haven’t been able to talk to you for days!”

Kara squeezed Alex, then stepped back, holding on to Alex’s upper arms. She regarded her sister, and her heart broke in her chest at having to leave her behind. Leaving Alex? It would be the hardest part. She felt tears well up in her eyes once more and she swallowed around a sudden lump in her throat.

“What’s wrong?” Alex asked immediately, and Kara let out a choked, wet laugh, before bursting into tears. Alex led Kara over to the bed and sat her down on it, wrapping her arms around Kara’s shoulders. Not wanting to alert Eliza, Kara tried to muffle her sobs as best as she could in Alex’s shoulder, but it was hard. It was so hard. She didn’t know how to live without Alex.

As she hiccupped herself into silence, she wondered how much she could say to Alex. Alex, who was looking at her so kindly and with such concern. And then she realized that if this was going to be the last conversation she could ever have alone with Alex, then she wanted it to be the most truthful one she could have.

“I’m going to rescue Lena,” she said in an uneven voice, then bit her lip.

Alex looked aghast. “You’re going to what?!”

“I’m going to rescue Lena. Please don’t tell Eliza or Jeremiah. They won’t understand and it’s illegal I know but I can’t let Lena die, Alex! I love her!”

There was a silence while Alex gaped at her, and then:

“She didn’t bewitch you, did she?” Alex asked slowly, and Kara shook her head. “She hasn’t— she didn’t put you under some kind of spell, like everyone’s saying?”

“No, she didn’t. Alex, I love her. I love her the way everyone says that I should love a husband. But I’ve never … I’ve never loved a man. I’ve only loved Lena.”

Alex stared at her for a few moments, and then her eyes cleared in understanding. “You love her.”

“Yes.”

“You love Lena.”

“Yes, Alex.”

“You love a woman,” Alex stated in a hushed whisper. “You mean that you … you don’t want to marry Michael Matthews at all. If you could, you’d marry Lena, right?”

Kara nodded, her eyes on Alex’s wide ones. “Yes, if I could, in the future I would marry her. I’d spend forever with her, because I— I care for her so much, Alex, and I love her, and I can’t be without her.”

“I can’t believe this,” Alex said, and then shook her head. “No, I can believe it. You haven’t talked about Michael for months, and you hardly talked about him or any other man for years before that either. But every chance you get, you’ve gone to see Lena, or she’s come here. And you’ve looked … you’ve looked so happy. I’ve never seen anyone look as happy as you do when she’s around or when you’re talking about her.”

Despite her worry, Kara smiled. “She does make me happy. She makes me happy in a way that I never thought I could be. I didn’t understand … for years, I didn’t know why I felt nothing for any man I’ve met. Surely, I would eventually find one that I would love? But I never did. And then I met Lena and we spent a lot of time together and somehow my heart … I just knew. And … and Alex, you helped me with that.”

“I did?”

“You— do you remember,” Kara asked, her voice lowering to a whisper of her own, “do you remember years ago you told me late at night that you didn’t want to marry a man?”

Her eyes guarded now, Alex nodded. “I remember that.”

Kara took one of Alex’s hands in hers. “I thought about that. I thought about that a lot. And finally I had the idea that maybe it meant that you … maybe you meant that you wanted to marry a woman instead. Alex … I don’t know if that’s what you meant, but the more that I thought about it, the more I found myself thinking the same thing. I don’t want to marry a man. I know now that want to be with Lena. And I know that she and I can’t marry, but I want to spend my life with her anyway.”

Ale’s eyes were the ones brimming with tears now, and she clutched at both of Kara’s hands. “I did mean that, but I’m so scared of it. I have kept it inside all these years, until I told you, but I never thought that you would be the same as me … and Lena, too? Knowing that I’m not alone comforts me. She makes you happy. I could see it. How long have you and Lena been … how long have you been … a couple?” she asked, her voice low.

“Since before the end of December,” Kara replied. “So, so the kiss in the woods … it wasn’t the first. There have been hundreds. Too many to count.”

“God would be scandalized,” Alex said with a wet-sounding laugh.

Kara smiled. “Let him be scandalized, then.”

Alex cleared her throat and played nervously with Kara’s hands. “So … so what are you going to do?”

Kara’s heart sank once more at the thought of the daunting task, and what it would mean for the Danvers, in front of her. “I have to rescue Lena. I’ve only got one chance to do it. Sam came around earlier and told me that Lena is refusing to plead either guilty or not guilty to witchcraft, so they’re going to try to force it out of her tomorrow. But the method might kill her, Alex, and even if it doesn’t and she pleads something, they’ll probably hang her anyway and I just can’t— I can’t let that happen. Please understand…” Kara beseeched. “I have to do something. But it means that I can’t stay here.”

There was a silence while Alex digested what she’d said, and then her hands tightened around Kara’s. “They’ll arrest you if they catch you breaking Lena out of the watch house. Or if they catch up with you afterward.”

“Yes,” Kara replied solemnly. “And we can’t stay in Town for the same reason. And we can’t stay in Boston because Sam and I think that Lillian and Lex are actually behind all of this, because of Lena’s servant Eve. They really didn’t like that Lena inherited everything when her husband died. They didn’t get any of it. And Lena’s fairly wealthy and I think they want what she has. I don’t know all the details and I don’t know much about them other than that they’re horrible people. But we may not be able to stay in Boston for very long.”

“What about New York?” Alex asked, her voice quivering now. “Or Philadelphia?”

“I don’t know, I don’t know,” Kara said. “I haven’t thought much past getting Lena to safety. Sam and I, we have a plan—”

“Sam?” Alex asked, sitting up a little straighter, and Kara could see the faux nonchalance in her eyes. “She’s helping you with this?”

“She is,” Kara confirmed. “She loves Lena like a sister, and she wants to help. We’re going to try to get Lena out, and then probably go to Boston, at least until we decide what to do next. Lena’s mother and her brother live in Boston, though, and they’re … not very nice people. They’ll probably try to get Lena arrested again, and that’s why we probably won’t stay there long but we can’t plan anything until we’ve got Lena out … but … Alex. Alex, I don’t want to leave you,” Kara said, her chest feeling tight. “I’m thinking about breaking the law and it’s to save Lena’s life but I can’t live without you, either! And saving Lena’s life … it means leaving you behind.”

Kara’s voice was wavering more and more as she felt panic settling into her bones.

The most exciting thing about the quilts for Victoria was that Elizabeth Ruston had traced her ancestry back through the Civil War, the Revolution, and all the way back to the Salem Witch Trials, where the first mentions of her family entered into the records. She was descended from a woman called Samantha Arias, known in Salem records as the Widow Arias. There were few records of Samantha before she’d had a daughter, Ruby, but there was proof in letters that she had known a woman called Lena Luthor, who had founded the Luthor Quilting Company in 1695 in New York.

And that was what really excited Victoria.

The Luthor Quilting Company, or LQC, the subject of the exhibition that Victoria was working on, was a small company founded and run principally by a woman, Lena, which was unusual for the time. There was another woman who cropped up in the records, though, named Kara Danvers, sometimes named Kara Luthor. The discrepancy between the names had caused much interest among historians, who could not confirm the link between them. It didn’t help that not much was known about Kara Danvers, other than in a few documents from Salem and some more from New York; she hadn’t been much involved in the business side of LQC. It was one of the greatest losses in historical study that private citizens, especially women, almost always faded from the historical record unless they were mentioned in court records or wills.

All those millions and millions of lives that were lived, and that people today knew next to nothing about. But Lena Luthor had existed, and so had Kara Danvers. And the quilt exhibition had given them a voice in history. Victoria wanted to give them a greater one.

The Luthor quilts were renowned for their beauty, their delicacy. Victoria was sure that she would recognize Lena Luthor’s hand in a quilt anywhere, because she had a flair for elegance in her work that Victoria had never seen the equal of. The Museum had collected a range of quilts made by Lena, as well as by Kara, and every time Victoria laid eyes on them, they took her breath away.

And in the very next room were some of the oldest Luthor quilts in existence. They had been in the Ruston family for generations, and some were mentioned in the Arias-Danvers letters that the Museum held. And today, she’d finally get to see them.

Victoria took a deep breath, and pushed the door open.

The storage room was also humidity- and climate-controlled. Storage room was really the wrong term for it, Victoria often thought, for it was also a display room. True, there were large shelves and smaller rooms off it where the large, flat boxes held clothes, quilts, blankets, tapestries, handkerchiefs, or any other number of items made of fabric. But it was also a bright and airy room, with large windows covered with beige roller blinds; they let in natural light to complement the movable spotlights that conservators and other museum workers used to see a piece better. There were three large tables, upon which huge pieces of fabric could be spread.

On the first one, already unboxed, was a large item wrapped in acid-free tissue paper. It was clearly one of the quilts, and Victoria’s excitement grew. She’d deliberately avoided seeing any of the photos of the pieces mentioned in the will, wanting her very first sight of them to be in person.

“Hey Charlie,” she called to her assistant, a young woman working on her master’s degree in early modern social history.

“Hi!” Charlie replied, stepping out from behind one of the shelves and smiling at her. “You look like all your Christmases have come early.”

“It certainly feels that way,” Victoria murmured, moving closer to the table with awe. An early Luthor quilt was right there, something that Lena Luthor herself had made in her early twenties, and she was about to unfold it for the first time. Victoria felt an overwhelming sense of respect and deference as she pulled her gloves onto her shaky hands, her eyes on the quilt the whole time.

“I took the liberty of requesting the Arias-Danvers letters for you in an hour’s time,” Charlie said, pulling on gloves of her own, “in case you needed to cross-reference something in these new ones with those. The Ruston letters are over there,” she said, pointing at a small, ornately-carved wooden chest sitting on the second display table.

Victoria smiled graciously at Charlie. “You’re a saint. Thank you,” she replied. Charlie was right: it really was like Christmas. The quilt, silk pieces, letters, other objects; she’d get to see them all today. But first … she turned her attention back to the quilt.

The room was quiet, the only sound the ticking of the clock on the wall, as the two women stood on opposite sides of the table with the folded quilt between them.

“It’s funny,” Victoria said quietly. “I feel a little nervous. Is that weird?”

“Not at all,” Charlie replied, her voice just as quiet. “You’ve spent a long time studying Lena Luthor and her work.”

“I feel … I don’t know. I don’t know how to explain it. It’s like there’s another person in the room with us, you know?” Victoria said, reaching her hand out towards the quilt. “It’s survived all these years, and now it’s come to us. And maybe it’s as old as some of the ones on display, or even older. Maybe it was with Lena Luthor during the Witch Trials …”

“The letters might tell us more,” Charlie said, her eyes holding as much reverence as Victoria felt. “But … would you like to open this?”

“I would love to.”

Together they reached forward towards the quilt. They carefully unfolded it, the white tissue paper crinkling under their gloves. The quilt was delicate, showing signs of wear on the back; Victoria surmised that the quilt had been hung on the wall for several years, perhaps a long time. The thick, brown protective cloth had done its job, though; she could see no sign so far that it had worn through onto what she was sure would be a beautiful design on the front. Victoria could feel her excitement mounting as they reached the final fold. The quilt had been folded in quarters, and just one final pull would reveal the quilt to them both.

“Are you ready?” Charlie asked.

Victoria let out an adrenaline-fueled giggle and blushed when Charlie raised an eyebrow at her. Clearing her throat, she tipped her head from side to side and shook out her arms. “I’m ready.”

“Are you sure?”

“I’m … yeah. I’m sure,” Victoria replied firmly.

Charlie stepped back, and Victoria held her breath as she leaned over and picked up the edge of the quilt. Her fingers trembled, and she pulled it carefully. Charlie let out a little gasp at what she could see, her face breaking into a smile as Victoria gently lifted the edge of the quilt towards her.

When it was finally laid out, she let out her breath in a whoosh. Her next breath caught in her throat and tears sprung up in her eyes as she swept her gaze over the astonishing sight in front of her.

It was an ocean scene, embroidered onto blue silk. Victoria recognized it as a Lena Luthor original instantly. With a bright smile, Victoria stepped closer, holding a shaking hand over the design of a fish, delicately picked out in orange thread. The ocean looked alive on the table. There were shoals of flashing silver fish, a crab hiding behind the waving frond of a sea plant and others walking along the seabed. There were rocks, shells, a pink starfish, and what looked like seaweed floating on the surface of the ocean. To the left, a small wave lapped at the sandy shore. And on the bottom right, two fish side by side, one dark and one light.

“It’s beautiful. It’s beautiful,” Victoria managed, her voice tight with barely-restrained emotion. “And it’s hers.”

There was no doubt in her mind that Lena Luthor had embroidered this. It was full of the exquisite detail that she was known for, and it contained the same fish that had appeared in some of her later works. Some of the colour had faded over the years, leaving the blue lighter; Victoria suspected that it had originally been a deep blue. The fading took nothing away from the piece, though, and she marvelled at the craftsmanship displayed in front of her.

“It’s truly remarkable,” Charlie said, her voice full of awe. “The detailing is absolutely exceptional. These tiny fish here, the extra stitching in the fronds over here,” she said, her hand hovering just above one of the plants. Victoria leaned closer, and saw the different threads of green in it, evidently stitched to reflect the sunlight filtering through the ocean water to the plant.

The reason that the LQC had been so successful was that Lena had been one of the most talented seamstresses and embroiderers that had, in Victoria’s opinion, ever lived. Each quilt was made of high-quality silk, and would have taken months upon months of hard work to make. They generally always featured nature scenes; there were oceans, rock pools, forest floors, meadows, and woods. Each had the extraordinary detail that Lena had become known for, and they all fetched a high price when sold. Records showed that Lena had been a wealthy woman, but even if she hadn’t been, she would have made a very good living from her quilts alone. She used the finest imported European materials; LQC documents showed that the London-based Spheer family were the main suppliers. LQC quilts occasionally turned up in auction, but overall there were only about 22 known surviving examples, maybe 16 of which were made by Lena’s own hands.

Victoria thought that this ocean quilt was one of the most beautiful she’d seen.

“We should prepare this for display,” Victoria commented, lowering her hand slowly. She brushed her finger lightly over the orange fish, tracing the threads from the head to the tail, and closed her eyes. She imagined Lena, over three hundred years ago, sitting in a room somewhere, this quilt spread out over a table. Had she sewed in sunlight, or by candlelight, when she’d carefully threaded the needle through the silk and brought this fish to life? What could she hear as she’d worked? What had the weather been like outside?

Nobody knew what Lena looked like. Victoria wished that there was some record of it, even if only so that she could have a clearer picture of the remarkable woman Lena must have been. To have created these beautiful works of art, to found a business, to own stores in Boston and Salem … yes, a remarkable woman. Victoria felt like she knew her a little. Lena clearly poured her heart and soul into every quilt she’d ever made, her hands bringing scenes to life on the silk. Yet, little was known of Lena herself. What kind of person had she been? It was her work that was remembered, except for one very significant moment in time that marked her out.

Lena Luthor had been accused of witchcraft during the Salem Witch Trials, and escaped.

She had not been the only escapee during the Trials. There had been several, including Mary Bradbury, the ancestor of the author Ray Bradbury. But the circumstances around her escape had been unusual, because nobody knew how it had come about.

As far as the records showed, Lena Luthor, as Lena Spheer, was arrested for witchcraft at the end of April 1692. She had been accused by an Eve Teschmacher, of whom almost no records existed. She had undergone an examination in the meetinghouse in the Village, not the Town, and had been accused of afflicting none other than Kara Danvers, who had gone to New York with Lena from Salem. Records also showed that she had been examined for witches’ marks, and that one had been found, although what it was had not been recorded. She had refused to plead guilty or not guilty to witchcraft, and was sentenced to pressing or crushing as Giles Corey also would be, in order to force a plea. But somehow, between being locked up in the Village and being taken to the Town for the pressing, she had vanished, turning up in Boston briefly before finally surfacing in New York.

Victoria stood in front of the ocean quilt that Lena herself had created, and thought about her. There were so many unknowns in Lena’s story. The records did not state precisely why Lena had been arrested for witchcraft. Nobody had any idea why she had not been re-arrested in Boston or in New York for escaping custody in Salem. What relation was Kara Danvers to Lena, and why had she travelled with Lena, if she had been supposedly afflicted by her in Salem? As was the fate of so many other questions, historians resigned themselves to never knowing the answers.

Lena was a person who loomed large in Victoria’s life and work. Yes, she felt like she knew her a little, as though threads of the present were connected to Lena in the past through the remarkable quilts and mysteries she’d left behind. It was as if Lena were reaching through the ages, and Victoria imagined once more Lena’s hands on the quilt in front of her, and she lightly lay her own hands on top of it.

Victoria wondered whether Lena would be amused at how desperately Victoria wanted to solve the mysteries of her life. She wondered whether it would make Lena smile. She liked to think that it would, even though she had no way of knowing.

She also wondered what Lena would think of the fact that her quilts were hanging in museums, that her embroidery was studied by historians. What would Lena think if she knew that people marveled at the work she had created, work that echoed through the years and mesmerized people so many years later?

Victoria was a sentimental soul, and she knew it. Charlie knew it, too, which was probably why she’d left her alone to think. She hadn’t even noticed Charlie leaving.

She stayed with the quilt a while longer, photographing every inch of it with the Canon. She’d upload the images to her computer later when she had a chance.

Her eyes strayed over to the box of letters that Charlie had left on the second examination table. There were a few other smaller silk pieces she could look at, but looking at her watch, she knew that she would not have time before the letters arrived. Covering the quilt for now with the tissue paper, she walked over to the table and pulled out one of the stools that were stored beneath it. She had barely sat down when there was a knock at the door and an intern came in.

“The Arias-Danvers letters for you,” he said with a smile, and left her to it after Victoria thanked him.

The letters were stored in archival polyester wallets, in folders, in date order. There were about thirty of them all told. They’d been discovered among the records of the LQC, and were mostly letters written to Lena Luthor and Kara Danvers (or Luthor) by Samantha “Sam” Arias, Ruby Arias, and Alexandra Danvers, who was Kara’s older sister. They documented various visits that the women had made to each other over the years, talked about daily life in Boston, midwifery, and shared news of Ruby growing up and of her finding a husband, of Sam becoming a grandmother. They were wonderful letters, warm and affectionate, and showed that Sam clearly thought of Lena as a sister. Sam was also quite cheeky, teasing both Lena and Kara. She must have been quite the character.

And there was something else, too. Reading between the lines, historians thought that it was possible that Sam and Alex had been a couple. It was an extraordinary thought, given that during the period, same-sex relationships were extremely rare. There had been several executions over the period, and if it were true, Sam and Alex would have had to be very, very careful about their conduct.

There were some historians, too, who thought that it was possible that Lena and Kara had also been a couple.

Victoria tended to err on the side of caution. Without direct evidence, she could not say whether she thought it was true or not. She believed it about Sam and Alex, and marveled at their bravery, but Lena and Kara? She had no idea.

She looked again at the box of letters. They had been long kept private by the Ruston family, as had the rest of their colonial-era artifacts. Victoria knew that the quilts had been photographed, but that the letters had not been read. She was to be the first person in who knew how long to read them, and as yet, nobody knew who had written them or what they contained. Her guess was that they were written by Lena, possibly by Kara too, since they had been kept together with other Luthor artifacts.

There was only one way to find out.

With the utmost care, Victoria prised open the lid of the box. It was stiff with age, but eventually gave with a pop. Inside, there was a handful of letters, tied with a ribbon, and some other folded documents. Victoria lifted the letters out first. With excitement, she recognized Lena’s elegant penmanship; she had signed many of the LQC documents, and her handwriting was impeccable.

The paper was soft with age, and Victoria handled it delicately. With slow, deliberate movements, her breath caught in her throat, she unfolded the letter and lay it on the table. The parchment was a little stained with age, but Lena’s handwriting was clear and precise.

To her surprise, the letter was not to Sam and Alex at all, but from Lena to Kara.

 

Dearest one,

I’m sure you know by now that I have hidden this letter in the pocket I sewed into your cape, otherwise you would not be reading it. I hope that you are having a wonderful time with Sam, Alex, and Ruby. I have written them a letter, too, but in case it is lost, please pass them all the love that I have that does not belong to you alone. You know that I wish I could have come with you this time, but I know that Eliza and Jeremiah would not be pleased to see me again. I have plenty of work to keep me busy while I wait for your return, though I will miss you every day you are gone from my side.

Kara. I cannot wait to feel you close again. You will be gone a while, and we have not been separated since those awful few days in Salem. Always I remember how you saved me then. You save me still, every day. Your smile is as sunshine in my heart. In this year we have spent in New York, with our time together, I have known happiness beyond my dreams. I look at the forest quilt with the addition you made, and I dream about new roads we may walk down together. Darling, I am holding your hand in mine – can you feel me? Even when we are apart, we are together, for I have given my heart to you, and you have given yours to me. Your love is a gift that keeps me warm at night. To sleep and wake next to you every day is joyful, and the memory of your love will keep me warm until you are returned to me. I shall think of the soft way you hold me, the gentle way you love me, and keep the memory of your laughter next to my heart until you are home.

When you are home, I shall kiss you, and kiss you, and give you all the love that I am saving for you.

Your Lena

 

When Victoria put the letter down, she found that she had tears in her eyes. They had been a couple. They had been. Here was a piece of Lena, a hugely important piece of her, laid out in ink before her, in Lena’s own words. Victoria could almost feel the love she had for Kara echoing through the centuries.

And Kara Danvers. She wasn’t just someone who helped Lena make her quilts. According to Lena, Kara had apparently had some hand in her escape from Salem. Could it be that back in Salem, Lena and Kara had already been in some kind of relationship? Victoria’s head spun at the thought. How had they kept that a secret? Keeping a same-sex relationship under wraps in the stifling Puritan society would’ve been difficult at the best of times, but keeping it a secret during the Salem Witch Trials, when people were on the watch for anything unusual? Extraordinary.

Victoria suddenly had so many more questions. How had they found each other? Did their relationship have something to do with Lena’s accusation of witchcraft? Had this … had this Eve person discovered them? The records showed none of it.

She stared down again at the letter.

She felt almost rude, intruding on such a private letter. There was something about knowing, though, Victoria thought. She knew now that they had had a love that was forbidden and rare, that was dangerous in society at the time, and that they had bravely loved each other anyway. It would be a feeling familiar to so many, even in today’s world; she knew instantly that this letter, as private as it was, would be going on display too. It was too important not to. Kara was clearly someone of great importance in Lena’s life, and it would be only right for her place in Lena’s life to be acknowledged.

They were each other’s partner. They had loved each other, and now the world would know it.

At least now we know why Kara Danvers is sometimes known as Kara Luthor.

She looked down at the letter once more, careful to not let her tears stain the letter or Lena’s delicate words, written so boldly on the parchment.

She felt a flicker of amusement that Lena had been far less careful about writing her feelings down than Sam and Alex had been. Victoria let out a laugh and wiped the tears from under her eyes. Lena was far more daring than she had ever supposed, if she felt unafraid to set her feelings like this down so permanently.

Victoria set the letter aside, writing a number 1 on a notelet and laying it on the table beside the unfolded letter on the table, before taking up the next letter from the pile.

This letter seemed to be a reply from Kara to Lena, after her trip to Boston. Victoria let out a little squeal of excitement, not caring whether anyone passing the storeroom could hear. This was the first piece of writing that, as far as anyone knew, could be directly attributed to Kara. It was not dated. Kara’s penmanship was far less neat than Lena’s, even a little childlike, but it was still clear.

 

My Lena,

I already miss holding your hand in front of the fire, as is our way. I remember the second time I went away to Boston without you, and you left your letter in my cape pocket for me to find. I am leaving this in your needle box, and I am sure you will have found it not long after I have left. As you always request, I will pass your love to our family, but I admit that I will keep some back for myself. Quite a large part, for I am more selfish with your love than I would admit to anyone but you.

Being away from you is hard. It is always hard, but I understand your reason for it. You are the most noble woman I know. One day I am sure that Eliza and Jeremiah will understand that I did what I did to save you, an innocent woman, from a fate undeserved. And what I gained was so much more than I lost, because I have you, my love, my heart.

While I am away, I will think of you. Of your lips on mine, and your hands on my skin. I will think of the way you make me feel, so happy and so important, so loved. I will think of the way that your beautiful eyes always shine when you smile at me. I think often about when I came home from Boston the first time and you leapt into my arms and held me tight, about how we were not parted for hours. I blush to think of it, but I hope for a repeat of it when I come home this time. I never want to be parted from you again, my Lena. My love. I cannot wait to touch your face once more, to feel the gentle curve of your waist, your hands in my hair, and your kisses on my mouth.

I have brought the ocean square you embroidered for me. I carry it everywhere I go, because it’s a little piece of you. I have been working on something new for you, and I have brought it with me. I hope to finish it while I am in Boston, and give it to you when I return to our home, and to you. I have chosen the green thread specially to match your eyes.

I miss you already. I love you! When I hold you in my arms again, all the world will fade so that it is just us. Just you and me, my Lena.

Yours forever, Kara

 

Victoria’s hands were shaking in her lap by the time she’d finished Kara’s letter to Lena. They had loved each other so much. And here was Kara, her voice ringing out from the silence of history, to tell her story. That she had lived, that she had loved Lena, that she had somehow saved her from a dire fate during the Trials. That she had gone with Lena, lived with her in New York, and continued to love her.

Her eyes were blurred with tears as she lay the second letter next to the first.

After she had composed herself, Victoria read through the rest of the letters. Many of them were in the same vein as the first two, and no more of them spoke of distance between them. Victoria drew the conclusion that maybe they had never travelled without the other again. In a time where journeys could take weeks, where danger and sickness lurked around every corner, perhaps they had simply wanted to spend as much time together as they could.

What was also evident from the letters was that Lena and Kara liked to leave each other notes. All of the letters that were written strictly between Lena and Kara were colorful and descriptive in the ways they described each other. It was clear that neither of them were shy with each other. If they had not spent a significant time apart, then maybe these notes had been left in secret places for the other to find, rather than sent between cities. The language used within them made it clear to Victoria that they were meant to be read with each other.

With an amused smile, Victoria knew that with the exception of the first two letters, none of the other private ones would go on public display. They were, all too clearly, notes between lovers.

The rest of the letters dealt with the business, the quilts they were making, or were letters between themselves and the rest of whom they called their family in Boston. They described meetings between the five of them, six once Ruby had married a man named Daniel. Victoria wondered whether this Daniel had known about Sam and Alex, and Lena and Kara.

She supposed that Ruby must have known. There had to have been a reason that these private letters between Lena and Kara, who had lived in New York, had ended up in Boston when the rest of their belongings had not. More research was needed, but Victoria drew the conclusion that Sam, Alex, or Ruby had retrieved the private letters before someone else had seen them.

She was also glad to see mentions of meetings where Lena was with Kara when she met Eliza and Jeremiah Danvers. The hatchet must have been buried, somewhere along the way. She wondered what that first meeting had been like, and whether Eliza and Jeremiah had had any inkling that Lena and Kara were more than friends.

Victoria looked at her watch. She had another half an hour with the Arias-Danvers letters before they’d be returned to the archives. Now that the Ruston letters were set up in a grid system on the table, she had access to more information on this group of women than she’d ever had before. She pulled off her gloves and fetched a notebook and pen from one of the shelves.

By cross-referencing the original documents with the new Luthor letters, along with her knowledge of the group and some swift searches on Google, Victoria quickly sketched up a timeline of events.

These were the facts, as far as she could tell.

Lena Luthor had been born in October 1667 in Boston. She married Jack Spheer in 1685 aged 18 and moved to Salem in 1687. Jack Spheer was lost at sea in 1689, and Lena reverted to her maiden name after his death. Victoria wasn’t sure why that was, as it seemed a very modern thing to do, but she didn’t question it.

There were no records of a Kara Danvers being born, but she appeared in the Salem records from 1679. Victoria guessed that Lena and Kara were of a similar age, but without a birth record for Kara, there was no way to know. Alex had been born in 1665, and Sam in 1662, but records of them both were scanty at best. Most of the information known about them came directly from the Arias-Danvers letters and records of business in Boston.

According to both sets of letters, and going by the dates on them and working backwards, Lena and Kara had started up a relationship in late 1691, a few months before the Salem Witch Trials really got going. The Salem records stated that Lena had been accused of witchcraft in late April 1692, and that she had escaped, apparently with the help of Kara, Sam, and Alex, if she went by the information in the letters. They had all, Ruby included, surfaced in Boston in late April or early May 1692.

By mid-summer 1693, Lena and Kara were living in New York. There were references in the letters to Lillian and Lex Luthor making nuisances of themselves in Boston, and apparently Lena and Kara had enough of them and moved.

In 1695, Lena formed the Luthor Quilting Company. Lena, together with Kara, had produced some of the finest quilts to come out of the colonial age. According to both sets of letters, Lena and Kara had lived comfortably, and happily, as a result, until the end of their days. Lena had passed away in November 1744 at the age of 77, leaving half of everything she owned to Kara, and the rest to Sam, Alex, and Ruby. Kara passed away in May 1745. The records of their deaths recorded no disease or infirmity, leading Victoria to suppose that they’d both died of natural causes, or old age. Knowing what she did now, how deeply they’d loved each other, those dates made Victoria sadder; the idea that Kara had lived for several months past Lena caused tears to spring to her eyes.

Sitting alone in the storage room, Victoria laughed wetly to herself as she wiped her eyes. She’d done a lot of crying this afternoon, over people who had passed away long before. But the thing was … to her, they weren’t just people in history.

To her, they lived still. She was sitting before their letters to each other, filled with love and light and joy. Just to her right was one of the most beautiful quilts that Victoria had ever seen, which the letters confirmed was made by Lena in Salem and completed in Boston, left as a gift for Ruby. Ruby’s family had kept the quilt safe for all these centuries.

With another sniffle, Victoria carefully photographed each of the letters. There were 27 of them, all told; all wonderful pieces of evidence about the lives of these extraordinary women. As she was finishing up the photos, Charlie came back into the room.

“How are you feeling?” she asked when she reached the table, looking at Victoria with a kind expression on her face.

Victoria bit her lip to keep from bursting into tears once more, and nodded at the letters. “I’ve learnt so much, Charlie. So much. I’m about to put them away, but you should read them before I do.”

She put the camera away, then fetched a new pair of gloves. She put them on while she sat at the table, watching Charlie read them. It didn’t take her long, but Charlie too was teary-eyed when she finished.

“Wow,” she breathed, a hitch in her voice. “Wow. They really loved each other.”

“Yeah. Yeah, they did. It’s wonderful, isn’t it?”

“It really is …” Charlie replied. “This must mean so much to you, to learn about them both like this. Especially Lena. You’ve spent so long working with her quilts and trying to fit the pieces of her life together. She must feel so—” she waved her hand in the air while she searched for the word, “—so whole to you now.”

“She really does. I can’t believe it. I wish I could know her, you know?”

Charlie smiled gently. “Victoria. I think that you know more about Lena than anyone else living right now. You’ve kind of made her part of your life.”

“That’s true,” Victoria murmured. She carefully folded each of the letters once more, leaving them in a pile with the fragile ribbon lying next to it.

“Wait, what are those?” Charlie asked suddenly, pointing into the box.

“Hmm?”

“Those.”

“What?” Victoria leaned over the box. She’d forgotten that the letters weren’t the only documents in the box. There were a few pieces of parchment folded up at the bottom. “I feel wiped out just from the letters,” she laughed. “I don’t know how much more I can take.”

“Let’s look anyway,” Charlie said, fetching a pair of gloves of her own and pulling them on.

There were three thick pieces of parchment. They seemed to be quite large, and Victoria was shocked that she’d forgotten all about them. They lay them on the table where the letters had been, and Victoria pulled the first toward herself.

She unfolded it slowly. It seemed to be heavy with ink, and it crackled as she opened it. It seemed to be some kind of crude art piece, done with a quill and ink rather than with paint or anything else they’d used in the period. Victoria was confused for a moment, but the more that was unfolded, the more she realized what she was—

“Oh, my god,” she said, stepping back from the picture once it had been revealed, her hands frozen by her sides.

“No way,” Charlie whispered.

It was a large piece of parchment, and it contained an ink portrait of a woman, a very beautiful young woman, actualized in as much detail as the artist could muster with the equipment they had. She seemed to be sitting by a window, but her eyes were on the artist. She had flowing hair hanging over one shoulder, which was dark if the amount of ink the artist had used was any indication, a strong jawline, and a smile that seemed caught halfway between cheeky and loving. The artist had even captured her dimples.

The caption for the portrait was simply Lena, and by Kara, 1699 was inked in the bottom right corner.

“That’s Lena,” Charlie said, her voice full of awe now. “She’s beautiful.”

“And her eyes were green,” Victoria managed, her chest tight with emotion. She couldn’t take her eyes off the portrait. She couldn’t believe she was looking at the face of Lena Luthor. It was the only known portrait of her and, taken together with the letters, Victoria had no cause to doubt the authenticity of it. It was a large piece of parchment, about twice the size of a piece of legal paper. On it, Kara had drawn the face of her lover, her partner, in ink forever. Charlie was right, Lena had been beautiful. “She was like art herself,” Victoria whispered. “I can’t believe I’m seeing her after so many years …”

They both gazed at the portrait for a few long moments, Lena’s amused gaze holding them with a smile across three centuries of time. Victoria wondered whether the picture had been drawn from life or from memory; either way, the fact remained that in it, Lena looked like she was about to laugh. This portrait, along with what she’d learnt about Lena today, gave Victoria the impression that Kara was used to the sight of Lena on the edge of laughter. The thought made her bright smile widen.

There were two more pictures. The second was of a pair of hands in the process of embroidering something, entitled Lena Working, also by Kara Danvers; she wondered how long Lena had had to pose like that in order for Kara to set this picture down. The third was a forest scene similar to her favorite quilt, the one hanging up in the exhibition.

Knowing what she knew now, she desperately wanted another look at it.

“Charlie, will you help me put these away?” she asked, taking photos of the pictures, sending them to her phone, and putting the lens back on the camera.

“Sure,” Charlie replied, and the two of them packed up the documents as carefully as they could. Victoria would be in early tomorrow morning with the documents team to begin processing the letters and the pictures for preservation and archiving, but for now she felt the overwhelming urge to go and look at the quilts.

Once she was back out in the gallery, she made her way through the milling visitors and stood in front of the forest quilt, as she had done so many times before. There was something about the quilt that made her feel free. She couldn’t put her finger on it. It was very well-preserved, like all of the LQC quilts were, but this time her eye was drawn straight away to the image of the two women.

She knew, she just knew, that the two women were Lena and Kara. The forest quilt had been mentioned in one of the letters as one that Kara had produced, and that Lena had bought. She guessed that it was near the beginning of their relationship, since the two figures had been added after the original had been made. Some of the letters also mentioned a small blue silk square with an ocean on it that Lena had made Kara, which Kara had loved very much, but that had never been found.

Victoria’s heart beat hard in her chest as she stared at the quilt, at the stitching, at the two women at the end of the path. Her eyes welled with tears yet again, but this time, at least, she had come prepared. She pulled a tissue out of a packet in her pocket and dabbed beneath her eyes.

“I’ve never seen anything like this,” a woman said, coming to stand next to her.

“It’s— it’s wonderful, isn’t it?” Victoria replied, glancing over at the woman. “It’s my favorite. It was created by a woman called Kara Danvers, around 1691.”

“I wonder who the two women are?” the visitor mused, before eventually moving on.

Victoria couldn’t wait to process all of the information she’d learnt today, to add Lena and Kara’s names to the information cards. To hang up copies of the three pictures she and Charlie had seen. To add details from the letters. It would make the collection so much more poignant.

To the right of the quilt hung a smaller piece of silk. It had once been black, but like all things it had faded over time. The other colors were likewise a little muted, but it had lost none of its potency for that. It was an image that consisted of two joined hands in front of a fire. Nobody had been able to make much sense of it before. Some historians posited that it was part of a larger piece. It had definitely been made by Lena, because it matched her style, but after reading the letters, Victoria knew that the hands in the image were Lena and Kara’s.

Victoria stood there, lost in thought, thinking of all the things she’d learnt today, but especially about Lena and Kara. A while ago, when she’d first started working on this exhibition, she’d visited their grave in the small garden of a private home in Boston. It had, records showed, once belonged to Lena Luthor, and it was the house that she and Jack had lived in after their marriage. Lena and Kara had chosen to be buried there, along with Sam and Alex, Ruby when she passed, and several generations of the Arias family over time. The gardens of the home had been quite expansive, and due to the age of the graves, they were cared for by one of the city’s historical associations.

Victoria looked at the piece of silk with the hands and fire embroidery on it, and made a decision.

Two months later, Victoria shivered in her coat as she walked up one of Boston’s oldest streets. It hadn’t snowed for days, but the wind made her feel like ice was wrapping itself around her body bit by bit. She hitched her heavy backpack up around her shoulders, then snuggled deeper into her coat.

A few moments later, she was outside the old Luthor house. Aside from restoration here and there, it retained its old features, and Victoria smiled when she thought of Lena and Kara walking here, treading the very same street, and coming home to the very house she was standing outside. It was here that Sam and Alex had lived with Ruby, where Ruby and her descendants had continued to live until the early 1920s.

She’d made an appointment with the current owners, who she’d met a few years ago upon her first visit, and after ringing the doorbell, she was welcomed in. The owners, gracious elderly gentlemen named Benjamin and Thomas Davies, made her a cup of tea before they allowed her out into the garden. They were aware of what she was there to do, and why, and left her in peace.

Victoria walked through the quiet garden, sheltered by the high stone walls, and made her way towards the gated off section. She felt choked with emotion as she read the names on the small gravestones as she passed. Several generations of Ruby and Daniel’s descendants, names she was less familiar with. But as she got closer to the wall, more familiar names appeared, and Victoria took her hat off.

As she passed them, she ran her hand gently over the tops of each and whispered their names.

Daniel Stanton.

Ruby Stanton.

Samantha Arias.

Alex Danvers.

And then, in the corner, closest to the wall and what looked like wild fox grape plants, were two graves side by side, the gravestones touching.

Kara Luthor.

Lena Luthor.

Her eyes blurry with tears, Victoria swung the backpack off her shoulders and set it on the ground next to her feet. She knelt down to the side of the graves, and leaned forward to brush stray leaves from the tops of them. Then, leaning over a little further, she traced their names with her fingertips, before sitting back on her heels.

Their graves were simple, done in the same style as one would find graves in any of the colonial burying grounds of Boston or other older cities. The gravestones were small, thin, and slightly cracked with age. But unlike most other graves, they didn’t contain birth or death dates. The only adornment, aside from their names, was the word Beloved carved on each.

The last time that Victoria had come here, she’d known only the barest details about Lena, and next to nothing about Kara. The mystery of Kara’s last name was still unsolved. But now, after years of study and with the revelation of the letters, Victoria knew so much more.

She believed she knew, now, where the little blue ocean silk square that Kara had loved so much was. Lena had made it for her, and she’d carried it with her throughout her life. Victoria believed that it was with her still.

She had spent so long in Lena and Kara’s company, so many countless hours trying to learn about them, so much time with their remarkable quilts. And she had read their private letters to each other. She felt like there were ghostly connections, reaching forward through time from Lena and Kara, all the way through to her. And Victoria would be the one to tell their story.

They’d had a life together. They had met and fallen in love in one of the most turbulent times in United States history. Lena had been accused of bewitching Kara and arrested. And somehow Kara, along with her sister and Sam, had rescued Lena from what would almost certainly have been her death. And they had lived.

They had lived. Lena and Kara had laughed together, created beautiful things with their hands that still survived today. They had loved each other, held hands before fires, kissed each other, held each other at night. They’d fallen in love, loved each other all their lives, and now they lay together for eternity.

But their lives had not ended with their deaths.

Through the Luthor Quilting Company, their works had come through the ages.

Now, through the words of the letters Victoria had so recently read, so too would their love.

And Victoria wanted to honor them and their love.  

For years, historians had debated the relationship between the two women, but now, in their own words, Lena and Kara had told the world who they were.

Victoria opened the backpack and unwrapped a heavy memorial stone. It was a small, squat obelisk, specially made. She lifted it carefully out of the bag and rested it on the grass beside her. Leaning forward, she cleared an equidistant space in between the two graves, right in front of them. The stone obelisk had been measured, and would not obscure or harm the graves, nor the gravestones; it was not large enough. She hoped, instead, it would acknowledge who they were to each other in death, in a way that they wouldn’t have been able to.

Picking up the heavy stone, Victoria braced herself on her knees and slowly placed the stone in its new home.

On the side facing Lena’s grave were carved the words: When I hold you in my arms again, all the world will fade so that it is just us.

Facing Kara’s grave were carved the words: I shall kiss you, and kiss you, and give you all the love that I am saving for you.

On the front was carved a fire, with two hands clasped before it.

In death, as in life, they would forever hold each other’s hands.
