OR, Lena sponsors a youth center, and Kara works there. OR, Esme is an unwitting and unrequested wingwoman, Kara and Lena fall in love in less than five minutes, and they’re both incredibly useless but Lena hides it better.

Chapter 1

Chapter Notes

She’s perfect. Mesmerizing, really. And Kara’s about sixty percent in love with her.

Which is inconvenient, because Kara is also too much of a disaster to do something crazy like introduce herself.

Meaning she’s almost exclusively spent the last hour staring at someone she’s never met, wondering if she’s a new volunteer or if maybe Kara just doesn’t venture to the arts-and-crafts room often enough to have met her. 

In her defense, she’s pretty busy in her specialty: the gym.

Not to brag, but she’s very good at getting hit in the face eight times a day by twelve-year-olds with no aim and some type of projectile—whether it be the baseball or the bat—and not suffering any serious head injuries.

(The invulnerability helps.)

Currently, however, she’s exiled from the gym. Mostly because she’s really stupid, and managed to very publicly snap her arm in half last week during a solar flare. It’s all peachy now that her powers are back and her left radius is no longer sticking out of her forearm, but human bones do not heal that fast. 

So for pretenses, Kara Danvers now has a bright pink cast up to her elbow.

And sure, the kids (and Nia) love signing and scribbling all over it, and that’s been fun, but overall, it’s itchy and clunky and she’s struggling to do things one-handed. It’s the worst.

Except the part where it takes her to the arts-and-crafts room, and she meets Lena. 

And then falls in love with her in less than ten minutes. (Fifteen, tops.)

That part is pretty great.

It would be better if they didn’t meet because Kara heard her laugh for the first time and got so flustered she smacked her forehead against the door of the cupboard she was rifling through, searching for googly eyes, but alas. You can’t have everything.

“Are you alright?”

Rao, her voice is pretty.

Kara turns around so fast that she rams her face, again, into the door. If she were human, she’d probably be in a lot of pain. (If she were better at pretending to be human, she’d probably be acting like she was in a lot of pain.)

Instead, she just sort of stares, because golly, she is so much prettier up close.

Like a fish out of water, Kara opens and closes her mouth without managing any real words, and the concern on the woman’s face grows.

“Do you have a concussion?” She asks, and for a moment, Kara forgets how low the odds are that she does. But the woman takes her silence as an: I’m not sure, and gestures for her to sit down—at the low-set table with kid-sized chairs behind her, where she’d been coloring with . . . Kara’s niece.

Oh no.

Kara loves Esme. Adores her, really. 

And, objectively, the habit she’s developed of rambling about her new family to anyone who’ll listen is pretty cute. However, she doesn’t discriminate between fun stories and embarrassing ones, which means that this actual goddess has probably already heard far too many ridiculous stories about Kara to ever find her even remotely attractive.

“Hi, Aunt Kara!” Esme chirps, and Kara waves weakly back at her, voice a little too high as she responds: “Hi, Esme.”

Beside her, the woman’s eyebrows shoot up, and she looks at Kara with a dangerous sparkle in her eye as she shifts her chair closer to the table, and— oh no. What does she know?

“So you’re the famous Aunt Kara,” she says, glancing between Kara and Esme like she’s putting puzzle pieces together in her mind.

“Yes?” Kara says, but it sounds a bit like she’s asking. Like she isn’t sure that’s her name. 

“Are you sure?” The woman teases, and wow Kara is like, eighty percent in love with her. “You don’t sound sure.”

Right now, Kara isn’t sure of much of anything, except that no full-grown adult should look as adorable squished into such a small chair as this woman does. 

(Forget kryptonite.

Clearly, all it takes to knock Kara out is a pretty girl.)

“That’s probably the concussion.” 

Internally, Kara fist pumps at the fact that a sentence came out of her mouth and it wasn’t stupid or strangled. (Well, it was a little stupid, but at least it was coherent.)

She laughs. It’s light and brief, but it’s there, and Kara is under no illusion that her joke was anything but terrible.

And yet, she laughs. And it’s like magic.

Last time Kara heard her laugh, she ran face-first into a cupboard door. (‘Last time,’ as though it weren’t less than thirty seconds ago.) This time, she gets to see it: bright and warm and oh Rao she has dimples.

“I don’t know what she told you, but it’s probably not true,” Kara says, pointing a thumb at her niece, who, without missing a beat or looking up from her task of choosing between two very similarly colored purple crayons, fires back: “Yes, it is.”

“All good things, don’t worry,” the woman says, still smiling. It’s a beautiful, life-altering smile, but it’s odd, like her face isn’t used to making it. (Kara briefly wonders what the best way to keep it there forever would be.)

“Well, now that you know my name and my life story and my traitorous niece, can I know one for you? You can pick which one.”

Surprise (mixed with what Kara really hopes is ‘impressed’) flickers across her face, and Kara is one-hundred percent sure it’s reflected on her own. She was not expecting herself to say that.

And she wonders: Was that bad? Should I not have said that? as the woman hesitates and seems to brace herself, though Kara has no idea what for.

“Lena,” she says. 

Kara thinks it’s quite possible that a name has never suited a person more. 

In a rush, the woman adds, like if she slows down she’ll convince herself not to say it: “And my life story is a bit of a mess, but my niece is named Ruby and she’s the snarkiest person I’ve ever met, and she’s only eleven. I’m terrified for her teenage years and I pity her mother.”

The grin that spreads across Kara’s face is unstoppable. She beams at Lena, who seems to be struggling to hold back her own smile. 

(Something soft and warm is blooming in Kara’s chest like an opening flower, or sunlight through the window on a sleepy morning.)

“Is Ruby here? Can I meet her?” Esme’s crayon falls to the table (‘Orchid Explosion,’ specifically, which just looks to Kara like pink with glitter) as she sits up, straight as a pin like she’s expecting Ruby to pop out of the woodwork.

Lena’s soft laugh is like what Kara imagines fairies sound like.

“No, sweetie, she lives in Metropolis. I haven’t been able to visit since I moved.”

“You lived in Metropolis?”

“I did. I moved here a few months ago for work.”

Esme nods sagely. 

“I have family in Metropolis, but I don’t see them much. We should visit together,” she says, decisive, like that’s a completely normal thing to suggest to a stranger. “Then I can meet Ruby and we can finish all the pages in my coloring book.”

“Oh, look!” Kara says, more than a little relief coloring her voice as she notices her sister shaking off the cold as she slips through the door. She waves her over with an arm that nearly takes out a passing pre-teen. “Your mom is here, Esme.”

“Which one?” Esme asks, not looking up from scribbling in the trunk of a bright yellow elephant. (Scientifically unlikely, but Kara isn’t about to stomp on any dreams.)

In the corner of her eye, Lena’s dropped her chin into her palm and is neatly coloring the wing of her owl a deep blue. (Also scientifically sketchy—for Earth, anyway.) 

A little thrown off, Kara asks: “Does it matter?” 

“Yes.” Esme’s voice is so matter-of-fact that Kara almost just believes her, no questions asked. “Mommy said we could stop for ice cream on the way home if she picked me up, and mom didn’t.”

Fair enough.

Alex stops beside them, her hands buried in the pockets of her jacket and a warm smile on her face as she looks down at her daughter.

“Hey, kid, how’s the elephant?”

“Mommy!”

With only a small oof, Alex catches the blur that’s flung itself headfirst into her, and mumbles something indecipherable against her stomach.

“What was that? Couldn’t hear you, Essie.”

“Are we getting ice cream?” Esme’s head falls back and she beams sweetly up at her mother, batting her eyelashes just a little.

Internally, Kara cackles: her sister’s had a decade-and-a-half to develop immunity toward her pout, but hasn’t quite gotten there yet with Esme’s. (And Kara’s not afraid to say she’s been teaching Esme the ropes, but she’s definitely afraid to say it within hearing distance of Alex.)

“I guess so,” Alex says, her sigh loud and dramatic as Esme giggles into her jacket.

“Can Aunt Kara and Lena come?”

Despite the absolute panic that shoots up Kara’s spine like a bolt of lightning, she thinks there isn’t much she’d like more than to get ice cream with two of her favorite people in the world and the woman she’s pretty sure is definitely the love of her life. However. . . .

“I still have to clean up before I leave, otherwise I would one-hundred percent be up for ice cream,” she says, and she doesn’t even have to pretend that saying no is the end of the world, because it feels a bit like it might be. 

Still, the drama has the intended effect, and Esme giggles instead of frowns.

“Next time?” She asks, glancing between Kara and Lena with childlike hope.

“Next time,” Lena agrees, shooting her a wink, and Kara sends a quick thank you up to Rao that Lena hadn’t aimed a wink at her, because she definitely would’ve passed out.

“Alright, well, we’ll leave you to it.” With a hand on Esme’s back as she herds her toward the door, through the crowd of other parents, Alex waves over her shoulder. “It was nice to meet you, Lena. See you later, Kar.”

“You too,” Lena says, even as Kara realizes that they were not actually introduced at any point in the conversation. (Oops.)

Absently, Kara moves to pick up the crayons and reaches for the box, but forgets about her cast and bangs it on the side of the table. The sound and the vibration that shoots up her arm makes her jump, and so do the soft fingers that uncurl hers, taking the crayons and pulling the box away. Lena begins organizing them neatly into their rows, taking care to put the colors back in order. (This little quirk is cuter than Kara thinks it should be, but she’s never met anyone who cares about doing anything more than randomly shoving the crayons into the empty spaces. 

It’s sweet, and Kara feels it in her chest.)

“Do you need help with cleanup?” She asks after a moment, not looking up from her task, and Kara realizes she’s been staring. (Still, she doesn’t look away, though she tries.)

(Not very hard.)

“Huh?” 

“Cleanup,” Lena repeats, glancing up. Her gaze is amused as she meets Kara’s, who wonders how close she’d have to get to count all the different shades of green in her eyes. “I don’t usually stay this late, but it looks like you’ve been abandoned, and I don’t think arts-and-crafts is your usual scene. I’m not sure you know where everything goes.”

Kara’s brain chooses to skip over the part where Lena noticed that she’s new to arts-and-crafts because if she stops to think about it, she might stop breathing.

And she doesn’t mention that it would really only take her a few seconds to clean up on her own. (Superspeed has its perks.)

Instead, she blinks, brow furrowed in confusion. (Though she doesn’t know it, this is the first time Lena wants to reach out to press her thumb against the crinkle and smooth it out.

It’s the only time she holds back.)

“Abandoned?” Kara says, glancing around the room, which, except for a few remaining parents herding their children toward the door, is empty. “I thought Nia was here?”

“She left about an hour ago. I’m pretty sure she told you she was leaving.”

Oh, yeah. She did. She did do that.

“Of course. I knew that,” Kara says, unconvincingly. Lena fixes her with an amused look as she shuts the crayon box and reaches for the cupboard door that Kara’s pretty sure she carries an imprint of on her forehead.

“Of course you did.” Lena pauses, fingers still curled around the door as she drops back down to her heels, the box safely tucked away on one of the middle shelves. “Which means you also know not to lock up when you leave, because Winn is going to be here until six.”

Kara definitely did not know that.

“I definitely knew that,” she lies, face screwed up in a way that screams: I’m lying! “But thank you for telling me, you know, in case I’d forgotten.”

Lena laughs in response, and the sound fizzles in Kara’s chest like a mouthful of Pop Rocks as the arts-and-crafts room door swings shut behind the last straggler.

The fuzzy feeling morphs into something strong and peaceful as they fall into sync cleaning the room, organizing things back into drawers and picking scraps of construction paper off the tables. Kara swipes glitter off a countertop and it lands in Lena’s hair as she leans over to drop a handful of markers in a bin.

Conversation has never flowed so easy.

Silence has never felt so calm.

Kara decides she might believe in soulmates and love at first sight and all that jazz. (What other way is there to explain Lena and how her smile makes Kara feel like being welcomed home?)

They talk and talk and talk and with everything Kara learns, she wants to know more.

Lena’s favorite color is blue, but she has a favorite pen and the ink is black. She likes tea over coffee, but coffee keeps her awake and she works long hours. She reads scientific journals for fun and loves to play chess and was adopted (“So was I! How cool is that?” ) when she was four from a village in Ireland that she’s too afraid to visit, though she longs to go home. 

She loves kale (Kara tries to hold back her grimace and doesn’t do a very good job, if Lena’s laugh is any indication) and has a cat named Spider (“I didn’t name her. I had a work event at a shelter, and she claimed me. By the time I learned her name, it was too late. I believe ‘Spider’ is for her unusually long legs.” ) who is the sole subject of all thirty-one pictures on her phone.

And Kara is falling in love. 

She tells Lena that her favorite color is green. (And doesn’t mention that until a few hours ago, it was yellow.) She talks at length about her love for potstickers. She tells her that she’s adopted too and rambles about her family and her rocky-at-first-but-really-great-now relationship with her sister, and does an overall bad job hiding the fact that she’s an alien.

(Lena’s face contorts into something bittersweet when Kara talks about Alex, and when she asks, Lena tells her she lost her brother a few years ago, then asks how long Kara’s been working at the youth center.)

The closer they get to finishing up, the slower Kara moves, and she hopes she’s not imagining it when she thinks Lena’s doing the same.

But she can only drag it out so long, and eventually:

“The parking lot’s empty; did you walk?” Lena asks, peering out the window as she shrugs on her jacket, and Kara continues her streak of being really bad at pretending she’s human as she fumbles to get her arm through her coat sleeve and says: “Oh, no, I flew here. On . . . on a bus.”

Grimacing at her own stupidity, she glances up at the sky like maybe Rao will come save her from herself, but Lena just laughs. 

(Kara thinks she really needs to get better at being human.)

(The sad thing is, she’s not usually this terrible. Lena’s just really pretty.)

“You’re cute,” Lena says, eyes warm and bright as her laugh settles into a smile and she lifts her hair out from under her jacket collar. The entire world focuses down to just Lena Lena Lena, glowing under the early-evening autumn sun, looking all soft and suddenly shy after calling Kara cute .

Kara, who thinks if she were capable of cardiac arrest, she’d be hurtling straight for it. But Lena isn’t finished.

“Do you want to grab ice cream? I could tell you were disappointed you couldn’t get any with Esme, earlier.” 

She’s perfect. She is literally perfect. 

“I’m going to marry you.” 

(Smooth, Kara. 

That’s not creepy at all.)

“How about ice cream?” Lena says, instead of running away screaming. “I don’t get engaged before the first date. I’ll admit to being a bit of a U-Haul lesbian, but I have to draw the line somewhere.”

“I’d love to get ice cream with you,” Kara says, more than a little awe-struck. Lena beams, eyes sparkling like the specks of glitter still stuck in her hair, and yeah. 

Kara is definitely going to marry her.

“I’m going to marry you,” Kara had said. She’d agreed to go for ice cream with Lena, smiling like she was glowing, and immediately started rattling off a list of her favorite parlors that would still be open, and Lena promptly fell in love with her.

She’d held out her hand for Lena to take and led her down the sidewalk toward Main Street, where they crowded into a small booth by the street-side window of her “absolute favorite seasonal ice cream parlor,” Kara with a sundae the size of her head, and Lena with a small mint-chip cone. 

Here, too, Lena falls in love with her. 

She laughs when Kara wrinkles her nose and calls her ice cream “toothpaste with chocolate chips.” 

“It’s bad enough that you like kale.” The disdain is clear in her voice. Fighting a smile, Lena says: “Is that a deal-breaker?”

“Not anymore,” Kara says, and Lena falls in love.

“I’m going to marry you,” Kara had said. Five minutes before, she’d nearly started crying over a picture of Lena’s cat asleep on her chest.

Now, they’re walking downtown for ice cream and Kara’s swinging their hands back and forth, telling her about her childhood cat named Streaky and Lena thinks, yes. Yes, she is.

She’s going to marry her.

 

-

 

“I’m going to marry you,” Kara had said. There was something about the awe in her eyes and the matter-of-fact in her voice that, for a moment, Lena had wondered if she could see the future. (It was clear to her that while Kara was trying—keyword: trying—to appear human, she was likely not.) 

Instead of something stupid like: “Okay, looking forward to it,” Lena had smiled like she wasn’t screaming on the inside and said: “How about ice cream?” 

Three months later they’re back in that ice cream parlor on the corner of Chestnut and Main, pressed into the small booth by the street-side window, and Kara’s telling her about her day with an enthusiasm it doesn’t really deserve. 

“Someone broke the printer at work again,” she says, and Lena thinks she’s aiming for an angry face, but it turns out a little too guilty to be believable. 

“Someone?” Lena asks, raising a single eyebrow. Kara’s spoon pauses halfway to her mouth.

“Fine,” she sighs. “I broke the printer at work again.” 

The look on her face probably shouldn’t be adorable, but it is, and Lena smiles at her, the kind of smile she tries to straighten out, but the effort just makes it grow. And Kara’s expression changes. Her guilty ‘I broke the printer for the third time in as many months’ face morphs into something soft and warm. It’s an expression Lena is familiar with, one she’s seen many times before. (It’s been popping up a lot more frequently, lately.)

“I love you,” she says, and oh.

That’s what that face means. 

(She’s been wearing it for almost two months.)

Kara tells Lena she loves her for the first time at an ice cream parlor in the early evening, mint-chip slowly melting in the dish, and Lena thinks she might have already known. 

She considers saying: “I hope so, since you’ve already declared we’re getting married,” but exchanges the joke in favor of a bright, affectionate smile and a soft: “I love you, too, Kara.”

(And she almost adds: “I think I already loved you when we met,” but holds her tongue.)

 

-

 

“Lena,” Kara whispers, her breath warm against Lena’s ear. They’re tangled together under the same blanket, out on the balcony of their apartment as the moon rises after a late dinner. 

The plan had been to go out to eat—a proper Date Night, but Lena had gotten stuck at work and they’d missed their reservation. 

Pizza, potstickers, and wine had been a good consolation, though, and she’s feeling soft and sleepy, draped against Kara’s side with her face pressed to her collarbone. At the sound of Kara’s voice, she shifts a bit and hums, her body hovering on the edge of dozing off.

“Lena,” she whispers again, and this time, Lena opens her eyes, blinking a few times. She’s greeted by a gentle smile, one she fell in love with just over nine months ago and has fallen more in love with every day since. “Hi.”

“Hi,” Lena says, though she’s not sure why. 

“I love you.” There’s something strange in Kara’s voice, but her eyes are gentle and open, and Lena focuses on that, focuses on the love pouring out of her words and her arms around Lena’s waist, warm hands tucked just under the hem of her shirt.

“I love you,” she says back, tilting her chin up to press a feather-light kiss to Kara’s jaw. Under her touch, Kara softens further and leans into the palm Lena’s rested against her cheek, slow and content.

“Do you remember when we met?” Kara asks.

“Of course. You hit your head on the same cabinet twice and dumped glitter in my hair.”

“I told you I was going to marry you.” 

Oh. 

Oh, Lena thinks. She feels her heartbeat climbing in her chest and can tell Kara’s listening by the way she pulls her even closer. It feels silly to think—and hope—that Kara’s going to ask, but she does. Nine months wouldn’t be enough for most people, but Lena’s fairly certain she would’ve said yes if Kara had asked her to marry her the day they met, sitting by the window in her favorite ice cream parlor, talking and laughing and falling in love.

“Yes, you did,” she manages, her voice croaking out around the lump in her throat. Kara’s eyes catch hers and don’t let go, holding her still and waiting, heart pounding.

“I’d like to make good on that.”

(She’s not going to cry she’s not going to cry she’s not—

she’s going to cry.)

A small tear grows in the corner of Lena’s eye, breaks as she blinks and slips down her cheek, along the side of her nose and over her mouth. 

“Okay,” she breathes, and Kara’s smile softens, somehow, even further. 

“I, um, I got you a ring—and a bracelet; I was hoping we could do both?—but they’re under the bag in my family-size box of fruit loops. I had a whole plan, but I just. I didn’t want to wait any longer. I want to spend my life with you.”

“I want to spend my life with you, too.” (Lena’s not sure why she’s talking so quiet, so hushed, but thinks that maybe she just wants these words to be only for Kara. Even the night shouldn’t hear them.)

“Lena,” Kara breathes, her voice quiet and close and full of awe. Somehow, Lena thinks she sounds just like she did on that first day, outside the youth center with a smile like she was glowing. 

She strokes her thumb across Kara’s cheek and feels her lean into the touch, and of all the moments she’s lived, she thinks this one is her favorite. 

“Will you marry me?” 

Lena’s forehead drops against Kara’s and she watches her eyes flutter shut, her own going cross-eyed trying to stay focused on someone so close. She wants to see every moment of this memory. 

She wants to know what Kara’s smile looks like when she says yes, and she wants to watch it bloom. And she wants to remember.

With a gentle press of her fingers, she tilts Kara’s head toward her and kisses her soft and slow and it almost feels like that the first time, when she still had ice cream on her breath and the late-evening stars behind Kara echoed in her eyes. 

(Like something brand-new and forever is beginning, and yet like she’s coming home to someone she’s always known. Always loved.)

“Yes.”
