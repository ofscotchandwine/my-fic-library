Summary

Friday nights have belonged to Agatha and Rio for years. Dinner, bad documentaries, shared blankets, and a spare key neither of them remembers making permanent. Their friendship has quietly expanded into every corner of their lives, but neither has been willing to ask whether it might already be something more.

Then Rio mentions that someone asked her on a date.

Agatha reacts with all the grace and emotional maturity one might expect, which is to say, not at all.

A week of jealousy, one badly handled argument, and several years of mutual pining later, they are finally forced to admit that the friendship they were both so afraid of losing may have been love all along.

Notes

This one became a little more emotional than the word fluff might suggest, but the conflict stays small, the ending is soft, and no one remains miserable for very long. It is mostly about two people who have spent years building a life together while refusing to acknowledge what everyone else could already see.

Featuring jealousy, emotional repression, a disastrous attempt at communication, a date, and Agatha and Rio proving that intelligence in one area does not necessarily transfer to another. I hope you enjoy these two idiots finally catching up with themselves.

    By the time Rio let herself into Agatha's apartment, the kitchen smelled faintly of garlic, tomatoes, and something that had spent just a little too long against the bottom of a saucepan. She closed the door behind her with her hip, juggling a paper bag from the bakery and the canvas tote she carried almost everywhere, then paused in the entryway to take a more suspicious breath. She could admit that the idea of Rio sitting opposite another woman had lodged beneath her ribs in a way she could neither explain nor dislodge.     "You don't have to leave."

    "I have a date tomorrow. Apparently I'm supposed to look rested and charming."

    The reminder landed harder for being delivered as a joke. Agatha turned toward the sink and began stacking containers that did not need stacking. "That may be beyond the power of sleep."

    "Cruel."

    "Honest."

    Rio smiled, but something in the exchange had changed. She remained for another half hour, long enough to help wash the dishes and argue about whether Agatha's plants required water, yet the ease of the evening never fully returned. When she finally collected her coat, Agatha felt both relieved and disappointed.

    Agatha's gaze moved over her before she could stop it. The dark fabric fit Rio well, close enough through the shoulders to emphasize their breadth without appearing formal, and the open collar revealed the fine chain she wore almost every day. Agatha had seen the shirt before, though never while considering how another woman might see it. That perspective made the answer more difficult than it should have been.

    "It's fine," she said.

    Rio's mouth twisted. "A glowing endorsement."

    "You asked whether it worked, not whether it would alter the course of anyone's life."

    "I could wear something else."

    Agatha should have encouraged her to do so. Instead, she said, "No. Wear that."

    The words came out softer than intended. Rio looked at her for a second longer, then smiled in a way Agatha could not interpret.

    "Goodnight, Agatha."

    "Goodnight."

    This time, they did not hug. Rio descended the stairs, and Agatha closed the door before she reached the first landing.

    Friday arrived with insulting inevitability.

    Agatha woke before her alarm and spent the morning in a state of brittle efficiency that frightened two junior employees into completing an overdue report before lunch. She answered every message immediately, cleared her inbox, reorganized a project timeline, and volunteered to review a document she had successfully avoided for three weeks. None of it prevented her from noticing that Rio had not mentioned the date again.

Around noon, Rio asked whether Agatha wanted the remaining bread saved for Saturday, and Agatha told her she had already eaten it, which was untrue. At four twenty, Rio wrote: Rio: Leaving on time for once. A miracle.

    Agatha stared at the message until the screen dimmed.

    Agatha: Try not to frighten your colleagues with this sudden display of balance.

    Rio reacted with a laughing face. There was no mention of where she was going, what time the reservation began, or whether she had chosen the dark shirt. Agatha told herself she did not want those details. Knowing them would only create a timeline against which she could measure the evening, and she had no intention of spending Friday imagining what Rio might be doing at each particular hour.

    At six thirty, Agatha was at home, standing in front of the refrigerator without remembering why she had opened it.

    The apartment felt wrong. Not empty, exactly; Agatha had lived alone for years and generally preferred her own company. The silence was familiar, as were the creak of the pipes and the muted traffic below. What felt wrong was the day itself. Friday had accumulated expectations that the room seemed to share, as though Rio might still arrive at any moment with takeout and an unnecessary purchase from across town. Agatha caught herself listening for a key in the lock twice before abandoning the kitchen altogether.

She tried reading, but discovered at the bottom of each page that she had retained nothing. At seven fifteen, she opened a bottle of wine and poured a glass. At seven twenty-three, she checked her phone, found no new messages, and placed it facedown on the coffee table with unnecessary force.

Perhaps she had complimented the shirt. Perhaps Rio had smiled and touched the chain at her throat, the way she did when praise embarrassed her.

    Agatha took a larger drink.

    She was not jealous. Jealousy implied ownership, and she did not believe she owned Rio. What she felt was closer to the irritation of watching someone else wander into a room Agatha had spent years furnishing, then behave as though the warmth had appeared by accident. This woman did not know that Rio hated olives but continued ordering dishes that contained them because she disliked requesting substitutions. She did not know that Rio went quiet when worried rather than when angry, or that she slept badly after late coffee even while insisting caffeine had no effect on her. She had not sat beside Rio in emergency rooms or driven across the city at two in the morning when Rio's car failed. She had not earned the right to hear the laugh Rio used when she was genuinely surprised, or to see the unguarded smile that appeared only when she had forgotten to protect it. The thought was ugly enough that Agatha set her glass down. The woman had done nothing wrong. She had asked someone she liked to dinner, and Rio had accepted. There was no trespass in that. No theft. Whatever Agatha and Rio possessed remained untouched because neither of them had ever named it as anything another person could threaten. That should have been reassuring.

    Instead, it left Agatha staring at the empty space beside her on the couch and wondering whether the absence she felt was proof that she had named it privately long ago.

She pulled the device free and saw Rio's name on the screen.

No mention of whether the date had gone well, no complaint, no joke about the restaurant, no indication of whether Rio was pleased or disappointed.     She typed, Congratulations. Was she tolerable?

    Then deleted it.

    Deleted that too.

At the time, she had imagined the request as proof that nothing could disturb the intimacy between them. It seemed to demand either retreat or honesty, and Agatha had never been graceful at choosing between them.

    "I hated it," she said.

    Rio's eyes remained fixed on hers. "The date."

    "The idea of it."

    "Why?"

    Agatha laughed once, without humour, and turned away. "You have a remarkable talent for asking questions whose answers are obvious only after they've become impossible to say."

    "They aren't obvious to me."

    "Of course they aren't."

    The words came out sharper than intended. Rio's expression closed again, and Agatha felt the conversation slipping beyond her control. She had imagined this moment in fragments throughout the night: Rio asking, Agatha admitting enough to make her understand, some impossible relief following. In none of those private rehearsals had she accounted for the humiliation of standing before the person who mattered most and discovering that years of closeness had left Rio uncertain of what Agatha herself could no longer deny.

    "What is that supposed to mean?" Rio asked.

    "It means you came here after spending the week telling me about another woman and expected me to sit politely while you described how nice she was."

    "You asked me to tell you."

    "I didn't know what I was agreeing to."

    "Then you could have said you didn't want to hear it."

    "I tried."

    "No, you made cutting comments and expected me to understand what you refused to say."

    Agatha folded her arms, anger rising not because Rio was wrong but because she was not. "Perhaps I assumed four years of friendship had given you some ability to read a room."

    Rio stared at her for a long moment. "That's unfair."

    Agatha knew it was. The certainty arrived before Rio finished speaking, but pride kept the apology behind her teeth. "Is it?"

    "Yes." Rio's voice remained quiet, which made the hurt within it unmistakable. "I asked you what was wrong. More than once. I asked whether you were angry with me, and you told me I was imagining things. You don't get to punish me for believing what you said."

    "I'm not punishing you."

    "Then why does it feel as though I did something wrong by going?" Because you chose someone else, Agatha thought, though the thought was so unreasonable she could barely tolerate it within herself. Rio had chosen nothing except one evening. She had not broken a promise or withdrawn affection. She had accepted dinner because she was free to do so, because Agatha had spent years ensuring Rio would never suspect there was anything to wait for.

    "You didn't do anything wrong," Agatha said, and the strain in her voice made the words sound almost resentful.

    Rio exhaled and looked toward the door. "I think I should go." The suggestion cut through Agatha's defensiveness with immediate clarity. Rio had left this apartment hundreds of times, but never because remaining had become too difficult. Agatha's first instinct was to tell her not to be dramatic, yet the words died before she could make the situation worse. "You don't have to," she said.

    "I think I do."

    "Rio—"

    "I'm not leaving to make a point." Rio picked up her coat from the back of the chair. "I don't want us to say something we can't take back." Agatha nearly told her they had already crossed that line, though she could not decide which statement had done the damage. Perhaps it had happened much earlier, in every silence Agatha had mistaken for protection. She watched Rio put on her coat, the familiar motions suddenly formal, stripped of the ease with which she usually moved through the apartment. At the door, Rio reached for her shoes and paused. Her shoulders lowered slightly as she looked at Agatha again. "I care about you too much to keep guessing."

    The sentence held no accusation, only exhaustion, and that was worse. Agatha felt her anger collapse beneath the weight of it, leaving behind the fear she had been using it to conceal.

    "You think I don't care?" she asked.

    "No." Rio's expression softened with something close to sadness. "I think you care. I just don't know what you want from me."

    Agatha could have answered then. The truth was close enough to feel physical, pressing against her throat with every breath. She wanted Rio to stay. She wanted the dark shirt to remain something she had noticed without wondering who else might touch it. She wanted Friday evenings, spare keys, half-finished conversations, and all the ordinary intimacy they had built without ever naming. Beneath those wants sat another, larger one, impossible to separate from the rest now that jealousy had dragged it into view.

    She wanted Rio.

    The realization was not sudden. Nothing opened or transformed. It felt instead like finally turning toward something that had been standing beside her for years, patient and undeniable, while Agatha devoted all her effort to looking elsewhere.

    But understanding the truth and speaking it were different acts. Agatha had built their friendship carefully, layer by layer, until it had become one of the few structures in her life she trusted completely. One reckless admission could change its shape forever. Rio might step closer, or she might step away, and Agatha was not yet brave enough to risk discovering which. "I don't know," she said again.

    Rio nodded slowly. Whatever hope she had carried into the question disappeared behind a tired smile. "Then figure it out."

    She opened the door.

    Agatha's hand tightened at her side. "Are you going to see her again?" Rio stopped on the threshold without turning. For several seconds, she said nothing, and Agatha wondered whether the question had confirmed every uncharitable interpretation Rio might now hold of her.

    "I hadn't planned to," Rio said finally. "But that shouldn't matter this much to you unless you can tell me why."

    Then she left.

    Agatha remained beside the open door until Rio's footsteps faded down the stairs. She did not call after her. The words were there now, clearer than they had ever been, but they still felt too dangerous to send into the space between them. By the time the outer door closed below, silence had filled the apartment with a weight that made the previous Friday's emptiness seem almost gentle.

    She shut the door and leaned against it, pressing the heels of her hands to her eyes. The afternoon replayed in fragments: Rio asking what she was, Agatha turning uncertainty into cruelty, Rio saying she cared too much to keep guessing. None of it could be repaired by insisting she had been frightened or confused. Rio had offered her multiple chances to be honest, and Agatha had responded by demanding understanding while providing nothing that could be understood.

    Agatha could imagine the door opening again, Rio returning because she had left something behind or decided the argument had been foolish. Yet minutes passed without the sound of footsteps in the stairwell. Agatha went back to the kitchen wiped the counter, and folded the paper bag as though preserving order might undo the damage. Every task was completed with meticulous care, but the apartment refused to settle around her. Rio's absence occupied each room more completely than her presence ever had.

    When Agatha finally picked up her phone, their message thread remained open to the exchange from the previous night.

    She stared at the words, remembering how afraid she had been to hear that Rio had enjoyed herself. The fear seemed almost trivial now. Rio had not planned to see the woman again. The date had not threatened their friendship; Agatha's refusal to admit why she felt threatened had done that far more effectively.

    She typed an apology, deleted it, and began again.

    I shouldn't have said you imagined it.

    The message looked inadequate because it was. Agatha added another sentence.

    You were right that something was wrong. I didn't understand it well enough to explain.

    She read the words several times before sending them. The message delivered immediately, but no response followed. Agatha placed the phone on the table, then picked it up again less than a minute later. She considered writing that she did understand now. The confession could be made in text, where she would not have to watch Rio's expression change. She could reduce four years of fear and affection to a few controlled sentences and allow distance to absorb the consequences. The idea was tempting for precisely the reasons that made it cowardly. Agatha locked the phone without sending anything else.

    Evening arrived gradually, drawing shadows across the apartment while she remained at the table. Rio still had not replied by seven. At eight, the message showed as read. At eight fifteen, a response appeared. Rio: I know. I need some time.

    There was no anger in it, and no reassurance either. Agatha had rarely seen so much distance contained in so few words.

    She wrote All right, then stopped before sending it. The answer sounded dismissive, as though time were a small accommodation she could grant rather than something Rio had been forced to request. She tried I understand, but that felt untrue. Understanding had arrived much too late to justify claiming it now.

    Eventually, she sent: Agatha: Take whatever time you need. I'm sorry.

    Rio did not answer.

    Agatha left the phone on the table and walked into the living room. The blanket remained folded over the couch arm, exactly where she had placed it after cleaning that morning. She sat in Rio's usual place without intending to, the cushion yielding beneath her in a shape made familiar by years of Friday evenings. From there, the apartment looked subtly different. She could see the kitchen doorway, the shelf where Rio always left her keys, the faint scratch on the coffee table from the night they attempted to assemble it without reading the instructions. Everywhere she looked, there was evidence of a life shared in increments too small to recognize as devotion until the possibility of losing it made the pattern obvious. Agatha had spent years believing silence protected what they had. She saw now that it had only protected her from having to ask whether they might have more.

    Outside, the sky darkened over the city. Agatha remained on the couch long after the room became too dim to see clearly, trying to determine how she could tell Rio the truth without making it sound like an answer produced by jealousy alone. She had not begun wanting Rio when the date was mentioned. The date had only stripped away every explanation that allowed her to pretend she did not.

    For the first time, the possibility that Rio might not feel the same seemed less frightening than the possibility that Agatha had waited until it no longer mattered.

    Agatha slept badly and woke with the unpleasant clarity of someone who had spent the night reaching the same conclusion from every possible direction. There had been no revelation waiting for her in the early hours, only a succession of memories she had previously allowed herself to examine one at a time: Rio asleep beside her during the winter storm, Rio standing in a hospital corridor with two terrible coffees and no intention of leaving, Rio laughing so hard on Agatha's kitchen floor that she had been unable to speak. Each recollection had once belonged to its own neat category—friendship, habit, loyalty, convenience. Taken together, they refused to remain so harmless.

    By six, Agatha had given up on sleep and gone to the kitchen, where she made coffee strong enough to be punitive and stood beside the window while dawn reduced the city to colourless shapes. Agatha's message remained the last thing in their conversation. Agatha had checked twice during the night despite knowing no answer would appear simply because she wanted one. The distance in those few words still felt unnatural, like seeing a familiar room with the furniture removed.

    She opened the message thread again and began typing.

    I know what I should have said.

    Agatha stared at the sentence, then deleted it. It placed the burden on Rio to ask, and Rio had already done enough asking.

    She tried again.

    I was jealous because—

    That disappeared as well. Starting with jealousy made the truth sound smaller than it was, as though one inconvenient date had created feelings rather than exposed them. Agatha could imagine Rio reading the message and wondering whether this was panic speaking, whether Agatha would regret it once the immediate threat had passed. Rio deserved something clearer than an admission composed at dawn and sent because Agatha lacked the courage to say it in person.

    By seven, she had decided to go to Rio's apartment. By seven fifteen, she had recognized that arriving uninvited after being asked for time would transform honesty into another act of selfishness. She remained home instead, moving through a morning that seemed determined to last several days. She washed clothes that did not need washing, answered two work emails despite it being Sunday, and attempted to read before realizing she had turned three pages without understanding a sentence. At ten, she walked to the bakery Rio liked and bought the focaccia loaf, then became annoyed with herself for choosing a gesture that could be interpreted as either apology or bribery. She carried it home anyway.

    The knock came just after eleven.

    Agatha froze in the middle of the kitchen with the unopened loaf on the counter and one hand still resting against the paper. For a second, she wondered whether lack of sleep had produced the sound. Then it came again, three measured knocks instead of the familiar turn of Rio's key. That detail hurt more than it should have.

    Agatha crossed the apartment and opened the door. Rio stood on the landing in yesterday's coat, though she had changed into a black T-shirt and jeans. Her hair was tied back carelessly, and the shadows beneath her eyes suggested her night had not been much better than Agatha's. She held no coffee, no takeout, none of the ordinary offerings that usually gave her visits an easy beginning. One hand remained in her coat pocket. The other was curled loosely at her side.

    For several seconds, neither of them spoke.

    Agatha moved aside. "Come in."

    Rio glanced past her into the apartment, then stepped across the threshold. She removed her shoes without being asked but did not take off her coat, and when Agatha reached automatically toward the hook beside the door, Rio's hand tightened around the lapel as if she had not yet decided whether she intended to stay. Agatha withdrew her hand and led the way into the living room.

    Rio noticed the bakery loaf on the kitchen counter through the open doorway and gave Agatha a brief, unreadable look.

    "I wasn't sure you'd come," Agatha said.

    "I wasn't sure either."

    Rio remained standing until Agatha sat, then chose the armchair opposite rather than the couch. The distance between them was only a few feet, but it changed the shape of the room. Agatha had never seen Rio sit there. Guests used the chair. Colleagues and acquaintances sat upright with their cups balanced on the armrest while Rio belonged beside her beneath the blanket, one leg folded under herself and her shoulder within easy reach. Agatha placed her hands in her lap to stop herself from fidgeting. "How much time were you planning to take?"

    Rio's brows lifted slightly.

    "That sounded worse than I intended," Agatha said. "I'm not asking you to hurry. I only—" She stopped and drew a breath. "I didn't know whether you meant hours or weeks."

    "I didn't know either."

    "Do you now?"

    Rio looked down at the floor between them. "I know I couldn't keep waiting for you to decide whether you were going to explain."

    "I was going to."

    "When?"

    "This morning. Then I remembered you asked for time."

    Rio's mouth shifted, not quite a smile. "That's unusually considerate." "I'm making an effort."

    "I can see that."

    The gentleness of the answer loosened something in Agatha, but not enough to ease the fear beneath it. Rio had come back; that did not mean the damage was repaired. It might only mean she had decided that whatever followed should happen face-to-face.

    Agatha leaned forward, resting her forearms on her knees. "What I said yesterday was unfair. All of it, but especially the part where I expected you to understand something I was refusing to acknowledge myself. You asked me directly, and I made you feel foolish for noticing. I'm sorry." Rio listened without interrupting. When Agatha finished, she was quiet long enough that the apology began to feel insufficient again. "I know you were confused," Rio said at last.

    "That doesn't excuse it."

    "No."

    Agatha accepted the answer with a small nod. She had not expected Rio to rescue her from accountability, though some cowardly part of her had hoped the apology might restore enough warmth to make the rest easier. Instead, Rio's expression remained careful, open but protected.

    "I also know why I hated the date," Agatha continued.

    Rio's gaze rose to hers.

    The truth seemed straightforward now that the moment had arrived, yet Agatha found herself strangely aware of the room: the hum of the refrigerator beyond the doorway, the faint traffic outside, the clock on the wall marking each second she failed to speak. She had delivered presentations to rooms full of people without losing a word. She had argued with supervisors, doctors, lawyers, and members of her own family while maintaining perfect control. Nothing had prepared her for the difficulty of saying one honest sentence to the person who knew her best. "I was jealous," she said.

    Rio's expression did not change, though the hand resting on her knee became still. "I thought you might be."

    "That wasn't all of it."

    "All right."

    Agatha looked briefly toward the window before forcing herself to meet Rio's eyes again. "Jealousy was the part I could no longer ignore. It wasn't the beginning."

    Rio waited.

    "I kept telling myself I was protective of our routine," Agatha said. "That I disliked having plans changed, or that I was worried someone new would take up time I had grown accustomed to having. All of that was true enough to be useful, but none of it explained why I couldn't bear the thought of you wanting her."

    Rio inhaled softly. Agatha saw the reaction and nearly lost her nerve, but there was no safe retreat left. Whatever happened after this, she would not make Rio drag the truth from her.

    "I have wanted you for a long time," she said. "Long enough that I don't know when it stopped being something I could dismiss as affection between friends. Maybe there wasn't a moment. Maybe it happened so gradually that by the time I might have noticed, you were already everywhere in my life and I was too frightened to examine what that meant."

    Rio's eyes remained on her, wide now with an emotion Agatha could not yet trust herself to interpret. She had imagined several possible responses during the night: shock, pity, anger, the careful kindness of rejection. Rio's silence contained none of them clearly enough to prepare for. Agatha continued before fear could make her fill the space with something defensive. "I didn't say anything because what we had already felt too important to risk. That's the truth, though it sounds less noble now that I've nearly damaged it by saying nothing. I thought silence preserved the friendship. I thought if I never asked for more, I could never lose what you had already given me."

    Rio looked away then, turning her face toward the window. Her throat moved as she swallowed, and Agatha felt the first cold edge of panic. "You don't have to say anything immediately," she said. "I know this is unfair after yesterday. I know it may sound as though I only reached this conclusion because someone else asked you out, but I didn't begin feeling this on Friday. Friday only made it impossible to keep lying to myself." Rio gave a quiet, disbelieving laugh.

    Agatha straightened. "What?"

    "Nothing."

    "That was very clearly something."

    Rio pressed the heel of one hand against her forehead, and when she looked back, there was something almost pained in her smile. "You really had no idea."

    "No idea about what?"

    Rio stood. For one alarming second, Agatha thought she was leaving, but instead she crossed to the window and stopped with her back partly turned. Her hand remained at her forehead as though she were trying to contain several thoughts at once.

    "Do you remember Alice's birthday last year?" she asked.

    Agatha frowned at the unexpected question. "The bar with the terrible music?"

    "The one where you spent most of the night complaining that every drink had too much syrup."

    "They did."

    "You went home with that lawyer."

    Agatha remembered. The woman had been intelligent, attractive, and sufficiently unimpressed by Agatha to become interesting for roughly three hours. They had kissed outside the bar, gone back to the woman's apartment, and parted the following morning without exchanging promises. Agatha had mentioned almost none of it to Rio because there had been nothing worth mentioning.

    "What about her?"

    Rio turned from the window. "That was when I decided I had to stop." Agatha stared at her. "Stop what?"

    "Waiting for you."

    The room seemed to contract around the words. Agatha could hear the clock again, louder now, though she was no longer certain whether the sound came from the wall or the pulse in her ears.

    Rio lowered her hand. "I'd known for months by then. Maybe longer, if I'm being honest, but Alice's party was when I finally admitted it to myself. You kept touching my arm every time you leaned over to speak because the music was loud, and I spent half the night thinking you were going to ask me to dance. Then that woman bought you a drink, and you left with her." "You never said anything."

    "No." Rio's smile was brief and tired. "Neither did you." Agatha stood without realizing she had decided to move. The coffee table remained between them, but being seated had become impossible. "You liked me."

    Rio's eyebrows lifted. "That's what you took from that?"

    "I'm trying to understand."

    "I loved you."

    The correction was quiet and devastating.

    Agatha's breath caught. Rio seemed to realize only after speaking how much she had revealed, because her gaze shifted away and one hand folded around the opposite wrist. The posture was familiar. Rio used it when she was keeping herself still.

    "I loved you," she repeated, more carefully. "I still do, which is probably obvious now, but I had stopped letting myself believe anything would happen. There's only so long a person can live inside a maybe before it starts feeling foolish."

    Agatha moved around the coffee table. She stopped a few feet from Rio, close enough to see the exhaustion in her face and the uncertainty beneath it. "Why did you think it was one-sided?"

    Rio looked at her as though the answer should not require explanation. "Because you dated other people. Because whenever anyone joked about us, you made it very clear we were friends. Because you trusted me with everything except the possibility that you might want me." Agatha winced. She could remember every joke now, every occasion on which she had rolled her eyes and dismissed the idea with a cutting remark before Rio had the chance to respond. At the time, she had believed she was protecting them from scrutiny. She had never considered what Rio might hear beneath the performance.

    "I thought you knew," Agatha said.

    Rio gave her a look of exhausted disbelief. "How?"

    "I don't know." Agatha gestured helplessly toward the apartment. "This. All of it."

    "This was the problem." Rio's voice remained gentle, but the words carried years of restrained frustration. "You gave me something that looked like love from every angle and called it friendship whenever anyone noticed. I didn't know which part I was supposed to believe."

    Agatha had no defence. She looked around the room, seeing it suddenly through Rio's eyes: the mug, the blanket, the spare umbrella, a thousand quiet acts that might have meant everything or nothing depending on which version of Agatha spoke afterward.

    "I'm sorry," she said.

    Rio shook her head. "I'm not telling you to make you feel guilty." "I do anyway."

    "That isn't useful."

    "No, but it's accurate."

    Rio's expression softened despite herself. "You're impossible." "So I've been told."

    The familiar exchange settled between them, fragile but recognizable. For the first time since Rio entered, some of the tension in her shoulders eased. Agatha wanted to reach for her, but the habit of assuming access to Rio's closeness now felt dangerous. She kept her hands at her sides. "The date," Agatha said after a moment. "Was it because you were trying to move on?"

    Rio nodded. "She asked. I thought I should say yes instead of inventing a reason not to. Alice has spent months telling me I can't keep comparing everyone to someone I've never even asked."

    Agatha's mouth tightened. "Alice knew?"

    "Not at first. She figured it out."

    "And said nothing to me."

    "She was on my side."

    "There were sides?"

    "There are always sides with you."

    Agatha nearly smiled, though the thought of Alice observing the situation with superior knowledge remained irritating. "How long has she known?" "Long enough to become unbearable."

    "I'll deal with her later."

    Rio laughed, a real one this time, though it faded quickly when Agatha asked, "Did you enjoy the date?"

    "She was lovely."

    Agatha's face betrayed her before she could stop it. Rio saw, and something warm entered her expression.

    "That wasn't the problem," she said.

    "What was?"

    Rio's eyes held hers. "She wasn't you."

    The simplicity of the answer did what Agatha's jealousy, fear, and sleepless night had failed to do. It removed every remaining doubt so completely that for a second she could only stand there, looking at Rio while years of uncertainty collapsed into something almost embarrassingly clear. Rio continued, perhaps because Agatha had gone silent. "She told a story about getting lost in Prague, and I thought about the time you refused to admit we were lost in Brussels even after we passed the same fountain three times. She ordered dessert and offered me the first bite, and I remembered you stealing half my cake on your birthday because you said yours was inferior. By the end of dinner, I knew agreeing to see her again would be unfair. She was perfectly nice, and I spent the whole evening wanting to tell you things."

    Agatha's throat tightened. "You could have."

    "You were the reason I was there."

    "That has never stopped you from complaining to me before." Rio smiled faintly. "I thought texting the woman I was in love with during a date intended to help me move on might be counterproductive." "Yes. I can see the logistical difficulty."

    For a moment, neither spoke. The silence no longer felt hostile, but it was not yet easy. Too much had changed in too little time. Agatha had imagined the confession as a line they would cross, after which everything would become immediately understandable. Instead, the room held the weight of all the possibilities they had denied themselves, mixed with the uncertainty of what they were allowed to do now.

    Agatha looked down at the small distance between their hands. "So what happens?"

    Rio's gaze followed hers. "I don't know."

    "That's becoming a troubling theme."

    "It has been less than ten minutes since you told me you wanted me." "I said I'd wanted you for a long time."

    "Yes, which somehow makes it worse."

    Agatha looked up sharply, but Rio was smiling. The expression was tentative, the humour offered carefully, and relief moved through Agatha so suddenly that she almost laughed.

    "Worse," Agatha repeated.

    "I could have used this information two years ago."

    "You could have said something too."

    "I considered it."

    "When?"

    "Several times."

    "And?"

    "And then you would call me your best friend in a tone that made it sound like the highest possible honour, and I decided I'd rather keep that than risk discovering I'd imagined everything else."

    Agatha absorbed this. "So we both chose silence to preserve the same thing."

    "Apparently."

    "And nearly ruined it with the silence."

    Rio nodded. "We're very intelligent."

    "Exceptionally."

    "Just not about this."

    Agatha finally allowed herself to smile. "Idiots."

    "The biggest."

    The laugh that followed belonged to both of them, quiet at first and then helpless once the absurdity of the situation fully settled. It was not enough to erase the argument, but it released something that had been locked between them since Friday. Rio covered her face briefly with one hand. Agatha looked away, laughing despite the ache in her chest, and for a few seconds they were themselves again.

    When the laughter faded, they remained standing close together. Rio lowered her hand, and Agatha saw that her eyes were bright. "I don't want yesterday to be how we begin this," Rio said. Agatha's smile disappeared. "Neither do I."

    "I'm still hurt."

    "I know."

    "And I need you to understand that I can't keep guessing what you mean when you refuse to say it."

    "I do understand." Agatha hesitated before adding, "Or I'm trying to." Rio nodded. "That's enough for now."

    The words were kind, but Agatha heard the boundary within them. She would have to earn back the ease she had treated as permanent. The realization did not frighten her as much as it might have the day before. At least now there was something honest to build from.

    "I don't expect this to repair everything immediately," she said. "I only needed you to know that it wasn't the date. Not really. It was the possibility that you might build with someone else what I had been too afraid to ask for."

    Rio looked at her for a long moment. "What are you asking for?" Agatha had spent years avoiding that question in every form. Now that Rio offered it plainly, the answer felt almost modest compared with the fear surrounding it.

    "A chance," she said. "Not to pretend yesterday didn't happen. Not to leap from this into something we haven't defined. Just a chance to stop behaving as though wanting more would destroy what we have."

    Rio's expression softened, but she did not answer immediately. Agatha resisted the urge to fill the silence. This was Rio's choice, not a conclusion Agatha could argue her toward.

    Finally, Rio said, "I'd like that."

    Agatha exhaled, only then realizing how shallowly she had been breathing. "But," Rio added, and Agatha's relief sharpened into attention, "I don't want us to treat this as though we've already been together for years and simply forgot to say so."

    "We have essentially been dating."

    "We have been friends."

    "Friends who spend holidays together and keep clothes in each other's homes."

    "Friends can do that."

    "Friends who shared a bed."

    "Because of a snowstorm."

    "You put your head on my chest."

    "I was asleep."

    "You were not."

    Rio's smile widened despite her effort to remain serious. "This is exactly what I mean. We can't skip the part where we actually choose it." Agatha understood then. Their familiarity made it tempting to absorb romance into the life they already shared, to assume nothing needed to change because so much was already present. But choice mattered precisely because habit had concealed their feelings for so long. Rio did not want to become Agatha's partner through inertia. She wanted to be asked. Agatha straightened slightly. "Would you go on a date with me?" Rio stared at her.

    Agatha felt a flicker of alarm. "Was that too direct?"

    "No." Rio's smile arrived slowly, transforming her entire face. "It was just very fast."

    "You complained that I'd taken too long."

    "That doesn't mean I expected you to correct four years in one morning." "I'm efficient once properly motivated."

    Rio shook her head, still smiling. "Yes."

    "Yes?"

    "Yes, I'll go on a date with you."

    Agatha had imagined hearing that answer from Rio in several impossible scenarios over the years without admitting what she was imagining. None of them had included the aftermath of an argument, Rio still wearing her coat, or a loaf of bread sitting forgotten on the kitchen counter. The imperfection of the moment made it feel more real.

    "Good," Agatha said, then grimaced at the inadequacy of the word. Rio's eyebrows lifted. "A glowing response."

    "I'm overwhelmed."

    "You look mildly inconvenienced."

    "That is my overwhelmed expression."

    "I'll learn to recognize it."

    The future tense settled warmly between them.

    Agatha's gaze dropped again to Rio's hand. This time, she did not merely wonder whether she was allowed to touch her. "May I?"

    Rio followed her gaze and held out her hand.

    Agatha took it carefully, which was absurd considering how many times their fingers had intertwined without significance assigned to the act. She had pulled Rio through crowded rooms and warmed her fingers between both of hers during winter walks. This felt different because neither of them could pretend it was accidental. Rio's palm was warm, her grip tentative only for the first second before her fingers closed fully around Agatha's. The contact sent an almost adolescent nervousness through her. Agatha looked at their joined hands, then at Rio. "This is ridiculous." "A little."

    "I've touched you before."

    "Not while admitting you wanted to."

    "That seems like an unnecessary distinction."

    Rio stepped closer. "Does it?"

    The distance between them narrowed to less than a breath. Agatha could see the fine lines around Rio's eyes, the slight movement of her throat as she swallowed. Instinct urged her forward. Years of imagining made the possibility of kissing Rio feel both inevitable and impossibly overdue. Rio's hand tightened around hers, but she did not close the distance. Agatha understood why. They had only just agreed not to skip the choosing. She leaned back slightly, though every part of her resisted. "Not yet." Rio searched her face. "You don't want to?"

    "That isn't the issue."

    "Then what is?"

    Agatha glanced at their hands. "I asked you on a date."

    "You did."

    "And you accepted."

    "I did."

    "So I'm going to take you on one."

    Rio's mouth curved. "Before you kiss me."

    "Precisely."

    "That's unexpectedly traditional."

    "Only when it suits me."

    "You're going to regret this rule."

    Agatha met her eyes. "I already do."
