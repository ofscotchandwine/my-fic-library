Kara hated working late. 

She didn’t like leaving the office when it was already quiet around her, everyone gone except for Steve, the private security guy at the front desk.

“Do you have somebody to walk you home, Ms. Danvers?” He asked her whenever she put in overtime to meet a deadline, as though he expected the answer to change.

“No,” Kara smiled. “But it’s only a few blocks anyway.”

“If you’re sure.” He invariably replied, and although Kara couldn’t see his face, she could tell from the lilt of his voice he didn’t like the idea of her walking home alone. 

A few more steps and she was outside. The last time she’d worked so late — burning the midnight oil , Snapper liked to call it despite never walking out of CatCo later than 5pm — it had been warm. The last sweet whiff of summer tickling at her nose. 

Nights were colder now, not quite fall but certainly not balmy summer anymore. The air had an edge to it that pressed cool against her cheeks after so many hours spent inside the stuffy confines of the office, and she regretted not having thrown a light scarf inside her bag that morning. 

Then again, she hadn’t known she’d be working so late .

She left CatCo and turned right. The city was quiet, as still as it ever got in the middle of downtown. The only sounds were the clak, clak, clak of her white cane striking the sidewalk a few paces ahead of her feet, and every so often, the rumble of a car passing her by. 

Others might think that it was easier to walk without having to negotiate with the rush hour crowd, and to a certain extent they’d have a point. 

Kara had lost count of the times she’d been jostled, growled at only for the anger to subside the moment people noticed the cane in her hands. 

However, there was also safety in numbers. The brush of unknown bodies against hers brought a sense of grounding that the emptiness of midnight didn’t have. 

The path was familiar. She greeted the cracks in the sidewalk like old friends. Had counted the number of steps it took to get from one street corner to the next so many times that distance was now muscle memory. 

The vacuum left by the absence of all other movement beside hers was like nightfall for other people, Kara guessed. Known buildings became strange when wrapped by shadows. Foreboding and unfamiliar. The darkened windows akin to empty eye sockets that stared down from above, the barred doors like leering mouths, ready to swallow the unwary whole.

Not that Kara had ever gotten to see the transformation, mind you.

She went by the late night kebab kart on Fifth, stopping briefly to pick up dinner. Lamb skewers, falafels, two large portions of fries. The smell, rich with spices, was enough to make her stomach rumble and her mouth water. 

Kara dug a handful of fries out of the greasy paper bag the vendor had stuffed them in and wolfed them down, juggling the rest of the food and her cane one handed for a couple of steps. The fries were too hot against the roof of her mouth, but she hummed in delight through the brief pain and licked her fingers clean. 

Two pedestrian crossings later, and she knew the park was on her left. The stretch of woodland wasn’t big, but it had a presence , especially at night.

In the summer it would be crowded, even this late. Busy with people walking their dogs, or sitting at the tables of the kiosk that sold ice cream, snacks and beverages all summer long. 

Everything was shuttered now, stored away in anticipation of the colder months. The kiosk would reopen, briefly, for Halloween and then Christmas, with a Santa look-alike stationed outside to attract children. 

Bare branches rustled in the wind with the sound of dry laughter, but that didn’t scare Kara. She could tell, somehow, that a number of the trees were ancient — so old they had been growing here before the city was more than the idea of a settlement in the heads of its founders — and she estimated they must be angry to be confined among cement. She respected that, and when she happened to walk across the park during the day, she made sure to greet her favorites. 

It was when the end of her cane caught on a particularly nasty pothole that Kara heard it. 

Footsteps, stopping a moment after the echo of her own heels had faded. 

“Hello?”

Nothing.

Just the sound of her steps bouncing weirdly then, or her imagination playing tricks on her. 

It happened, sometimes. What people believed about the blind — that their other senses were heightened as a reaction to the lack of sight — was bullshit . Kara, and others like her, had simply learned to rely on their ears and their noses more than the average person, but it was possible to listen for danger too hard. Hear things that weren’t there.

The path that stretched in front of her, of loneliness and despair, was one she had been thrust upon. Even so, though it took a lot of rage and suffering, she’d made her peace with it. But now, she discovered she didn’t want to follow it anymore. 

And she felt lost. 

Outside the one unblocked window, the moon was rising, brushing past the rooftops. Because of the orange glow of the streetlamps, however, its reflected glory was dimmed. Swallowed up and near erased by the glaring neons of the shops’ signs.  

There had been a time when the moon was regarded as a portent, but with progress the lines between night and day had blurred. Blinded by the glow of its cities, humankind had forgotten about the moon, put the stars out of its mind. 

It was just a hunk of rock, now. A sliver. A blink. Men only dreamed of it in terms of conquest, the memory of the blood their ancestors had spilled to win the moon’s favor wiped away by the passing of time. 

Only beings as old as Lena still recalled.

She remade the bed in haste, almost afraid that touching the blankets for too long would mark her. The shallow dent Kara’s body had imprinted on the mattress was hard to ignore, and Lena reached out, fingers skimming across the place where the mortal had been resting.

Rubbing her hands together, Lena straightened and heaved a sigh of disappointment. Another contradiction. How she feared what the tingles may mean, yet yearned to feel them.  

Then, it was time to put the box with her supplies back where it belonged, which was the other thing Lena had done her best to postpone dealing with. 

She dragged it out from underneath the bed, staring at it with pursed lips before carrying it to the middle of the room. 

There she knelt and opened it. 

The surgical instruments she’d used to mend the gash she’d cleaned while Kara was still fast asleep, and putting them away took no time at all. 

As always, Lena made sure the blades and tweezers were secure in their casings before turning her attention to other things. Hard won experience had taught her to be packed and ready for travel at a moment’s notice. And, since the box was one of the few objects she wouldn’t leave behind, she’d made a habit of keeping it nice and tidy at all times.

What she was really looking for lay at the bottom of the box, covered in tightly bound cloth. 

A wooden statue, which had been laquered once. Only traces of color remained, sparks of gilt and midnight blue on the figurine’s flowing cape. It had been in Lena’s possession since Chios. She remembered buying it, if vaguely, but not from whom.

Much of that life was lost to her. The majority of her earlier pilgrimages too. Lena suspected it was a self-preserving mechanism. There was a line inside her mind, like borders on a map, beyond which memories grew blurry. Just flashes of color. Sometimes a face. 

And the few things she remembered clearly were the ones she’d tried her hardest to forget.

Placing the statue on a stretch of moonlit floor, Lena pulled out a small stone basin next. The inside of it was charred and smelled faintly of herbs and the rim was chipped from use. It would serve well for what she had in mind. 

A quick trip to the bookshelf to retrieve a few ingredients and she was ready to begin. It felt senseless to pray to gods she knew wouldn’t listen, or grant a simple blessing. For most of her existence Lena had wanted nothing to do with gods and portents. Her brush with the divine had come with a high price, and she didn’t much care to draw attention to herself again. 

Assuming that some gods were still around to hear.

But tonight she was in need of guidance, of a sign — and for that reason she’d brought the statue out of storage.

The only one she imagined could take an interest, at least long enough to hear her out, was Hecate. One that existed on the fringes, who presided over crossroads and the night. Who cared for all the creatures seeking shelter in the shadows. 

Bits of cedar bark, crushed mugwort and lavender went into the basin. Once satisfied with the quantity, Lena used a flint and steel to set the mix alight. 

Thin ribbons of fire rose from the bowl, the dry bark burning so fast she didn’t even have to blow the flames out. They died on their own, leaving reddish coals and a whiff of fragrant smoke in their wake. 

Blowing over the bowl, Lena sent the smoke to curl around the statue, stilling her mind as she did. Reaching inside herself for the place of quiet at her core, from which she could stare out while the world faded away. 

In her youth she’d had an easier time of it, but back then she’d readily believed in gods and the miracles they worked. Bitterness had made her cynical and jaded, and prayer was hard as a result. Like leaving a sickbed after a long illness to walk on legs that still couldn’t bear full body weight. 

Lena wasn’t sure how long she knelt on the floor, begging for a sign. For anything that would strengthen her resolve. She’d thought her mind made up — stay until Kara was healed, and then move on as she had done countless times before — but the more she contemplated the idea the more she found it flawed. 

She didn’t want to leave, was the thing. It ought to be impossible to yearn this badly for the company of somebody she barely even knew, but Lena’s face twisted, reflecting her internal torment. 

Thanks to her efforts, all traces of Kara’s visit were gone, but Lena felt her anyway. There was no need to close her eyes to picture Kara sitting at the table, or listening as Lena read to her from a book. 

The domesticity of those simple desires was a kick to the teeth, and Lena recoiled. Such ordinary things weren’t meant for her. She shouldn’t want them. Allowing Kara to get too involved with her meant pulling her further and further away from the world she knew. 

A world Lena didn’t belong to. Not anymore.

When it was clear that she’d get no answer to her pleas, Lena climbed back to her feet, grabbing the bowl to safely dispose of its smoldering contents. 

As she smothered the last of the coals, Lena scoffed a little to herself. She should have known it’d be a waste of time. Appealing to the gods hadn’t worked for her, ever. No reason they should start to listen now. 

She was so caught up in her recriminations she completely missed the way moonlight gathered around Hecate’s figure. A nimbus of silvery light cloaked the statue’s shoulders, and spilled across the time-worn features of her serene face in a play of light and shadow that made her lips seemingly twitch.

Like she was smiling.

About, you know, our side project .”

Their side project.

Investigative journalism. What she’d thought she would be doing when she applied to work at CatCo.

Just her fingertips — back and forth, back and forth across the raised script.

And if anyone found out who Lena was, what she could do —  

Kara broke into an unsteady run.

She had to warn her.

Sitting as still as she could, with Kara fast asleep by her side, Lena started to fret that  calling onto Hecate had been a bad idea. 

The gods — Lena huffed quietly. They were seldom merciful, almost never kind. Fickle and incostant, they helped mostly out of boredom and asking for their intercession lightly could be foolish. To call upon them was to wish for a storm without knowing if and where lightning would strike. And those such as the Mistress of the Crossroads were the worst of the lot; one ought to especially beware of the few who answered after dark. 

In retrospect, she shouldn’t have done it, except for the fact that she was tired. Tired of hiding, tired of running. Envious of the lives she saw flowing around her and that she knew she could never have. 

Despite knowing the dangers, she’d gone ahead with it. She’d taken out the figurine of Hecate for the first time in centuries, and words so old they had long been forgotten had passed her lips. Now she was at the goddess’s mercy, regarding all the things that were happening to her with suspicion. Were they signs? Was the goddess trying to show Lena the way? Or had she decided that rather than be pointed in the right direction, Lena needed to be pushed?

Next to her Kara shifted, pressing closer, and Lena’s thoughts promptly unravelled. Kara was soft against her, and the fleece hoodie she wore retained traces of her perfume. Underneath were the round tones of the same soap Lena had showered with, and the sleep-warm scent of Kara’s skin. The snakes had memorized her too, and Lena knew that if needed she’d be able to find her in the middle of a crowd. 

More distracting still were the tingles that accompanied the closeness. They didn’t startle Lena anymore, but she couldn’t say that she was used to them. The sensation settled somewhere between an itch and actual goosebumps, but if she checked her skin for evidence she found nothing. It was pale, and impervious just like stone — but, in blatant disregard for Lena’s nature, Kara had figured out a way to burrow underneath. 

Kara mumbled something in her sleep, and when Lena risked a look at her face she could see her eyes dart behind closed eyelids. Maybe she was dreaming of the night she’d almost gotten robbed, but Lena hoped for something happier. The loose bun she’d gathered her hair in had come partly undone, and locks of gold shadowed her brow, spilled across her cheek. Before she could inhale some of her own hair, Lena tucked the errant strands behind her ear, snatching her hand back when her knuckles brushed warm skin. The tingles increased, and she had to curl her hand into a fist to keep herself from reaching out again.

She wanted to touch Kara and. 

Not stop. 

It had gotten very late, Lena noted from a look at the smartwatch on Kara’s wrist. She admitted that the would you look at the time moment was entirely engineered. She’d not just suddenly realized; her eyes had sought out the numbers ticking by on Kara’s watch. She’d needed an excuse to stop staring because, objectively, it was hard not to look. Hard not to drink in the sight of Kara snoring softly, face half-smushed against a pillow. Open and vulnerable and moonlit — Lena had to put real effort into dragging her gaze away.

Outside the moon hung low, yellow like aged cheese. Seemed bigger so many floors away from the ground — fuller than Lena recalled having seen it in some time. The nearby buildings were dark, the people inside long gone to sleep, and the only sound Lena could hear was the soft gurgle of water in the pipes. 

Lena was starting to get drunk on these small freedoms. Gazing at the moon, sunning on a lazy afternoon. An existence separated by the constant state of hyper-awareness that had characterized her last few centuries. When rest was only a brief pause, the deep intake of breath before the jump in the water, and home was nothing more than temporary shelter. 

She sighed and pinched the bridge of her nose. It’d be easy to become addicted.   

Falling asleep with her nose full of the smell of Kara’s sweater would be easy as well, but Lena knew it wasn’t wise. Not for Kara, anyway. 

Lena had slept in far less comfortable positions, but Kara was going to pay for a night half-draped on the couch with plenty of aches in the morning.  

Waking Kara was out of the question; Lena would have to carry her to bed.  

The task wasn’t a hard one in itself. Lena was more than strong enough to see it through. And, thankfully, even though balancing a person was harder than carrying bags or boxes Kara turned out to be a heavy sleeper. At the very least she was tired enough that being lifted from the couch didn’t wake her up; she did stir, but only to rub her face against Lena’s chest, fingers finding purchase on her shirt.

It was only a few short meters to Kara’s bedroom, but they were the longest of Lena’s life. Halfway there, Kara shifted again, and when her lips skated the underside of Lena’s jaw she almost dropped her. It was involuntary, certainly not calculated; Kara was asleep, how could she mean it? But Lena’s heart crashed hard against her ribs, cut her breath short. It didn’t care for Kara’s intentions, only for the heat of her breath, and the soft press of her lips to Lena’s skin.

She stumbled through the threshold of the door to Kara’s room with none of the sense of propriety that had kept her away before. It was shrouded in darkness, only a silver blade of moonlight knifing through the murk. There was more than enough light for Lena to see, but she tried her hardest not to look. The snakes were under no such compunction, and it took all of her concentration to ignore what images and scents were bombarding her senses. 

Once Kara was stretched out on the bed, Lena breathed a little easier. She saw no way of getting her under the duvet without waking her, so she grabbed what she could find — an extra blanket, a hoodie Kara had tossed over the back of a chair, and laid them on her, making sure she was fully covered.

That done, Lena pulled back, disappointed hisses coming from the snakes. They wanted Lena to stay, lie down next to Kara and spend the night in her bed. 

Which wasn't gonna happen.

"Shh." Lena shushed without success. "You're going to wake her up." 

"Lena?" Her name, mumbled in the dark. 

Lena froze. Went as far as to hold her breath.

She had been so busy bringing the snakes under control that she had not noticed Kara rolling over to face her.

She was not quite awake yet, but she was gonna get there. It was fine; having carried her to bed, Lena had a legitimate reason for being in Kara's room. It was a-okay. Kara was gonna understand.

But now she was staring. Hovering. Creeping . And how could Lena explain that?

The answer was she couldn’t, so Lena (quietly) fled.

Kara woke up with a small start, not expecting to find herself in bed and wondering how she'd gotten there. 

She didn't remember falling asleep. Just sitting on the couch with Lena, a show with descriptive audio turned on low, her belly stuffed with naan and biryani. 

Lena . 

It must have been Lena, who carried her to bed. 

And that was. Well, Kara’d never much liked people taking care of her. Too often that quickly became people deciding she needed help downstairs, or help crossing the road. With groceries or a menu at the restaurant. Grabbing and pulling and steering her where they thought she intended to go, without even ascertaining she did, in fact, need help. It was worse sometimes if she wasn’t alone. People tended to talk to whoever was with her, as if Kara wasn’t just blind, but incapable of understanding too. 

It wasn’t the same with Lena. Kara didn’t feel defensive, or that her agency was being taken away. If anything, the thought that instead of shaking her awake so that she could go to bed on her own, Lena simply picked her up and carried her filled her with warmth. 

It was hot, actually.

So much so that Kara was forced to bury her face into an unused pillow to cool off.

Kara could get used to this. Not Lena carrying her to be every night, of course, but having the gorgon around. When people asked, Kara denied feeling lonely, but the truth was that Alex and Eliza lived a three hour plane away, and that her friends — Winn and James and Leslie were colleagues too. 

They were great people, but work always found its way into the conversation somehow. Whether it was the latest story they’d worked on, or commiserating over Snapper. A roll of eyes when Siobhan’s name came up. Kara cared for them all, especially for Winn, but she wanted someone in her life she could share other things with too. Someone she could talk with of her fears, her hopes past maybe, someday, winning the Pulitzer or becoming editor-in-chief, and admit that it didn’t matter to her if it was CatCo or a small town newspaper only a couple hundred souls read as long as she could make some sort of difference. The others — all of them had lofty goals, things they wanted with a degree of certainty that left Kara steeped in jealousy. James wanted to become a war photographer, for Leslie CatCo was the stepping stone to the Times or the Washington Post. And even now, Kara was sure she’d find Winn working on their exposé on Edge — he wanted justice like she did, but he also wanted glory.

The one time she’d timidly admitted she wouldn’t mind teaching journalism to others she was met with disbelief. Still remembered James’s gentle baritone; surely you don’t want to live in someone else’s shadow.

Maybe it would pass, the infatuation she was feeling. Kara wasn’t that naive. Perhaps she was crushing on the idea of a new person in her life rather than on Lena herself. But even as the thought formed in her mind, Kara knew it was a lie. 

The previous evening the low noise of the tv, the water rushing in the pipes, the one window that didn’t fit well and always rattled when the wind picked up; everything had felt new because Lena was there, seeing it for the first time where Kara could just hear. It made her pay attention to the well-worn corners of the world she often ignored; out of habit, out of tiredness and boredom. 

It had felt like having eyes — eyes that worked — for once in her life. 

But if Lena wanted to go, who was Kara to tell her to please stay? Over something that may turn out to be nothing, over a whim, over a fondness that may be as fleeting as a summer storm. A rush of wind and rain, blackening the horizon and then gone. 

So Kara pushed it down. Locked it away in that part of her mind where she kept her fear of needles and of heights, where she stored unfulfilled wishes and all the things that she'd been told were much too dangerous to want.

She pushed off what Lena had covered her with next, recognizing the fuzzy material of the blanket she kept at the foot of the bed for when she got too cold. Lena had used one of her old college hoodies too, and the lengths she'd gone to make sure Kara'd be warm through the night brought on another smile.

It was early still — the unbroken silence was clue enough to know — but Kara figured that some home-made breakfast was going to be a nice way to say thank you.

She was passing by Lena's bedroom — already it was weird to think of it as the guest room — when she heard them. Muffled whimpers, the small noises of somebody having a bad dream. She should wake her this time, since she was up and able to do something. 

The previous night Lena had fallen quiet before Kara could investigate, but now she was here, standing barefoot at her door. If it was her she'd want to be woken up. Night terrors were uncomfortably familiar to her too, even if they didn't jolt her awake in a cold sweat as often as they had done in the past.

She tested the doorknob, and finding the door unlocked, pushed into the room slowly. Kara knew her apartment like the back of her hand, had spent the first week after she'd moved in mapping every inch. She knew which floorboards creaked if stepped on and how to avoid corners. She measured space in steps: three strides from her bedroom to the bathroom, ten big ones to walk across the living room. But having another person living here provided a new set of challenges.

Like the corner of the (wooden?) box she couldn't see, that almost sent her flying on the floor face-first when she stabbed a toe against it.

Kara floundered, hands outstretched to cushion a fall she thought inevitable. When they met the soft resistance of Lena's bed, relief pulled the air from her lungs in a gasp that was too loud to her ears. Heart hammering in her chest she went completely still, mind working to find a feasible excuse to her presence. Somebody may argue that this was still her house, and Lena was a guest here, but Kara disagreed. She wanted the gorgon to have privacy, to feel safe, but also welcome. That sharing space with her was a pleasure not a burden. 

And while she would have wanted to be woken up from a nightmare, Kara wasn’t sure what Lena’s reaction may be. She should just go; Lena’s breathing was still even, she was still asleep but the sounds had stopped. 

She was pulling away when something cool and leathery feathered her knuckles. The contact was brief, but Kara recognized one of the snakes. She didn’t know which one it was, but it rested its head on the back of her hand, triangular nose booping her skin as if to thank her. 

“it’s okay.” She said, like it understood. Maybe it did. The snakes were clever enough to steal good naan when they smelled some, so. “I just wanted to check on her.” 

Her hand was nudged again, then the snake retreated, leaving her alone with her thoughts. How nice it’d be to wake up to Lena puttering around the house every morning for example, or to wish her goodnight every evening. The two of them finding a balance as the days went by, their existence intertwined. 

They were dangerous thoughts, ideas that could take root if she weren’t careful.

Best not to think these sorts of thoughts at all, but to plan what food to make for breakfast.

Breakfast. 

Right.

A day passed. Then another. A week. Three. 

Coming on the one month anniversary of Lena moving in was something of a shock. Lena still talked of leaving the city, planned well into the night. Kara had procured National Park guides from the library, repurposed an old laptop for Lena to use. One day they spoke of getting her out to Muir Woods, the next it was Alaska. Further away. Norway, or Iceland. The jungles outside Jakarta. 

“I can wait for nightfall, sneak down to the harbor.” Lena said one day over tea and Noonan’s blueberry scones. “Hide inside a container. Two weeks in the hold of a cargo ship and I could be anywhere I wanted to.” 

“Planes are faster.” Kara argued a couple of days later while they were restocking the pantry. “That’d take you across the ocean in hours.”

“Harder to get on than they used to.” Lena sighed. “What will all the extra security these days.” 

“True.” Kara’s snort turned into a sneeze when she inhaled a pinch of the pepper she was transferring to a new glass jar. “Hard to get the snakes by TSA.”

But that was all it remained. Idle talk. Hypotheticals. Plans that never moved from paper to the actual thing. Lena didn’t give Kara a date for her departure, and Kara didn’t ask for one. They recognized the fragile ecosystem they’d created, and knew that one wrong move was enough to destroy it. 

Which, at least on Lena’s part, meant she had to come to terms with the fact she liked living here. And that it wasn’t just the apartment, even though a home where she didn’t feel like an intruder definitely had its perks. Sure, she liked the view, and the fact that the window in her bedroom allowed in so much light during the morning that she could sit cross-legged on the bed and read for hours on end. She enjoyed the cozy kitchen, having water for tea always on hand and not needing to collect it when it rained. And she’d rediscovered the pleasures of food too — she did skip meals, but ate more often than she had in the last four hundred years.

The highlight of it all, however, was Kara. 

Kara, who had hair that reminded Lena of the sun and eyes of a clouded blue that, whilst bilnd, seemed to stare at Lena a little too intensely, as if her body was transparent, her heart and her emotions in clear view. Kara who smiled the widest when she thought Lena wasn’t looking, grinning like she could read into her thoughts. Who was pretty at all hours of the day, but never more beautiful than in the soft light of early in the morning, when she shuffled into the kitchen sleepy and half-dazed, wearing only underwear and an oversized t-shirt. 

It was hard for Lena not to pull her in for a hug then, but thus far she’d managed to resist, and if they maintained contact longer than necessary as she offered Kara the cup of coffee she’d just brewed, well it was only to make sure her grip around it was secure. 

“I took the day off.” Kara announced the morning of the day marked in red on the calendar, wandering into the kitchen a little past 7am. Sun slanted through the dusty glass of the window at an angle, reflected off of the copper pots hanging from the hooks Lena had helped hammer into the wall painting the room in soft strokes of gold. 

“Figured it’d be nice to spend it together.” Kara continued, and Lena was not able to tell whether the pink dusting her cheeks was due to the light or to a blush. “Cause, y’know, a month ago things might have gone a heck of a lot different if you hadn’t been around.” 

The enthusiasm in Kara’s voice died down as she spoke, and once she was done she stuck her head into the fridge, rooting around for the slices of pizza she’d saved from the previous night’s dinner. Lena didn’t have to see her face to know she felt anxious about what she’d just said. They had marked today on the calendar, but neither of them had brought the topic up. What had brought them together; the guy assaulting Kara and Lena turning him to stone was not a thing they liked to revisit.

“Sounds lovely.” Lena edged closer, if only because the pizza was out of the fridge and the snakes wanted their share of the crust. “Maybe we could repot the plants like you were talking of doing the other day.”

They spent most of the morning doing just that, the living room cleared of clutter, couch and coffee table pushed to one side to free up space on the floor. Everything smelled of potting mix, verdant and a touch musky, and surrounded by the bright green of Kara’s plants Lena was transported to the mediterranean bush she’d walked through in her youth. In an attempt to make Lena feel at home Kara had bought some amaranth and chicory, their fragrance heady in the enclosed space despite the fact they’d thrown the window open. 

The wind blew in from the sea that day, and even so deep inside the city Lena could smell it. Brine and salt, with a hint of the gasoline the majority of the ships were powered by these days. It reminded her of her plans, but she refused to dwell on them. As the weeks went by, leaving had become less and less urgent. 

At first she’d fretted Kara would grow tired of having her around, but that fear had waned and passed. There were things that scared her, but the reasons behind had changed.

“I was adopted, did you know that?” Kara spoke up around noon, breaking the silence that had wrapped around the two of them this far. They’d worked their way through half of the plants that needed bigger vases, and Lena paused, fingers buried in soft dirt. “Actually scratch that. How could you? I never mentioned.” 

The people in the picture frames Lena had noticed on her first day here had names now. Alex and Eliza, sister and mother respectively. The man with a spray of grey at the temples was Jeremiah, Kara’s dad, but he’d stopped appearing halfway through the collection of photos. Left them, or dead perhaps. Lena had thought it more polite not to ask. 

“Do you want to talk about it?”

“Well, yes and no. Bio-mom left me at the hospital when they told her there was a good chance I’d never see, and it hurt, but I mean, you get over it eventually. I guess knowing what day it is today got me thinking about it.” Kara chewed her lower lip the way she did when she was about to say something that mattered to her, but hadn’t quite figured out the words. “What I want to say is… I don’t care how long you decide to stay. I remember how it felt being unwanted and—” A shrug, and she was tugging the vase Lena had just filled closer, ready to give one of the uprooted African violets its new home. “You’ll always be welcome here, and I figured today was as good as any to let you know, is all.”

Lena didn’t know how to reply. 

There had been a tightness in her chest she hadn't known was there until Kara’s reassurances. It loosened, left her in a rush like steam whistling from a teapot, making her shoulders sag a little. 

Before she could stop herself, Lena had sought Kara’s hand,  laced their fingers together. They had touched other times, but it was almost always accidental. Not this time. And the tingles were different too, so intense Lena felt as though her hand was burning. 

She hastily withdrew, but Kara didn’t give her time to go very far. Or rather, it was what she said that stopped Lena in her tracks.

“I feel it too.” Trepidation weighted her voice down, reduced it to a whisper. “Whatever you’re feeling when we touch.”

“I don’t know what it means.” Lena confessed, the snakes rustling and hissing as they sensed her unease. Asklepios bumped its nose against her earlobe, seeking comfort. “But whatever it is I don’t want it to hurt you.”

“I’m not afraid of finding out.” Kara offered her hand, palm up. Smudges of dirt stained her skin, gathered in the creases. “If you want.”

After that all was quiet. It was only a moment, but to Lena it felt that the entirety of the years she had already lived was flashing by. 

“Alright.” She watched her fingers curl around Kara’s, squeezing gently. “We can figure it out together.”

She closed her eyes and for the first time since they’d met she didn’t fight the warmth that came with touching Kara.

Winn brought Leslie into their project. 

One phone call, and the story they’d been working on for so long wasn’t just theirs any more. 

After, Kara tried to tell herself she was overreacting. It wasn’t as though he’d cut her out. “I simply think we can use the help.” He explained. “A fresh set of eyes to analyze what we’ve gathered so far. Maybe Leslie can find something we’ve missed.”

Winn was the one person she felt she could truly be herself with. To him she could admit when she was scared, when the absence of her eyesight became a weight too heavy to carry. He didn’t… freak out about it. He didn’t meet the admission with pity. The others did their best, but if Kara let it slip that she was feeling overwhelmed their worry soon became a cage. They meant well, all of them, but they were too much sometimes.

A lot of the time.

That he too had finally bought into what  the majority of people already believed in. Kara’s lack of eyesight stood, somehow, in the way of their success. Something had changed.

The shift was subtle. In the beginning, Lena barely noticed. And even after she did, she initially thought nothing of it. An off day could happen to anyone and Kara was certainly not immune to a bad mood. But the clouds lingered, first for a week and then more. 

Lena grew restless.

“I think something is wrong.” She confessed to the snakes one morning, while Kara was at work. She’d thought they were getting somewhere over breakfast. Kara had looked like she wanted to unburden, had actually opened her mouth, but nothing had come of it as always. “She doesn’t say, but I feel it.” Lycus flicked his tongue out, tasting the air. “You feel it too, don’t you?” 

She didn't need his answering hiss to know that he did. 

"Well, if I knew why I wouldn't be so concerned, would I?" 

Safe inside Kara's apartment, Lena had no need to direct her thoughts to the snakes. Speaking them aloud felt liberating, and also helped her untangle her emotions. Mostly. 

She’d made a habit of it, and had gotten used to hearing her own voice again. Sometimes, she forgot herself enough to sing — old songs that had not passed anyone’s lips in centuries — but never with Kara was around. It was a tempting thought, to hum to her softly when she was on the verge of sleep, but Lena was far too self conscious to give in.

"I said I don't know!" Lycus writhed despondent in front of her, and she had to push him out of her eyes not to stumble. Thinking about Kara had set him off, and the rest of the snakes stirred as well. Lena’s worry was theirs, too, but they also fretted about her. She had done everything in her power not to become too attached, but thoroughly failed. The snakes knew she’d leave if she had to, and that it’d irreparably reduce what was left of her heart into dust.

"She's slipping away,” Lena shook her head, as if that was enough to dispel the bleakness from her thoughts, “and I have no idea what's changed." 

Maybe it was something she’d done. Maybe her time here was coming to an end. Her heart dropped, her stomach tightened. Shelter was not going to be a problem. Lena had lived rough before, had gone without a roof for months. The idea didn’t bother her. But the lack of company, reverting back to a lack of solitude… She wasn’t alone, obviously, and the snakes filled her mind with all the affection they could muster, even though they dreaded it as well. Going back to an existence without Kara. Now that they were starting to explore their strange connection.

Lena sat down on the couch and grabbed a magazine from the pile Kara made sure to restock for her every week. She leafed through the pages half-heartedly, her eyes skimming across the glossy print without one word registering. She tried to focus, to let the latest gossip be a bit of a distraction, but the lines of text blurred, and something pattered softly onto the page.

Something wet.

Tears.

The magazine thudded to the ground. 

Surely, she was mistaken. It couldn’t be. She hadn’t cried real tears in— 

They always turned to—

Raising trembling fingers to her face, Lena wiped at her cheeks. There was no mistaking it; it really was tears.

“How?” She licked her lips, unable to continue. Some unknown emotion had lodged itself at the back of her throat. Something dangerously close to hope. “How is this possible?” The only answer was a rustle, the snakes as confounded by this new development as she felt. “How?” 

She sat on the couch for hours, expecting the tears to become tracks of dust upon the page, but they did not. They dried to tenuous traces of salt on her skin, and she rubbed at her face again, asking herself questions she had no answers for.

She had no idea what was happening to her, not yet, but Lena knew one thing.

It had been the thought of leaving Kara to have made her cry real tears after so long.

Whatever that meant.

She lived week in week out through a fog of her own doing. 

Everything became routine. Kara woke, shared a quick breakfast with Lena, went to work and stayed there until she was numbed and so exhausted that all she managed to do once she got home was to drag herself to bed. It was unhealthy. It wasn’t a good way to deal, but the alternative; facing Winn and explaining why what he’d done sat so badly with her — Kara didn’t have the strength for it. 

It was easier to just roll with things. To hand Leslie a copy of their files and pretend that everything was fine. If Winn thought it strange that she declined to spend any extra time with the two of them after work, he didn’t say. She hadn’t been much company lately, anyway. Not since Lena had moved in. 

Lena who must have figured out that something was wrong by now. Who had become collateral to Kara’s awful mood. She had wanted the gorgon to find a haven in her house, but she’d ended up making her unwelcome. 

She had to make it up to her. She would, somehow.

On the way back from work, she bought flowers. The bouquet reminded her of the last real moment of happiness she’d had, potting plants with Lena well into a sunny afternoon. They’d talked, admitted to that strange feeling of warmth that touching one another brought. Kara would go back to that day if she could, and never leave it. Before Winn had called, before life started to slip from her fingers like sand.

And it had, she realized now that Winn had turned to someone else for help. It left a hole behind, a crater of dark thoughts Kara had allowed herself to fall into. She had forgotten all the things that really mattered; the weird familiarity of a gorgon’s touch, myrtle sweetening the air inside her house. 

She was not going to forget again.

“I’m sorry I’ve been so awful lately.” She entered the apartment flowers-first, but got no response. “Lena?” 

She was there, Kara could tell. She heard the snakes, and heard her breathe, and even if she hadn’t she’d have felt her gaze. It was hard to explain to people with sight, but Kara could always tell when she was being watched. Gazes had a weight, the intent of the thoughts behind them like a brush of skin on skin. 

“Is it the flowers?” She should have known, flowers wouldn’t be enough. Perhaps bringing flowers to someone had a whole different meaning in ancient times. Maybe, instead of mending things, she had just offended Lena. But the florist had said— 

Kara frowned. She had left the shop not fifteen minutes before, but the memories of it were already hazy. It was the store she went to for her plants — she’d liked surrounding herself with greenery even before Lena came to live with her — but the woman who had helped her pick the right bouquet was new. 

She had been more than happy to describe the flowers to Kara — blue and purple, she had said their colors were — and then, while she rang her up, she’d added something else in passing. A few words about their meaning. That this particular plant belonged to a certain goddess, or that it was fit for the gods. Kara wasn’t sure.

“The flowers are beautiful.” Strong fingers curled around her own, Lena cradling both her hands gently as though she was made out of spun glass and one hasty touch away from breaking. “Nobody has given me flowers in such a long time.”

It warmed Kara to be the first to do so, but the slight quiver in Lena’s voice concerned her too. There was something else the gorgon was not saying, and Kara wondered whether she might be wrestling with some unpleasant memory. 

“I’m glad you like them.” She waited for a few long heartbeats then added. “I wanted to say how sorry I am. You must’ve thought so badly of me, and with reason. I’ve done such a bad job of being a host and—”

“Kara.” Ever so carefully Lena threaded their fingers together. “You have nothing to be sorry for.” The hold she had on Kara’s hands incrementally tightened, and she took an involuntary step forward. They were close enough Lena’s breath skated across her cheek, and the snakes bridged the rest of the distance — a multitude of tiny heads came to rest with their reassuring weight atop her shoulders. 

“So… you’re not upset?”

“I’m not. But you have been and I was thinking…” Lena trailed off and Kara wished, not for the first time, to see her expression. Gauging people’s reactions was for her a constant effort. Voices betrayed a lot, as did body language, but a lot of the time Kara felt that she’d been given only half of the vocabulary compared to everyone else. What she didn’t see, she had to infer, and it wasn’t always easy. Especially when it came to someone like Lena who, out of hurt and maybe diffidence, played her cards close to her chest.

“You were thinking…what?” 

“It’d be nice to set worry aside, for a while. Don’t you think?” Kara didn’t mind that Lena led her to the kitchen. She had no need for guidance here, these walls had long since relinquished all of their secrets, but Lena was not offering help. She’d just — they were still holding hands, and neither of them seemed to want to let go first.

“What did you have in mind?” 

“There’s a spot atop the roof you can see the entire city from.” Kara had gone on the roof only once, when she’d first moved into the apartment. The realtor had insisted she should see the view, as if that’d make a lick of difference. “I was thinking that… uh… we can sit there a while and I can…uh… describe it to you?” Lena let go of her hand, and Kara heard one of the cabinets open. She was clearly looking for a vase. “It’s a stupid idea, isn’t it?” Lena muttered as the silence between them grew deep, a living thing crouched in the far corner of the room. “Forget I said—”

“It’s a lovely idea.” Kara hurried to reassure. She had been so taken aback that Lena might want to spend time with her after her horrible behavior of the past few weeks, she hadn’t known how to convey her relief. The silence was obviously taken the wrong way. “But… are you sure it’s safe?” Last thing they needed was one of her neighbors catching one deadly glimpse of Lena. “I’m just as happy if—”

“It’s safe. I already checked.” With the flowers taken care of, Lena reached for her hand again. The tingles were a pleasant buzz at the base of her skull and, with a small start, Kara realized they hadn’t stopped when Lena let her go, like previous times. Direct contact made them more intense, but they’d started the moment she’d set foot in the apartment. Like… a background noise she had grown so used to she had to put actual effort into noticing. 

“Besides,” Lena continued, gaining momentum. “I wanted it to be… special.”

“Special?” Kara inhaled. Exhaled. Her heart didn’t stop racing. “As in a— a date?” Never in a thousand years, she’d thought she’d have the guts to say that word out loud. Did gorgons even date? And why on Earth would Lena want to date her? She braced herself for rejection. If Lena laughed, she decided, she’d laugh too. Pass it off as a joke. She could do that. 

 “Exactly! I mean…no?” Kara’s heart sank. “Unless— unless you want it to be? Because if you did—”

“I do!” Kara had no clue how she still managed to talk, since she was fairly certain that in her haste to reply, she’d just swallowed her tongue. “A date with you sounds—” Perfect. “good.” God, Lena must think her a fool.

“Great.” Lena was smiling into the word. Kara felt it, a wave of warmth leaving her cheeks flushed a bright red. Being in Lena’s presence was like stepping into a pool of sunlight after long months spent cooped up inside. She had missed this, the companionship, the easy silences, these tiny pockets of shared existence. Away from the world and its evils, from life and the scars it left behind. Her rift with Winn wasn’t mended, Morgan Edge had not been stopped, but it was okay to take some time for herself. Nobody’d begrudge her need for rest. She had been too hard on herself, too demanding, so blinded by what she couldn’t solve she had pushed away the one person who would understand. 

And though things had been awkward at the start of the night, if they had been unsure of where they stood in regards to one another, now the tension left. Kara felt herself smile, big and relaxed for the first time in weeks, and didn’t have to fight the urge to stifle it.

Her reward was Lena stepping closer. Right into her space. “Shall we head upstairs, then?” 

Friends. Kara thought desperately as she allowed Lena to take her to the roof. They were friends and Lena was doing this to distract her from her problems. Nothing more.

Plus. friends could go on dates too.

Her treacherous heart beat to a different tune.
