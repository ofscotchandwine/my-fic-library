    THE LIFE AND FILMS OF CAROL STURKA BY GRACE RAWSON

    **Excerpt pre-publication. For publicity only. Chapter 1: The Grand Entrance**

    Carol Sturka’s name first made headlines in March of 1996 when she was 25-years-old after the premiere of her first feature film at the Sundance Film Festival. The festival, a haven for independent films, invited and continues to invite indie and new filmmakers from across the US and gives many first time directors, like Sturka, their first shot at distribution. Several of Sturka’s peers also had their first films premiere that year, Michael Richards with Small Town (1996), Emily Lawrence with Standard Issue (1996), and Sam Wilson with Violet (1996). The beginnings of a class of directors Sturka would spend her life working alongside. 

    Sturka’s first film, Cloaked by the Moon (1996), was a surreal story set in 1776. It follows the life of an ostracized woman forced to join a cult after her father dies suddenly and debt collectors threaten to have her imprisoned. The cult believes themselves to be chosen by some mysterious higher power, the movie never fully clarifies what that higher power is, they believe that a time will come where they will be taken from Earth to Heaven because of their ‘virtue.’ 

    The film would go on to be cult classic, both written and directed by Sturka; it bears all the hallmarks that her future films would build on. Accounts from the festival attendees note that Sturka was present at almost every screening for the duration of the festival alongside her then “friend,” Helen Umstead. Sturka was seen being dragged from room to room by Umstead as the up-and-coming producer introduced Sturka to everyone in sight who might buy their film.

    It is just about impossible to talk about Carol Sturka’s career without talking about Helen Umstead. 

    The two of them are so linked culturally you can’t think of one of their names without finishing it with the others. Sturka’s early career and success are a direct result of two things, her talent and Helen Umstead’s ability to be in the right room at the right time.

    Cloaked by the Moon is looked back on fondly by film critics and fanatics alike. It’s far from Sturka’s best film, it’s not her best script or best concept but it’s the beginning of it all. In the movie you can see Sturka working out the beginnings of her style and her pacing. You can feel the unique care she would become known for giving her characters, the way she lets them breath and linger on screen for so long it almost becomes uncomfortable. 

    Cloaked would gain a passionate, almost cultish fanbase after its limited release, as would all of Sturka’s films. Sturka’s fanbase was and is particularly attracted to the directors portrayal of her female leads and in the early films; the underlying homoerotic tension—which would eventually become explicit.

    In Cloaked, a young woman, Anna, is brought in by the cult and is immediately enamored with their charismatic leader, Hanna. Over the course of the movie Anna becomes obsessed with Hanna and rises in the cult to become Hanna’s right hand. Anna does anything Hanna asks of her including murder a rising detractor amongst the cult who fights to take control away from Hanna. Anna is seemingly rewarded for the murder by being allowed to bathe Helena, leading to a particularly iconic bathtub scene. 

    The movie is shown every few years in smaller art house theaters with sold out shows where the crowd laughs and calls out at the screen in jeers and cheers as Anna and Hanna interact. The tone of Sturka’s films often walks the line between humor and darkness, something she would master in her third film, Fruits of Our Labor (2007).

    In interviews, speaking about Cloaked, Sturka says that her memories of making it are some of her fondest. It was her first time on a set that was completely her own, she wasn’t doing a project for school or making a movie in her backyard. Everyone looked to her for answers and they didn’t argue with her about her choices because she was in charge. She always mentions Umstead in these memories as well, noting that the only one who would argue with her was Helen. Other key figures on the set of Cloaked regale me during our interviews with memories of Umstead working tirelessly to solve any problem that might have arisen before Sturka could be told. 

    About the making of Cloaked by the Moon, Sturka had this to say, “We discovered how to make a movie on that set. We were only a few years out of school and suddenly two 24-year-olds were in charge. I learned more about myself and more about Helen than I had in four years of school or dating and I knew after we got the final shot of that film that we’d spend our lives working together, if only for the reason that no one else would be able to stand us.”

    Sturka’s second film solidified her place in the industry as a director to watch and after the film's release her name was on everyone’s lips. Sturka and Umstead had more meetings in the months after their second film’s release than some filmmakers had all year. 

    Their second film was a box office boom and was all anyone talked about for months after its release. The film was nominated for Best Director at Cannes where it debuted and Sturka, to the audience's shock, won. The film would win several other awards from festivals but would not win any from major awards bodies; every major outlet at the time called it, “the snub of the decade.”

    Over the years Sturka would be linked to hundreds of people as friends or colleagues but only a few hold the test of time and seem to build true long-lasting relationships.

    Manousos Oviedo, who began working with Sturka and Umstead in 2007 on their film, Fruits of Our Labor as an editor. He would serve as the lead editor on all of Sturka’s films after this first collaboration. 

    Manousos Oviedo had a somewhat similar early life to Sturka. He was born in 1971 in Asuncion, Paraguay. His father ran a storage rental business and his mother stayed home with the children. Oviedo is the youngest of three children with two older sisters, both of them remain in Asuncion with their own families. Oviedo expressed an interest in the arts early on and his parents, expecting him to take over the business when he got older, were not impressed by his interest in creative pursuits. He would work on films in secret with friends from school and create a portfolio for university while they were none the wiser. 

    Oviedo was accepted at the Universidad de Cine in Buenos Aires, Argentina and never looked back. He packed his bags and drove to the university saying goodbye to only his sisters, he would never return to Asuncion. When asked if he would ever return he would stare blankly into the camera until the interviewer moved on.





    Laxmi Bandara joined during production on Sturka and Umsteads, fifth film after their current director of photography left over ‘creative differences.’ Bandara and Sturka have a more confrontational relationship, on set descriptions of the two of them are often comparing them to children on a playground pulling at each other's pigtails. Obviously a misogynistic comment but ultimately true. They seem to delight in teasing one another and this teasing seems to pull the absolute best out of one another. 

    Just about every film of Sturka’s after Bandara joins their team has been nominated for an Oscar. Bandara herself has been nominated twice for Best Cinematography and won once for her work on I Wished (2027), Carol Sturka’s 11th film.

    Besides their brilliant work, Bandara and Oviedo become very close friends of Sturka and Umstead’s. Both of them are queer, Oviedo a gay man and Bandara a lesbian, and the four of them carve out a place for themselves to work in peace together. Bandara’s son is also a regular presence in behind the scenes footage of their films. He can often be seen trailing after Sturka or sitting in her chair as she works with wide eyes while Bandara rolls hers fondly at his obsession with the director.





    A less talked about but just an interesting friendship Sturka holds is with Mr. Koumba Diabaté . An unexpected duo for sure but no less present in Sturka’s life. 

    Diabaté , the only son of a business tycoon in Mauritania moved to Paris as a teenager to attend boarding school; while in France he developed a fascination and love for cinema. He would sneak out of school after dark to visit the old theaters and watch as many films as he could. Diabate found he had a particular love for American films and their simplicity of good and evil. He found them mesmerizing and when he came of age decided to move to Hollywood and join the business. 

    Diabaté found it hard to get the town to respect him and eventually fell into the role of a party boy. He would host lavish parties the whole of the Hollywood elite would attend and while he seemed to delight in the revels he still longed to be a bigger part of their world. After meeting Sturka at one of these parties she invited him to set and the rest in history. 

    He never worked on the films in an official capacity but he was always the first to offer financing and would use his connections in the industry to get Sturka or Umstead anything they needed and quickly. He’d come to set and sit behind the camera with Sturka and watch her work. 

    ** Photo of Sturka and Diabaté behind the camera, both with headphones around their necks. Diabaté leaning over with a delighted smile and Sturka with her head thrown back in a laugh.**

    Sturka seems drawn to those who have been counted out by the world and gives them a place on her set. Ask any actor who has worked with Sturka and they say they’d happily work with her for the rest of their lives. Sturka is often painted by the media as nothing more than a personality who relies on the work of those around her to elevate. All this does is negate the work she puts into every aspect of her films, the care and the attention she pays to her actors and the time she spends building relationships with her collaborators. What makes Sturka’s films special is not just what happens on film, but what happens behind it.
