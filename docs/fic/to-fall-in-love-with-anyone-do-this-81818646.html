Summary

Carol finds it in the third week of living alone with an unhived Zosia: a New York Times article, “To Fall In Love With Anyone, Do This.” An intimacy experiment involving thirty-six increasingly personal questions and, at the end of it, four uninterrupted minutes of eye contact, which sounds fake and humiliating and somehow still not bad enough to stop her.

Carol, obviously, prints it out.

Notes

I have been obsessed with the 2015 New York Times piece “To Fall In Love With Anyone, Do This” for years, and it became very obvious to me that it would be perfect for Stursia. The article is built around Arthur Aron’s intimacy experiment: 36 questions, asked in order, each set getting more personal, followed by 4 full minutes of eye contact.

Also, yes, apparently Mandy Len Catron, who wrote the piece, did in fact end up marrying the man she did the experiment with. Which is insane.

Residencies. Translation support. Audio work. Archive money. Enough practical help to make talent less vulnerable to humiliation and abuse. Enough money to let one generation lie a little less than the previous one had to.

    She also finishes Wycaro.

    Raban is a woman. The final book causes exactly the kind of scandal she would once have dreaded and now finds almost refreshing. After that she writes the stories she meant to write all along. Filthy, brilliant, queer, political, alive. Girls write to her. Women write to her. Old dykes in improbable corners of the world write to her. Some of them say thank you. Some say finally. Some say where the fuck were you twenty years ago, and Carol, reading those, has no choice but to admit they are right.

    —

    Zosia remains impossible.

    This is one of the great constants of Carol's life now and, to her ongoing humiliation, one of its deepest comforts. She is impossible in the morning, impossible about language, impossible about flowers, impossible in politics, impossible in bed, impossible in a black dress and in a blue suit, impossible in silence, impossible in grief, impossible in happiness. Carol never gets used to the sight of her moving through spaces that belong to them. She gets used only to the fact that she wants to.

    They fight, still. They fight about the world. About books. About translation. About Poland. About America. About whether a film was bleak or merely French. About taking care of themselves. About taking care of each other. The useful part is that neither of them mistakes a fight for the end of love anymore.

    —

    In late spring of the eighth year, Carol finds Zosia on the terrace repotting something with concentration and dirt on one cheek.

    “What happened to your face?” Carol says.

    Zosia does not look up. “This stupid plant.”

    Carol stands there for a second, watching the line of her back, the set of her shoulders, the competence of her hands.

    Then she goes over and kisses the dirt off her cheek.

    Zosia turns her head and looks at her. She is already smiling.

    “What was that for?”

    Carol thinks about saying something dry. She thinks about deflecting, or undercutting, or pretending there must be some external reason for kissing the woman she has built a life.

    Instead, because she is older now and the world ended once and came back differently and she has no patience left for bullshit in love, she says, "No reason. I just wanted to.”

    Zosia looks at her a moment longer.

    Then she says, “Good, kochanie.”

    And because this, in the end, is what their life becomes, Carol says, “Move over. You’re doing that wrong.”

    Zosia laughs.

    Carol takes the trowel from her hand.

    And the afternoon goes on.
