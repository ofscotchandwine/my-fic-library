    CAROL

    Carol is nine and she has opinions about games.

    Marbles is the best game there is. Carol always wins at marbles because she practices and the other kids do not, and that is the whole secret, and she is not going to tell them. Jacks is good. Tag is fine if you are It and boring if you are not, so Carol arranges to be It. Hide and seek is for babies, because all the hiding spots in this town are used up and everybody already knows them. Hopscotch she can take or leave. Monopoly is only good if Carol gets to be the bank and read out the rules, because somebody has to keep the money and read out the rules, and it should be whoever is best at it, and that is Carol.

    That is the most important thing to know about games. Every game has somebody who makes it work. Usually it should be her.

    And a good game needs two things in it. A way to win, and a way to stop. You have to be able to win or there is no point, and you have to be able to stop or you can never go in for dinner. Two rules. She worked them out herself and they have not been wrong yet.

    There is one game with neither. Carol does not know its name and won't for a while. But she already knows it's out there, the way she knew about the deep end before she could swim. You stay out of the deep end. You stay out of the bad game. Even if your best friend is the one holding it. Especially then.

    Anyway.

    It doesn't really start with games. It starts with a word.

    The word is metastatic. The doctor says it with his hands in his lap, and Carol's mother puts a hand flat on the counter like the floor moved, and that's how Carol knows it's a bad word before she knows what it means. Later she looks it up. It means the thing didn't stay where it started. It went looking. That's just spreading, which is a word she already had, so she doesn't see why the doctor needed a different one. Doctors do that. They've got an ugly long word for every plain short one, and the ugly long coats to match, and none of it makes her mother less sick. So the doctor can keep his words.

    She's not allowed to say it in the house. So she says it on the bus. Metastatic, metastatic, metastatic, the whole way to school, over and over until it's only sounds, until it's nothing. That's a trick she invented. It works on every word in the world except the four or five about her mother.

    And then there's a tin.

    It's her mother's. A small round tin, the kind that should have sweets in it and doesn't, with a merry-go-round on the lid, horses going up and down on gold poles and not getting anywhere. Her mother has had it since she was a girl. It's lived on the windowsill over the sink Carol's whole life, holding buttons and a spare key and a thimble nobody uses, and Carol's not allowed to touch it, which is why it's the best thing in the house.

    ZOSIA

    CAROL

    Carol has a list of how long things take.

    A hangover takes a day. A bad review takes a week. Less if it's stupid, more if it's right. A breakup takes half the length of the relationship, people say, which is a made-up number, and the people who say it have never lost anything that counted. A book takes a year if you're lucky and three if you're not.

    Grief does not go on the list. That is the thing about grief. It does not take a length of time. It takes whatever it wants and it is still there in the morning, on the end of the bed, every morning, for as long as it feels like.

    Getting off a floor takes longer than people tell you. That is the one nobody warns you about. The actual floor. The hardwood of a sublet in the Village, the night Zosia got in the cab. Carol got down there for a pen and then did not get up, for a while, for a length of time she does not have a number for.

    Anyway.

    It does not start on the floor. It starts with a word. The word is gone. Zosia is gone. Alive somewhere and gone, gone to Europe and then past it, to cities Carol learns the names of off captions.

    And gone does not mean away. That is the cruel part. Zosia is everywhere. Carol cannot buy gum without Zosia watching her do it off a rack of magazines. Zosia is four stories tall on the side of a building on Houston. Zosia is on the back of a bus that pulls off before Carol can decide what to do with her face. So Carol misses her all day, every day, but she cannot even do it quietly, because the city keeps showing her the exact thing she lost, bored and beautiful and forty feet up.

    She reaches for the phone all the time. She does not call. The dare said a year. Then it said more. Then it said nothing at all, and the silence has rules now, and Carol keeps them, because keeping them is the last thing she gets to do for Zosia, and because she is a coward. Both of those are true. She has gotten good at things being true at once.

    A year goes by. Carol does not call.

    She graduates. She is good, it turns out, properly good, and a major house hires her to do the thing she is freakishly suited for, which is writing in other people's voices. Ghostwriting. She becomes invisible for money. She puts words in the mouths of athletes and a senator and a woman who runs a lifestyle empire, and none of those words are Carol's and all of them are excellent, and Carol thinks, often, that there is a joke in here about a woman who can say anything in anyone's voice except the one true thing in her own, but she does not laugh at it.

    And in the second year, at the house, she meets Helen.

    Helen Umstead is an editor and she is kind, which Carol had stopped expecting from people, and she is steady, and she is funny in a dry careful way. Helen does not make Carol feel like she is standing at the edge of something with no bottom. Helen makes Carol feel like there is a floor and the floor will hold. They can talk for hours, about books, about the senator's terrible memoir, about nothing, and the hours are good, the hours are easy, and Carol is so grateful for easy that she could weep.

    It is not like Zosia. Carol knows it is not like Zosia. Nothing is, because Zosia is the thing Carol holds everything else up against, and everything else comes back short. But Helen is real and here and she is choosing Carol, with no box and no game and no sixteen years of almost, just a hand held across a table that means what a held hand is supposed to mean. And Carol is tired. So she takes the hand.

    She is less alone. That is allowed to be the truth. She is less alone and Helen is good to her and Carol loves her, the way you can love a person who is good to you while you are bleeding, which is a real way, which counts.

    When Helen asks, Carol says yes. She means it. And then she stands in the bathroom that night and looks at her own face and thinks about a sidewalk and a tin and you can't hurt me, Carol, and she knows that was a lie, she always knew, and now the proof of it is loaded and waiting, because Zosia is out there and Zosia is going to find out, and it is going to land in the one place Zosia keeps hidden, and Carol is the one who is going to put it there.

    —

    She finds out Zosia is back in New York the way she finds out everything about Zosia now, sideways, secondhand, from a magazine and then from a mutual nobody.

    Zosia is here.

    And Carol stands in front of the SoHo billboard in the cold, looking up at the face she has loved since she was nine.

    She is getting married. She wants Zosia there. She wants Zosia there so badly it frightens her, because there is no version of the day that means anything if Zosia is not there with her, that is just a fact, that has always been the fact, the most important days of Carol's life are the ones with Zosia in them and there are no others. And she cannot call Zosia and say I love you, come back. That door is bricked over. She bricked it herself, with a condom, four years ago, like a fucking idiot.

    But.

    She can call her about a wedding.

    She can do the most cowardly, most Carol thing available, which is to reach across four years of silence not to say the true thing but to ask Zosia to stand under a tree in a park in front of a judge and watch her marry somebody else. She can hand Zosia the cruelest possible version of I still need you in my life and call it an honor.

    She goes home and she finds the number she swore she deleted and never deleted, and she sits with her thumb over it for a length of time she does not have a number for, and then she calls.

    "Her name is Helen. She's an editor. She's good, Zosia. She’s… really good to me.”

And the worst part-” her voice breaks all the way here, snaps in the middle, and she pushes through it the way Carol pushes through everything, "—the worst part is I would still do it again. I would stand there again. For the tin. For you. That's what you did to me. You made me into someone who would do that.”

    Carol's whole body wants to cross the grass. Wants to say the truth. It is right there, it has been right there for sixteen years, I love you, I married the wrong floor, don't go, it was always you, only you-

    "So here's my dare," Zosia says, before Carol can be brave, because Zosia has always known to the half-second when Carol is about to almost be brave, and has always known to head it off. 

    "Ten years. We don't see each other. No calls, no letters, no turning up. You don't come find me and I don't come find you, for ten years, starting tonight." She holds the tin against her chest with both hands, the way you hold a thing you are not giving back this time. "I can't be your friend and you made very sure today I can't be the other thing. So I need it gone. All the way gone. I need to not know what city you're in.”

    "Don't," Carol manages. "Don't do that. Please.”

    "Don't make it a bit," Zosia says. "Not this one. You owe me one straight thing in your whole life and this is it." Her face is wet and open and ruined and looking right at Carol. "Game or not, Carol.”

    And there it is. The last one. Made back at Carol in her own grammar, with no hatch in it for anybody. Carol could say not. Carol could refuse the game right here, on the grass, in the dark, an hour into her own marriage, and finally say the true thing out loud.

    Carol looks at the open ruined face of the only person she has ever loved all the way, and does not say it.

    Because she is married. Because Helen is inside that tent telling her family how happy she is. Because the door is not bricked over now, it is a building with Helen's name on it. Because Carol is a coward, and the one time in her life it would cost her everything to be brave is the one time she cannot afford it.

    "Game," Carol says.

    It comes out of her like something dying.

    Zosia nods. Once. She steps back into the dark, off the edge of the light, the tin against her chest, and the last thing Carol sees before the night takes her is that face, open all the way, wet, broken past the point of going back, looking at Carol like she is finally seeing exactly what Carol is.

    Then she is gone.

    Carol goes back inside and dances with Helen and is a beautiful bride. And for ten years, every time she shuts her eyes, there is Zosia at the edge of the light, holding the box, telling her she broke her heart, going.

    —

    Here is what ten years is made of.

    Ten years is 3,650 mornings of waking up and remembering, in the first four seconds: that she is married, that she is married to Helen, that Zosia is gone, that Zosia is gone because Carol made her go. Four seconds, every morning, ten years. Carol has done the math. Carol does the math on everything.

    And in between the mornings, this. And this. And this.

    A Wycaro novel, the first one, the pirate book she said she would write when she was nine, the one with everybody dying except the one she likes. It sells. It sells stupidly, embarrassingly well, and the one she likes, the one who lives, the corsair at the heart of it that every reader falls for, is named Raban, and Raban is a tall and dark and bored woman, good at being looked at, because Carol could not make her anything else, because Carol's hand will not write a love object that is not Zosia, has apparently never been able to, and so Carol publishes five books over ten years and in every one of them is a woman who is Zosia, and nobody knows, and the woman herself most of all does not know, because the woman is not speaking to Carol and never reads anything Carol writes. So Carol writes her love into a corsair and sells it to strangers, who weep over Raban, who tattoo lines about Raban on their arms, who have no idea they are weeping over a real person in Milan who does not know she is being mourned in print by the only person who ever loved her.

    And this. The marriage. The marriage is good for a while and then it is not, and the not is Carol's fault, the way most things are Carol's fault. Because here is a thing about a woman who married the floor: a floor is not the sky, and after a while a person who has known the sky cannot stop noticing she is standing on a floor, and the noticing turns into a thing she pours into a glass. It starts as wine with Helen at dinner. It becomes wine without Helen, after Helen is asleep, alone, at the desk, under the framed magazine cover Carol never took down and Helen never asked about and they both pretended was nothing. It becomes the thing Helen finds, eventually, the bottles, the lie about the bottles, the second lie. It becomes the reason Helen, who is good, who was always good, who deserved a floor that did not drink itself stupid every night missing a face, finally stops being able to hold. The marriage lasts six years. Helen does not scream at the end. Helen is too good to scream. Helen just says, that she has spent six years married to a woman who was somewhere else the whole time, and Carol cannot argue with it because it is true, and Helen takes her good steady self and goes and rebuilds, and is happy later, with someone else, Carol hears, someone who is actually present, and Carol is glad, the way you are glad about a thing you ruined turning out alright without you.

    And this. Carol imagines her happy. That is the worst of the ten years, the imagining. Because Zosia is everywhere still, more than ever, and the headlines say things now, the headlines have names in them. Zosia married the photographer. The Danish one, or one like him, blond, blue-eyed, a jaw, a man who photographs beautifully beside her because everyone photographs beautifully beside her. And Carol, drunk, at the desk, under the cover, imagines it. Imagines them happy. Imagines Zosia waking up in a white room in some white city next to a man and being content, being fine, being a person who got over Carol Sturka and built a life and is glad every day that she issued that dare in a dark park and walked away. Carol imagines it in detail, to punish herself, the breakfasts, the easy mornings, the man making her laugh the way Carol used to make her laugh. She tortures herself with Zosia's imagined happiness and never once lets herself imagine the other thing, that Zosia might be as wrecked as Carol is, because imagining that is not allowed, imagining that would mean it could have been fixed.

    And this. The only news that is real. Twice a year, maybe, sideways, secondhand, Carol hears something from Amelia. Zosia's mother still calls Carol, still, after everything, because Carol was her good girl for nine years and Amelia does not believe in the silence and does not honor it, calls Carol on her birthday and at Christmas and tells her things, that Zosia is thin, that Zosia works too much, that Zosia does not come home enough, and Carol holds the phone and drinks in every word about a person she is not allowed to know anymore. And from the sister, once or twice, a message, a photo of the family at a table Carol used to sit at. That is the whole supply. That, and the paparazzi, and the magazines, and the interviews.

    The interviews are the worst supply of all, because in the interviews there is her voice. Carol can be in a hotel in another city ghostwriting somebody's nothing memoir, and a television will be on, and there will be Zosia's voice, that voice, low and sweet and accented under the polish, saying a bunch of nothing to a journalist, and Carol will stop whatever she is doing and stand very still and let it go through her like soup on a rainy day. Behind-the-scenes clips. A laugh, caught, real, that Carol knows is real because Carol invented half the things that make Zosia laugh like that. Ten years of stolen voice. Ten years of a face on a wall and a face on a building and a face behind Carol's own eyes every single night, at the edge of the light, holding a tin, open and ruined, going.

    Her heart is not broken. Broken implies it might mend. Carol's heart is just empty, scooped out. She lives in it. She writes pirates in it. She drinks in it.

    And she counts. Carol counts everything. She counts the years going down, nine left, then seven, then four, the way you count anything you are not allowed to want, and she tells herself she is not counting toward anything, that the dare is the dare, that in ten years nothing will be different except that they will both be ten years older and ten years sadder and Zosia will still be married to the Disney Prince and Carol will still be alone in a scooped-out room.

    She is lying. She is always lying about the one thing. She is counting toward the day she gets to see that face again, even hating her, even just once.

    Three years left. Then two. Then one.

    Then a phone call she is not expecting, near the end of the tenth year, about Amelia, who has died.

    —

    The call comes from the sister.

    From Zosia's sister, Alicja, whose number Carol did not know she still had, and Carol knows before she answers, the way you know, because Alicja has never once called her in ten years and there is only one thing that would make her start.

    "It's Mama," Alicja says. "Three days ago, Carol. Her heart." She takes a breath. "I'm calling everyone she would have wanted called. You're on the list, Carol. You were always on the list."

    And Carol, who has not cried properly in years, who drinks instead of crying because the drinking is more reliable, sits down on the kitchen floor of the apartment she lives in alone now and cries for the woman who held her face in both hands at a curb and called her good girl, the woman who set a place for her without being asked for nine years, the only mother Carol had after she had no mother, and the grief is real and enormous and it is also, underneath, a thing Carol is ashamed of, because some animal part of her is already saying it, already knowing what the funeral means.

    The funeral means Zosia.

    The dare said ten years, and the ten years are nearly up. Three weeks. Carol counts it on the kitchen floor with the phone still in her hand, because Carol counts everything: she made it almost the whole way, almost the entire ten, and the thing that finally ends the silence is not the clock running out, it is Amelia's heart running out, which is so much worse. But the dare said no turning up, and a funeral is not turning up, a funeral is the one thing that overrides every rule there is. You do not honor a no-contact dare at the graveside of the woman who fed you. Carol may be a coward but she is not a monster. So Carol can go. Carol is allowed to go. The thing she has wanted for ten years is suddenly, terribly, available, handed to her by the death of someone she loved, and Carol hates that the two things are tangled, the grief and the wanting, hates that she cannot have one without the other, and goes anyway.

    She does not drink on the flight. That is the one thing she manages. She wants to be sober for it. She wants to be exactly herself, no armor, no blur, for whatever this is.

    —

    The funeral is in the town. Of course it is in the town, the same town, the same streets, the church Carol blew out the votives in on a dare when she was nine, and Carol walks in late and sits at the back because she has no right to a better seat, and the whole front of the church is the family, the loud warm family, smaller now, older, and somewhere in the middle of them is the back of a dark head Carol would know from space, from the bottom of the ocean, from inside her own grave.

    She does not see Zosia's face for an hour. She sits at the back and listens to a priest she does not know say words about Amelia in two languages, and she watches the back of that head not move, held perfectly still in the way Carol taught her, or in the way they taught each other, and Carol's whole body is turned toward it like a plant toward a window and she makes herself stay in the pew.

    It is after, outside, in the cold, by the cars.

    Carol is hanging back, letting the family have her, not pushing in, when the crowd shifts and there is a gap and Zosia is at the end of it, in black, thinner than the magazines, real, three-dimensional, ten feet away, and she is already looking at Carol.

    Of course she is. She has probably known Carol was in the church the whole time. They have always been able to feel each other in a room.

    And Carol braces for it. For the wall. For the bored magazine face, the what are you doing here, for ten years of a dare being broken to land on Zosia's features as ice. Carol has earned ice. Carol is ready to take it.

    It does not come.

    Because Zosia looks at her across ten feet of cold churchyard, over her mother's coffin, on the worst day of her life, and her face does not go to ice. Her face does the opposite. It goes open, all at once, the wall just not there, the way it was not there at the wedding, except this is not heartbreak now, this is something Carol has no word for, this is ten years collapsing in a single second, this is a person seeing the one other person who knew her mother the way she knew her mother, who sat at that table, who got the good girl, and the relief of it and the grief of it and the seven years of it all arrive on Zosia's face at once and Carol watches it happen and forgets every reason they are not supposed to be standing here.

    There is no gap. That is the thing nobody could have told Carol, that she would not have believed. Ten years and a wedding and a dare and a ruined marriage and an ocean of silence, and it is gone, just gone, the second their eyes meet, like none of it was ever there. It is old times. It is the parking lot. It is the bus and the fence and the bed. Her body does not care about the ten years. Her body knows exactly who this is.

    Zosia crosses the ten feet.

    She does not say what are you doing here. She does not say you broke the dare. She does not say anything at all. She walks the ten feet of churchyard and puts her face into Carol's neck and Carol's arms come up around her without being asked, and they stand there, in the cold, by the cars, in front of the whole town, holding onto each other over the body of the woman who made them both, and Zosia is shaking and Carol is shaking and neither of them says one single word, because there is nothing to say, because there has never needed to be anything to say, because they are the two people on earth who do not need the words and never did, which is the whole tragedy, that they could do this, this exact thing, wordless and certain and home, and still have spent their entire lives unable to say it out loud.

    "You came," Zosia says finally, into Carol's neck. Small.

    "She was my mother too," Carol says. "Of course I came.”

    And Zosia holds on tighter, and does not let go, and for the length of that one terrible embrace there is no dare and no silence and no ten years, there is just the two of them, exactly as they have always been, exactly as they will always be, holding on in the cold like nothing was ever wrong and everything is.

    —

    Carol stays for the week.

    She does not plan to. She plans to fly back the morning after the funeral, get back to the nothing memoir and the scooped-out apartment and the rules. But the morning after the funeral Alicja calls her at the motel and says come to the house, Mama would want you at the house, everyone is at the house, and Carol goes to the house.

    It is the same house. Smaller, the way everything from childhood is smaller, but the same, the warm loud house with too many people in it that Carol drove across town to sit in because her own house had condiments in the fridge and a father who was a voice on a machine. And the warm loud people are sad now, moving slow, but they fold Carol back in like she never left, an aunt pressing food on her, a cousin she last saw at seventeen now with gray at his temples saying Carol, my God, Carol, and Carol sits at the table she has not sat at in a decade and is, somehow, home, in a house that is grieving, which is its own strange mercy.

    And Zosia is there. Everywhere Carol turns Zosia is there, doing the thing the oldest daughter does, holding the family up, making the coffee, finding the documents, being strong in the way that is its own kind of breaking, and Carol watches her do it and remembers that this is who Zosia is under all the magazines, the kid who took the blame, the one who went first, the one who would empty a milk carton into a boy's bag for a stranger having a bad morning. The cameras got a different Zosia. Carol knows this one.

    There is so much to talk about, ten years of it, a wedding and a dare and a marriage that died and a husband, and they talk about none of it, because there is a dead mother in the middle of the week and the dead mother makes everything else wait. So instead they just do the small things, the way they always did, falling into the old shorthand without deciding to. Carol washes the dishes and Zosia dries because that was always the way they did it, Carol's hands in the water and Zosia at her elbow with the towel, fourteen again, twenty again, no age at all. Carol carries the heavy things. Zosia knows where Carol is in any room without looking. They orbit each other through the grieving house and nobody comments on it because nobody in this family ever needed it explained, they have known since the girls were nine that Carol and Zosia are a fixed thing, a binary star, and a decade of silence did not change what the family always plainly saw.

    "You're still the only one who loads it right," Zosia says, at the sink, the second night, watching Carol stack the dishwasher.

    "Your family loads a dishwasher like animals. It's a miracle anyone in this house has clean plates.”

    "Mama loaded it like that.”

    Carol's hands go still in the water. Zosia's go still on the towel. They stand there, shoulder to shoulder, looking at a dishwasher loaded the wrong way by a woman who is gone, and Zosia leans, just slightly, just enough that her arm is against Carol's arm, warm through two black sleeves, and Carol leans back, and neither of them says anything, and the faucet runs.

    That is most of the week. Small things, and grief, and the leaning.

    —

    On the third night Carol finds her on the back step, outside, in the cold, alone, and Carol sits down inside it the way she did when they were nine, and the space closes, and now it is the two of them in it.

    "I keep thinking I have to call her," Zosia says, to the dark yard. "I reach for the phone. I got on a plane to come here and the whole flight I was thinking, I'll call Mama when I land, tell her I landed, she worries when I fly." She turns the cold cup in her hands. "And then I remember. It's like a stair that isn't there. Every hour. The stair that isn't there.”

    "It doesn't stop," Carol says. "I'm not going to lie to you. You know I won't lie to you about this one." She looks at her hands. "My mom's been gone twenty-five years and I still reach for the stair. It gets further apart. It never stops.”

    Zosia turns the cold cup in her hands and does not say anything for a moment.

    "You're the only person who would tell me that straight," she says. "Everyone else says it gets better.”

    "It doesn't get better. It gets further apart." Carol shrugs. "That's the best I've got. I'm sorry it's not nicer.”

    "No," Zosia says. "Don't be sorry. Nicer would be worse." And she leans again, the arm against Carol's arm in the cold, and they sit on the back step of the grieving house, two women in black, shoulder to shoulder, and the seven years go on being gone, and neither of them mentions that either.

    —

    It is the last night before Carol flies back, and they are on the back step again, and Zosia has the tin.

    Carol does not know she has it until she takes it out of her coat. Ten years Zosia has had it, since the wedding, and here it is again in the cold on the back step of the grieving house, the merry-go-round, the horses, the dent, and Carol's whole chest does something at the sight of it.

    "You kept it on you," Carol says.

    "I keep it on me." Zosia turns it in her hands. “I've kept it on me for ten years. It travels. It has been to more countries than most people." She does not look up from it. "I told myself it was Mama. That I was keeping it because it reminded me of being a kid, of the table, of home. That it was about the family." Now she looks up. "It was never about the family, Carol.”

    Carol does not say anything. There is nothing to put in front of that.

    "I'm not going to do the dare again," Zosia says. "The ten years. I'm not- when you fly back tomorrow I'm not starting the clock over. I can't. I tried it for almost ten years and it didn't work, it didn't make any of it stop, it just made me a person who didn't know what city you were in." She turns the tin. "Mama died and the first clear thought I had, before the grief even, the very first one, was Carol will come. Not I hope Carol comes. Just- Carol will come. Because you are the only fixed thing I have ever had." Her voice shakes. "And you came. So I'm not doing the ten years again. I don't have it in me.”

    "Okay," Carol says. Careful. Her heart going hard. "So what do we do?”

    And Zosia looks at her, and there is something in her face Carol has not seen in ten years, something Carol thought was gone, killed, buried in a park, and it is not gone, it is right there, the kid from the parking lot with a milk carton and a plan.

    "I have a dare," Zosia says.

    Carol's whole body goes still.

    "Small one," Zosia says. "Easy. To start." She holds the tin out, both hands, the way she has not held it out to Carol in a decade. "Dare you to call me when you land. Tomorrow. Like Mama used to make us. You land, you call, you tell me you didn't die. That's it." She takes a breath. "And then I'll have a dare for you. And you'll have one for me. And we'll see. We'll just- we'll see how far it goes.”

    Carol looks at the tin held out in the cold.

    She knows what this is. This is not a small dare. There are no small dares, there were never any small dares, the milk carton was not a small dare, it was the beginning of everything. Zosia is not asking Carol to call her when she lands. Zosia is asking whether they are going to do this. Whether the box comes back out. Whether two people who wasted twenty-five years and a wedding and a decade are going to pick the game up off the floor of a dead woman's house and try, one more time, to find their way to each other through the only language they have ever had.

    Carol should say no. Carol is the reason it all burned the first time. Carol drank a marriage to death and wrote a woman into five novels and counted down ten years of a silence she earned. Carol has no business reaching for that tin.

    Carol reaches for the tin.

    She takes it out of Zosia's hands, both of them, and her fingers close over the lid, her mother's lid, warm now from ten years against Zosia's body, warm the way it was the very first morning in a parking lot, and something comes back up through Carol that has been gone so long she forgot it was a thing a person could feel. Color. The world has been gray for ten years, gray and scooped-out and counted, and the second the tin is in her hands it is not gray, it is the back step of a warm loud house in the cold with the only person she has ever loved holding the other side of a game they invented when they were nine, and Carol is, for the first time in a decade, not in automatic. Carol is awake. Carol is alive. It hurts like blood coming back into a dead limb and Carol would not trade it for anything on this earth.

    "Game," Carol says.

    And Zosia smiles, the one with her whole face in it, the one Carol has not seen since before the wedding, and Carol feels it land in her chest like the sun coming up.

    "Call me when you land," Zosia says.

    "I'll call you when I land.”

    —

    Carol flies home the next morning with the tin in her carry-on, wrapped in a sweater, the way it flew the other direction twenty-two years ago, and she calls Zosia from the cab before she calls anyone, before she even gets to the apartment.

    Zosia picks up on the first ring. Because she was waiting by it. The way she has always waited by it.

    "I didn't die," Carol says.

    "You did the dare.”

    "I did the dare. Plane landed. Heart still going. Disappointing, I know.”

    "A little." Carol can hear it, the smile, three thousand miles of it down the line. "Now I owe you one.”

    "You owe me one.”

    "Give me a day to think of something good," Zosia says. "Don't die before I call you.”

    "I'll try to hold off.”

    And Carol hangs up, in the back of a cab, in a city that has been gray for ten years, and she is smiling so hard her face hurts, smiling like an idiot, like a teenager, like a person who has just been handed back her whole life through a phone, and she cannot stop, and she does not try to stop.

    Because they are back in the game.

    And it is better than everything. Better than drugs, better than drink, better than wine-beer-vodka-whiskey, better than the first-glass-fourth-glass-bottle-hidden-bottle-found, better than buzzed-drunk-blackout-gone, all of it, the whole ten-year medicine cabinet Carol built to make the missing stop. Better than sex, better than men, women, strangers, the dark, the phone in Paris, fingers-mouths-hips-teeth, fast-slow-rough-sorry-after, better than being wanted and better than being touched by anyone who was not, was never, will never be Zosia, which Carol now understands was the only thing all of it was ever standing in for.

    Better than Britney and Christina and Destiny's Child and TLC, better than No Doubt and Alanis and Fiona Apple and Garbage and the Cranberries, better than Shakira in the leather pants and Missy in the trash-bag suit and Aaliyah's sunglasses and Beyoncé going solo, better than TRL after school and MTV Spring Break and Jackass and Laguna Beach and the O.C., better than Mean Girls opening weekend and Bring It On and 10 Things I Hate About You and Cruel Intentions and Sarah Michelle Gellar kissing Selma Blair in a park.

    Better than the iPod and the Mini and the Nano and the click wheel and the white headphones on the bus, better than burned CDs and LimeWire and Kazaa and Napster, better than the Razr snapping shut and the Nokia brick and Snake and T9 under the desk and the first camera-phone picture, better than dial-up and the AOL door-opening sound and the little running man, better than AIM away messages and MSN nudges and screen names with x's around them, better than MySpace Top 8 politics and LiveJournal fanfiction.

    Better than low-rise jeans and butterfly clips and body glitter and black eyeliner dragged into the waterline, better than Juicy Tubes and a going-out top and a fake ID and vodka in a water bottle, better than the first Frappuccino and the first hangover, better than kissing a girl in a bathroom while somebody outside screamed the chorus to Mr. Brightside.

    Better than Y2K not ending the world, better than Britney's snake at the VMAs, better than Madonna kissing Britney, better than t.A.T.u, better than But I'm a Cheerleader on a scratched DVD, better than watching Bound too young, better than Bette, and Alice, and Dana from The L Word. 

    Better than freedom. Better than life.

    Zosia is back. The tin is in her bag. The phone is warm in her hand and the smile will not come off her face. And the city, for the first time in ten years, is in color.

    She has one day to wait for the next dare. She does not know yet what it will be. She does not know yet how far it goes.

    ZOSIA

    Here is what nobody tells you about leaving: you do not get to take the person with you, but you do not get to leave them behind either. Zosia tries both. She goes to Milan and she goes to Paris, and Carol comes with her every single time, folded up small in a part of her brain she does not visit if she can help it.

    She does not visit the bed. The radio, the tin on the floor, Carol's mouth, Carol’s tongue, it's just what we say, Zos.

    She does not visit the sidewalk. The condom in her hand. You always said I never could.

    She works. That is what she has instead. She is good at being looked at, and she is paid a lot of money for it. She stands in places full of people whose whole job is to want her, and she gives them the face, and she feels nothing, and that is a skill. Anyone can be wanted. Not everyone can stand in the middle of all that wanting and swallow it and keep her own face still.

    It is a constant sort of performance, but Zosia can do it. She has been doing it for years. She did it first with Carol, the whole back half of their friendship, a constant performance of being a girl who only liked Carol as a friend, and if she could do it for Carol then she can do it for a camera. She has read enough faces to know exactly what hers is doing at all times.

    There is a man, eventually. Henrik.

    He photographs her in Paris and asks her again to dinner and she goes, and she thinks, walking into it: This will make it right. Carol is married now, to an editor named Helen, and if Carol could marry the wrong person then so could she. After this, Zosia will be able to think about Carol's wedding without wanting to put her hand through a wall.

    Henrik is both gentle and good. He looks at her like he cannot believe his luck. There is nothing wrong with Henrik, which is the whole problem, because a thing being wrong would at least be a reason.

    She marries him in France. This will make everything right, she thinks. And then she spends a few years presenting as happy, selling it to her mother and her sister and the magazines and, on the good days, herself. She is unnervingly good at it. She lies, and creates lies to fit the lies, and smooths the whole thing over, because she is a professional. A model is a professional liar. 

    But the marriage does not make it right, and it is a flavor as well as a fact. It is in her coffee in the mornings across the table from a good man. It is in her own face in hotel mirrors. It is in the nights she shuts her eyes under Henrik and it is Carol she finishes to.

    —

    Carol writes books now. Pirate books, the ones she swore she would write when they were nine, and they are extremely successful. And how could Zosia explain it to anyone, that her heart breaks a little every time one comes out, every time she sees Carol's name in a bookshop window, every time a stranger on a plane is reading one two seats over and has no idea.

    She buys every one the week it comes out. She reads them on planes, with her sunglasses on, so the person in the next seat cannot see her reactions.

    She knows in the first book. She isn't stupid, and she's never been stupid about Carol, not for one day of her life. There's a woman in it. A corsair, the one who lives when the rest of them die, and her name is Raban, and she's tall and dark and bored. Zosia gets four pages in and has to put the book down on the tray table and her hand over her mouth.

    Carol made her the one who lives. 

    Oh, Carol. 

    Five books. Zosia reads all five, the week each comes out, alone. Carol writes her love into a corsair and mails it out into the world to strangers who weep over a woman they think is invented, and Zosia receives it in airports, and keeps her mouth shut, every time, even as it takes the skin off her.
