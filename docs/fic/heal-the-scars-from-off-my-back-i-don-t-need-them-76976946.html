    “Life changes fast. Life changes in the instant. You sit down to dinner and life as you know it ends. The question of self-pity.”

    Joan Didion, The Year of Magical Thinking

     

    Carol knows in her heart that she shouldn’t pour herself another drink. The glass is sticky enough to remind her that she’s spilled the amber liquid over her own hands at least three times, and she’s licked them clean as if wasting a single drop of her favourite poison were a crime. If anyone could see her now, they would deem her pathetic, disgusting, hopeless. The fact that she can still feel the stinging shame of knowing that she’s exactly all those things makes her realize that she’s not drunk enough.

    Whiskey sloshes around in the tumbler glass as she tips the bottle towards it with too much force and doesn’t put it down until the liquor reaches the rim. Only then, in her stupor, she’s satisfied, and she lowers her gaze to stare at the almost overflowing glass as she would a sunset, frowning and pursing her lips in solemn focus to determine the best plan of action to make sure she doesn’t spill it again.

    She places her lips on the edge of the glass and slurps, like a kid with a glass of juice, except her tongue tastes acid and her throat stings. She gulps it down, pretending that the bile hitting the back of her mouth isn’t there at all.

    She’s trying to pretend many things aren’t there at all, like the emptiness of her own house, the unread mail piling up on her doorstep, the unanswered texts, the cold, clean pillow next to her dirty flattened one, her clothes on the floor and all the trash scattered around the living room. But most of all, the weirdly pear-shaped bloodstain on the kitchen floor, now dried and crusty.

    Carol can’t bring herself to look at it for more than two seconds, but it weighs on the tiles, like it could warp them to replicate the shape of Helen’s head when she hit the floor, cracking her skull open and bleeding out right there, just the other day. Or week? Weeks?

    It’s hard to tell. Helen’s scent is still everywhere in the house, and as romantic as people make that out to be – smelling someone when they’re not there, when you’re missing them –, it makes Carol want to rip her nose off of her face.

    So, she drinks herself stupid night after night, so much that she’s still drunk by morning but not enough to just stop feeling.

    Once the glass is half empty, she fills it again. She grabs it and stands up from her spot on the floor, leaving the carpet wet with tears and whiskey, and the bottle on the coffee table. She looks at it – ponders her next stupid, senseless move.

    Helen would fucking hate you right now.

    She bites back a lacerating sob, but it escapes her nonetheless, leaving her breathless and shaking as tears start streaming down her face.

    “I miss you. Fuck. I miss you.”

    Words that reach no one’s ears but hers, and as soon as Carol hears herself speaking, she clutches the glass so hard that her knuckles turn white. She gulps the contents of the glass down in one big swig and tosses it on the carpet. It doesn’t shatter, but it falls with a heavy thud that sounds all too familiar.

    She closes her eyes, hands shaking once again. Grabs the bottle of whiskey and walks towards the bloodstain.

    Outside, wolves howl in the distance. Carol lets out a bitter chuckle and decides that they’re doing it for her, to show sympathy for her loss, to participate in her repulsive self-pity ritual as she falls to her knees and tries to scratch at the stain. Some of the dried-up blood comes off and piles up under her fingernail.

    She brings it closer to her eyes to inspect it. This is the last actual trace her wife left behind for her. It’s a morbid thought, the one whispering into her ear to just leave it like this for the foreseeable future.

    It’s not like anyone’s coming to visit, anyway. She mumbles under her breath, entertaining an idea that should be gross, one that any sane person wouldn’t have in a million years, but she’s not a sane person. She wasn’t before, when Helen grounded her and kept her safe and in check, and she certainly cannot be now that she’s alone.

    As she scratches at the stain once more, her head starts spinning. Suddenly she can’t tell if she’s closed her eyes or her brain has just disconnected and she’s drank herself to blindness.

    Who the fuck cares, she mutters, and then slowly slides her legs back to lay on the floor, her head resting next to Helen’s blood.

     

    ---

     

    She dreams of loud sirens and blue lights, of people in dark blue uniforms and nitrile gloves crowding her wife, hooking her up to portable monitors and pushing down on her chest, breaking bones trying to get through the limits of a strained heart, asking her questions she doesn’t register, until the only thing they can do is apologize and mourn her loss.

     

    ---

     

    The sound of the doorbell ringing startles Carol awake. She inhales sharply as her eyes shoot open and her body immediately locks into the tightness of her muscles, sore and aching from sleeping curled up on the floor. It takes her a minute to be able to move, her joints cracking under the weight she’s distributing onto them as she stands up.

    Her head spins as soon as she’s up, and she manages to grab the back of a chair before she trips over her own feet. Her mouth tastes rancid. Her face feels caked up in tears.

    But the buzzer is more insistent now, accompanied by heavy knocks and deep, authoritative voices.

    “Albuquerque Community Safety responders here! Miss Sturka? Can you hear us?”

    What the fuck?

    Carol tries to peek behind the drawn curtain and notices a few neighbours outside, watching the scene like vultures looking for some juicy bits to snack on, and she hates to give them the satisfaction of filling their metaphorical bellies with the image of a mentally ill alcoholic flipping off good, honest citizens who are just trying to help.  She’s been on the receiving end of pity for so long in her life that it’s not even a bother now, it’s just a matter of who she is. Pitiful, helpless, sad Carol.

    Still, it’s a slap she’s not willing to feel, because now more than ever she actually deserves it, and when things get real like that, that’s when Carol knows she has to run away from all the help people try to give her, because she can’t be responsible for fucking up, she can’t be responsible for being the way she is, she just can’t deal with people’s disappointment once they understand she’s beyond saving.

    And yet, she’s walking to the door. But before she can reach it, her stomach turns and gives up on her, pushing all the remnants of last night on the floor in front of her. Some of it sticks to the front of her shirt, some hits her bare feet. She feels disgusting. The room smells disgusting.

    I should just pretend that I’m dead.

    “Miss Sturka! If you can hear us, please, open the door! We just want to make sure you’re alright!”

    The knocking becomes unbearable, ringing into her head with a low buzz that fuels the pain festering behind her eyes.

    Fuck it.

    She wipes her mouth with the back of her hand – she’s already gross beyond repair, so, why not? – and resumes her walk to get to the entrance. Except, she steps right into the puddle of vomit. Her ankle twists in a painfully uncomfortable way, and something snaps as she slips and hits the floor, landing on her side.

    “Fuck!” She yells, more in anger than hurt, and the knocking on her hardwood door stops for a few seconds.

    “Miss Sturka! We can hear you! Are you in need of assistance?”

    Carol sighs. She tries to stand but her ankle gives out and a sharp pain shoots through her body, sending chills down her spine and tears into her eyes. Her hand shakes as she brushes tentative fingers against the already swelling flesh. She bites back a string of profanities.

    She hears some shuffling from outside, and thinks the responders might actually break in.

    Are they even allowed do that?, she wonders, as she crawls towards the door, pushing her weight forward with her hands, her feet limply dragging across the dirty floor. Once she’s there, she rests her back against the wall and reaches up for the doorknob.

    She hesitates a few seconds before pulling it down and opening the door, letting the sun and fresh air come in after days of being shut in. She breathes deep, but it’s hardly a satisfying breath, because she can’t smell anything but her own shame and grossness.

    If the people barging in notice the disgusting state of her place – and they do – they pretend not to. She’s glad for that. Pretending things are fine is something Carol can appreciate in herself and others.

    One of the responders kneels beside her feet and takes in the state of her even more swollen ankle.

    Carol sheepishly points at it.

    “It’s not that bad, right?”

     

    ---

     

    Turns out that slipping on a puddle of your own vomit and breaking your ankle is, indeed, that bad. Carol learns it the hard way as her ankle is being wrapped tightly in a stockinette to prepare it for the cast she’ll have to wear for at least a month. She hisses and grimaces, letting the consequences of her own actions befall her, and tries to focus on signing several forms that are being handed to her.

    Once the entire process is over, she’s left alone in the emergency room.

    It takes her a few minutes of staring at medical equipment and fleeting scrubs before she realizes that she’s sitting in the very room where her wife was legally pronounced dead.

    She glances at the clock. Of course, it’s not the same time. Helen died in the evening, the most ordinary evening ever, after what the doctors had described as a sudden aneurysm rupture. They asked her if she had complained about headaches lately, and Carol was too numbed out to blame herself for dismissing them and telling her to take an Advil. She couldn’t have known, right?

    But she could’ve been quick enough to catch her before the fall, had she not been focused on fixing herself a nice little drink before dinner. She could’ve started their car and driven her to the hospital as fast as possible, had she not already taken a couple sips of that fucking drink that couldn’t really harm anyone, right?

    Carol’s breath grows ragged and heavy. A veil of tears crowds her eyes, and she can’t really see anything anymore. She wipes at them with the back of her hands – still sticky from before – but they well up again, relentlessly pushing to fall.

    So, she lets them. Who cares if someone sees her.

    She sobs and gasps for air as images from that night come up in her mind’s eye, until she feels a hand on her shoulder and a thumb gently caressing her.

    She flinches away like a cat that’s been approached too incautiously.

    “I’m sorry. I didn’t mean to startle you.”

    Carol looks up and there’s a woman towering over her, pulling her hand back as if she’d just gotten burned.

    She wonders if all the alcohol she’s ingested the night before has damaged her brain, because she can’t come up with anything to say before the woman speaks up again.

    “You’re Carol, right? I’m Zosia. I hear you’ve had quite a day.”

    Carol scoffs, trying to conceal the cracks in her voice.

    “And it’s not even noon yet.”

    When Carol leaves the hospital, wobbly on crutches and permanently scarred by the embarrassment of looking and smelling like she’s been on an all-inclusive junkyard vacation, there’s no one out there waiting for her, of course.

    Helen used to drive her around every time she’d hit the bottle, which was every day when they were on her book tours, and most days when they weren’t. Sometimes Carol would try to persuade her wife to let her take the wheel, saying that she’d only have a beer and she’d be completely fine to drive, but Helen was adamant in her decisions and knew that Carol just didn’t have it in her to stop after one drink. And then, one night, Carol went and proved her right, crashed their car into a streetlight, got herself a DUI charge, a license suspension, a minor concussion and Helen’s look of defeat and disappointment, which only made her wish she’d crashed into a concrete wall instead.

    The breathalyzer lock in their car always reminded her of the days following the accident, when Carol felt like Helen could barely look at her, but she never asked whether it was out of worry or frustration. A mix of both, perhaps, which was old news to her.

    Still, for a while the air between them felt heavy, suffocating, and Carol struggled to say that she was sorry. She figured it was obvious, that Helen could read it between the lines of her effort in attending counselling sessions and passing up on drinks with her editor after turning in the final draft of Bitter Chrysalis but, of course, that was just an assumption, or rather an excuse she gave herself to avoid speaking of her fuckups altogether.

    Avoidance had always been one of Carol’s best talents. Even now, barely able to stand upright on the curb with no means of going back home, no phone to call for an Uber but also no money to pay for it, she’s avoiding the woman who spoke to her so kindly just a few minutes ago. Too kindly, even. The type of sickening kindness that often flows into condescension, something that makes Carol’s blood boil with deep-seated anger, but also fear, like prey that’s being played with before the mauling.

     

    ***

     

    “May I?” the woman asks, pointing at the space on the bed next to where she’s sitting.

    “Sure.” Carol shrugs, and the mattress dips slightly under the newfound weight. There’s a moment of silence, a beat where Carol feels too gross to be sitting next to someone else, let alone someone who looks like a supermodel fresh out of a fashion show or something.

    “How are you feeling?” Her voice sounds weirdly concerned. Calculated, almost rehearsed. It makes Carol want to get up and run.

    “Peachy.”

    Zosia doesn’t say anything else, for a while. It’s awkward, unnecessary and beyond weird. Carol starts picking nervously at her cuticles, but stops immediately at the sight of her dirty nails. She squeezes her hands into tight fists and then notices the gesture has caught Zosia’s attention.  

    “Do you have someone you can call to come get you?” She looks away and then straight into Carol’s eyes, a sympathetic smile forming on her face, sincere worry into her brows.

    Carol finds it ridiculous, annoying, stupid. But holding contact with those dark, gentle eyes makes her feel uneasy.

    She focuses once again on her hands, toying with her wedding ring that she just couldn’t take off, and wonders whether this woman is thinking of a tired husband back home that has to deal with the antics of a screw-up wife.

    Then, irritation surges into her and comes out before she can stop it.

    “I’m sorry, are you a doctor?”

    Zosia flinches ever so slightly, but her smile doesn’t falter. She sighs, and it sounds like a mother who’s about to unleash the overwhelming sweetness of gentle parenting onto her kid. But what does Carol even know about that? Her mother was cruel to her. It would be easier, now, to deal with someone cursing and insulting her, belittling and dismissing her thoughts and feelings. Yet, this stranger sounds so concerned about her that the only thing it makes her think of is Helen.

    The only difference is that Helen wouldn’t smile, but she’d still take her hands into hers to stop her from picking at her skin. She’d take her home, help her get cleaned up, ease her into a fresh set of clothes. She’d do it quietly, and then tell Carol to stay hydrated and eat something when she feels like it. She wouldn’t cook for her, because she’d have to show her she’s mad, somehow. But she’d always, undisputedly help her pick up the pieces.

    “No, but Dr. Martinez told me –”

    Carol doesn’t let her finish. She knows where this is going. It clicks as soon as Zosia confirms that she isn’t a doctor, because why else would a well-dressed, well-mannered stranger concern herself with the breakdown of a drunkard – with puke on her shirt and a broken ankle – whose demeanor screams “I’m a danger to myself and others”.

    “Then get the fuck out of my face.”

    Carol tries to get up, overly trusting her ability to keep balance on only one foot after her night-long bender. She staggers, and Zosia is quick to stand up and catch her, supporting her weight, one hand grabbing her forearm, the other on Carol’s hip. It stays there for a split second, but Carol is too aware of its warmth, so much that it almost burns.

    She’s also aware of the fact that this is the first time in weeks that someone has touched her (except for the doctors, but they were just doing their job). A fleeting, sudden touch, that is over just as quickly as it happened.  

    Carol thinks she needs to check whether that hand has left a dent on her hip, because the sensation lingers and makes it hard to look and sound angry and unapproachable. She feels like a wet kitten.

    Zosia remains in her personal space, still supporting her by the arm. She smells good, like tangerine, jasmine and fresh clothes, and it is, somehow, calming.

    Before she speaks, she steps back a little.

    “Carol, please, if you could just hear me out,” and when she understands Carol won’t interrupt her, she keeps going.

    “Dr. Martinez thought you could use someone to talk to, that’s it.”

    Carol scoffs.

    “Oh, wow. And he got that from my x-rays? Dude’s potential is wasted here. He should be working as a fortune-teller.”

    Zosia’s grip on her arm loosens, her eyes scrutinizing Carol’s face, maybe in search of an opening for a connection, something honest.

    “No, but I think he’s right.”

    “And why should I care about what you think?”

    “You don’t have to.”

    Those meek words ignite something else in Carol. The way Zosia makes it sound like it’s all in her hands, that she has a choice to ignore her, makes her want to prove her that she can do exactly that, and that she doesn’t need anyone to say so.

    “I know that.”

    They keep staring at each other. Carol is confrontational, hostile. Zosia is patient, mild-mannered, leaving an opening for the scared deer to run off into the woods, but still trying to reach for it.

    “And you don’t need to be a fortune-teller to know when someone’s struggling.”

    Here it is. Carol could feel the lecture coming from a mile away, and the urge to run for cover grows stronger.

    “Yeah, right, but you don’t know shit about me.”

    “I know you came in heavily intoxicated and injured after someone called a wellness check on you, for one.”

    That’s more like it. Carol doesn’t want kindness, patience. She wants someone to spell out for her what a fuck up she is, and although this still feels too controlled and soft, it’s a start.

    She shrugs, weighing how her next words can breach the façade of graciousness to the point that this woman flips her off and pushes her away, leaving her alone with her grievances.

    “People should just mind their fucking business. I wasn’t hurting anyone.”

    “Except yourself.”

    Carol stays silent, her lips tightly sealed into a thin line curling downwards, and shakes her head. She doesn’t want to tell this stranger that that was the point.

    “I was just having fun. Got a little carried away. That doesn’t mean I’m, uh, struggling.” She air-quotes the last word, mimicking Zosia’s tolerant, soft inflection to show her just how ridiculous she sounds.

    Zosia sighs, again. It sounds like a way to decompress, to ponder and avoid snapping. She does look like someone who counts to ten before she speaks.

    “Carol, you don’t want to talk to me, I get it. But I’ve been you, I know what it’s like. I just want you to know that –”

    “See, that’s the thing I hate about you fucking preachers,” Carol narrows her eyes, as if she’s studying an elusive subject (one that, actually, she recognizes all too well). “You all go around telling others that they can get better, that they deserve to get better, that people care, and you do it with those compassionate smiles and pious words and then you all get in your stupid circles in some room that smells like ass and burnt coffee and tell your sob stories as if that’s what it takes to stop feeling like shit all the time.”

    She’s almost yelling, now, pointing her finger at Zosia, spitting her accusations as if she’s the one who’s made her entire life crumble to pieces.

    “I’ll tell you what. A drink sounds so much better than all this bullshit.”

    Carol drops her hand, slightly out of breath. She holds Zosia’s gaze and waits for a reaction, hopes for something like “yeah, fuck you and your miserable life”, but doesn’t get anything.

    Instead, Zosia nods.

    “I know it does. There are so many things that sound better than that. I could name you all the ones I’ve shot up myself. Would that make you feel better?”

    Carol blinks.

    Shakes her head.

    “Just leave me alone, please.” Her voice cracks, her eyes start welling up.

    Zosia, still holding Carol’s arm, tries brushing her thumb against her skin. It does something strange to Carol. She’s angry, she wants to push this woman away. But comfort seeps into her pores as she caresses her, and for a moment she doesn’t want to let go, indulging in the idea that someone besides Helen might give a shit about her.  She’s about to say something when Dr. Martinez comes up behind Zosia and makes the bubble burst.

    “Miss Sturka, you’re all set,” he says, handing her some papers and resting a pair of old crutches against the bed, “these should be enough to get you back on your feet. No pun intended.”

    Despite saying that, he chuckles and waits for a split second for them to do the same. Neither Carol nor Zosia do. He looks at both of them, his eyes moving like tennis balls on a court, assessing the situation.

    Carol is the first to avert her gaze, and even so, she feels Zosia’s still on her.

    “Thanks, doc.”

    She listens to Dr. Martinez instructing her about basic cast care, and things to look out for during the healing process, nodding every now and then like a diligent student taking mental notes.

    “It’s all in those papers, anyway. Look out for numbness and tingling, and make sure to keep the cast dry. And, of course,” he subtly glances at Zosia for an instant, then back to Carol, “no drinking while medicating.”

    Carol wants to roll her eyes, but manages to politely carry the exchange to its end, when Dr. Martinez takes his leave.

    Zosia clears her throat awkwardly and, before letting go of Carol’s arm, she hands her one crutch to help her keep her balance. Carol grabs it and pulls herself free from Zosia’s hold, but she stays close in case she trips.

    “You got this? Yeah, uhm, careful –”

    “I’m fine. This is not my first rodeo.”

    Zosia hums, a flash of curiosity in her furrowed brows. Though she doesn’t ask, Carol wonders what she’s thinking. Has she drawn the conclusion that she makes a habit out of drinking, passing out and breaking bones?

    “I broke my leg in high school. P.E. wasn’t exactly my strongest subject.”

    Zosia offers her a smile and a hum. Besides that, she lets the silence hang heavy between them. Moments before, Carol was unnecessarily yelling at her and now she’s supposed to just up and leave? Say “thank you for trying, goodbye”? It feels weird. The hefty knot twisting in her stomach feels weird.

    But once she gets home, she can just forget any of this ever happened.

    She steadies herself on both crutches, appealing to young Carol’s muscle memory. It’s definitely more difficult than she remembered.

    She looks at Zosia one more time, taking in her deep dark eyes, her sculpted cheekbones. Her defined jaw. Her lips curling into a soft, sort of defeated smile. Carol finds that it’s a little harder to take her eyes off of it.

    “Huh. Alright, then. Bye.”

    She makes her way past Zosia, eyeing the exit, yearning for her escape from this humbling predicament in this godforsaken place.

    “Carol, wait,” Zosia rests her hand on her arm, again. Carol hates that she revels in the intimacy of that foreign touch.

    “Are you sure you’re good to go? Is someone coming to get you?”

    She nods, feigning confidence.

    “I’m fine, yeah. Don’t worry about it.”

    She decides she doesn’t want to give her a chance to push further and starts hobbling away.

    She ends up sitting for thirty minutes on a bench at the bus stop where, apparently, no buses stop.

    Not that she’d be able to pay for a ticket, but she was hoping some kind driver would take pity on her and let her on regardless.

    It’s a rather sunny day and, after spending weeks holed up inside her house with barely any light at all, feeling warm rays wash over her is like a panacea for all ailments. She chuckles thinking of tweets and web articles affirming the healing power of a walk over crippling depression. She breathes in, deeply as if pretending to express gratitude for the ability to do such mundane things. Maybe if she pretends hard enough, it could actually become true.

    The hospital behind her twists its silhouette along with the movements of the sun, and time, like all things, passes.

     

    ***

     

    After a while, Carol decides that her only chance is to slowly, very, very slowly start walking back home. It’s a ridiculous thought, since she lives on the outskirts of the West Mesa, and it’s a twenty-minute drive from there to the hospital. There are no other alternatives.

    Settled in her resolve, she hops and limps, but painkillers – the good kind – are wearing off and her ankle is starting to hurt again.

    She only makes about four or five steps before she hears a car slow down behind her. When she turns, Zosia is there, in an old blue Toyota Corolla that feels oddly inadequate for someone so – and Carol cringes at the juvenile adjective she comes up with – cool.

    “Carol,” she calls out, rolling down the window, then tries for a smirk. “Enjoying your walk?”

    There is a charming quality to the way her name rolls off of Zosia’s tongue and drips into her ear, and it makes her want to scream into her face, to tell her to stop saying it and carry on as if she never knew she existed, just like a few hours before. Because the only person who could ever use her name like that – while teasing, caring, supporting, confronting – was Helen. And this woman, this stranger, has been too keen on robbing her of that.

    Still, Carol walks to the door and opens it without asking for permission. Something about need over want.

     

    ***

     

    The ride home is silent, but not uncomfortable. Zosia doesn’t take advantage of the confined space with no exit routes, and doesn’t push Carol to tell her anything about herself.

    Unfortunately, Carol unwillingly shares that she’s starving when her stomach grumbles and it seems to resonate inside the car. She realizes that she hasn’t eaten in more than 24 hours. Still, she remains silent.

    Zosia keeps her eyes on the road and if she’s heard her, she doesn’t say anything.

     

    ***

     

    “Do you mind if we make a quick stop?”

    Zosia asks after a couple minutes, pulling over without actually waiting for an answer. Carol shrugs carelessly.

    She turns off the ignition and steps out of the car, briskly walking towards the entrance of a convenience store.

    Waiting in someone else’s car makes Carol feel like a restless, abandoned dog. She looks around, drums her fingers on the plastic dashboard, pulls down the visor, opens and closes the glove compartment compulsively. There’s nothing there besides Zosia’s car registration, a few pens, some crumpled paper and an empty pack of American Spirit cigarettes.

    Eventually, she settles for playing a random beat on the plastic and humming meaningless words.

    A few minutes later, Zosia’s back in the driver’s seat. Without a word, she puts a new pack of American Spirits in the door compartment. Then, she hands Carol a bottle of water and a bag of nacho cheese Doritos, while she sucks on an orange candy stick with white twirls all over. She moves it to the side of her mouth and smiles as Carol gingerly takes the offerings.

    “Uhm. Thanks.”

    “No problem.”

     

    ***

     

    The chips are enough to keep her from fainting, even though she hates nacho cheese. Still, beggars can’t be choosers, and while she’s a hater at heart, kind gestures like this from strangers almost never happen, which is why she has to be grateful. She doesn’t necessarily want to feel like she owes something to this woman, but small debts are starting to pile up.

    “I didn’t peg you for a smoker.” Carol blurts out mindlessly, wondering why she would even say something like that to a woman whose name is the only thing she knows.

    “Really, huh?” Zosia smirks, eyes still fixed on the road ahead. “How come?”

    “I don’t know. You seem so… classy.”

    Zosia clicks her tongue and then raises a finger upwards in a “Eureka!” kind of way.

    Carol sighs loudly, defeated. It’s just for this once. She’ll buy her a cup of coffee as a thank you and then forget all about her.

    Carol puts the phone on speaker on the sink while she brushes her teeth – nervously fast, and it makes her gums bleed a little. It rings once, twice, three times. She spits the toothpaste just in time to say something as Zosia’s puzzled voice echoes in her bathroom.

    “Carol?”

    She swallows the lump in her throat, but it still feels like a rock is stuck there when she speaks.

    “Zosia, hi. Sorry for calling like this –” She hears someone in the background of Zosia’s line call her name. “Uh, are you busy? I – sorry, I should’ve asked before calling –”

    “Carol, hey. It’s okay, really. Actually, I’m glad you called.” Her voice is warm and soft, and Carol can feel the smile in her words. It tugs at something in her chest, and pulls the corner of her lips upwards without her permission.

    “Oh. Okay, good, that’s – that’s good.”

    A pause.

    “Is everything alright?”

    “Yeah, yeah. I just – I need a favor.”

    “Oh? Well, what is it?”

    “It’s totally fine if you can’t, of course, and I don’t expect you to, I mean you barely know me and I would understand if you thought it’s weird, all things considered –”

    Zosia chuckles lightly. It’s bubbly, sweet, delicate.

    “Do you always do this?”

    “Do what?”

    “Jump to conclusions?”

    Carol exhales, taking Zosia’s interruption as her clue to stop rambling and just ask for what she needs.

    “Sorry. I guess I do that sometimes.”

    “Yeah, I figured. But seriously, what’s up?”

    “Huh… I need a ride.”

    Zosia doesn’t answer right away, and that makes Carol feel like a fool. She wonders what exactly has compelled her to think that she could send weird texts and then ask for favors to a stranger. And also, when has she become so socially inept? Maybe it’s the months of isolation, but even so, she doesn’t remember being this incapable of managing a conversation. She’s about to say something like nevermind, I’ll figure it out some other way, when Zosia speaks.

    “That’s it?”

     

    *

     

    Zosia pulls into the driveway of her house exactly ten minutes after saying that she’d be happy to help. As soon as she sees Carol walk out, with crutches and a small duffel bag slung on her shoulder, she gets out of her car and makes her way to her.

    “Hey,” she waves, and then her hand reaches for the bag strap. “Here, let me –”

    Carol recoils from the contact as soon as Zosia’s fingers graze her shoulder, but tries to give her a reassuring smile.

    “I got this, thanks.”

    Zosia doesn’t argue with that, and just nods. The air between them hangs a bit heavy, and Carol feels as if she’s guilty of something. Standing here, looking at this woman, noticing she isn’t wearing any make up and is sporting light blue scrubs that definitely aren’t her everyday clothes for hanging out with random people, Carol realizes that maybe Zosia had something more important to do than being her chauffeur for the day.

    But that’s not where her guilt comes from. That’s got to do with the giddy anticipation that Carol felt in her legs while she was waiting to be picked up. She tries to chalk it up to the fact that this is her first actual human interaction in weeks, but it’s a weak counterpoint against the way her chest – and cheeks – warm up under Zosia’s gaze, under her attention.

    Zosia clears her throat and does a weird finger gun gesture towards the car. Carol is incredibly endeared by the sudden show of awkwardness, and wishes she could slap herself for it.

    “Shall we?”

     

     

    The ride is, once again, silent for the most part, except for Tears for Fears playing in the background. Carol bops her head to the rhythm of Everybody Wants to Rule the World, and tries to focus on anything but the woman currently driving her around. If she were to think about it for more than a second, she’d end up conjuring images of Helen doing the same for her every day, and that would be the breaking point between sanity and Carol bashing her head into the windshield.

    Zosia stops at a red light.

    “So,” she starts, and Carol groans in her head. Here goes. “I have to say, I was a little surprised to hear from you, you know.”

    “Yeah, sorry –”

    “Stop apologizing,” Carol’s eyebrows shoot up. “I’m just saying it was a nice surprise.”

    “Right. But you clearly had other stuff to do,” she gestures to her clothing. “So, I’m sorry for the trouble.”

    “Not a big deal,” Zosia says, focused on the traffic light that seems to be taking forever to turn green. “I asked a friend to cover a few hours for me at work.” She turns to look at Carol, who wishes she could avert her gaze quick enough to avoid meeting hers. But she can’t, and Zosia gives her a reassuring, warm smile. The kindness infused in it is starting to become less and less annoying – though Carol wishes she could just get mad at it – and gives way to a familiarity that simmers in her chest. It does make the ache feel all the more evident, but it’s also soothing it.

    It’s confusing and disruptive, and it’s like being stuck in the middle of a cornfield with one side burning and the other being mowed down.

    The light turns green, and Carol sighs in relief.

    “I bet you can’t wait to get that thing off your leg, right?” She continues, and Carol is grateful that she knows when to drop a topic.

    “God, you have no idea.” Carol groans, and lets the back of her head hit the headrest softly, feigning exhaustion.

    “Oh, no, I do. I was a little reckless as a kid,” Zosia shrugs her shoulders casually. “I think I spent most of middle school in a cast.”

    “Wow,” Carol chuckles. She hasn’t heard herself make that sound in a long time, and it almost startles her how naturally it comes out. “Now I kind of want to know what you were doing.”

    “Well, nothing smart, really. When I was ten, I tried jumping off a porch banister and broke my elbow,” Zosia keeps her hands on the steering wheel, but lifts one finger as if starting a count.  “Then, this one time, I tried climbing up a tree to get my football back. My asshole neighbour had thrown it up there because he sucked and I didn’t and I sort of made fun of him for that. Oh, and at some point, I think I was twelve, I was obsessed with this song, Hell on Wheels, and asked for some roller skates for Christmas. I wasn’t very good with those.”

    “Ouch. Did you get it back, the football?”

    “Yeah. But then I couldn’t play for a couple months, and by the time my legs had fully healed, I was already onto my next interest.”

    “I’m sorry, legs? As in, both of them?!”

    Zosia nods like it’s nothing. “It was a pretty tall tree.”

    “Woah. Sometimes I wonder how most of us have survived our childhoods.”

    “Sheer luck and stubborn will, I guess.”

    Carol huffs a quiet laugh. That tracks, she thinks. They fall in a comfortable silence that lasts until Zosia pulls over into the hospital’s parking lot and stops the car.

    “There you go.” Zosia flashes a charming smirk and again, Carol wants to bash her head into the window, splitting it open to pull out one by one every weird thought she’s having. Instead, she responds with a sheepish smile and a quiet “thank you”.

    “I’ll wait here, is that okay?”

    “Wha – No, really, you don’t have to.”

    “What are you going to do, then, steal a car on your way back?” She raises an eyebrow questioningly.

    Carol considers Zosia’s proposition.

    “Yeah, I think I could pull off some grand theft auto.”

    “Come on. I’m serious, I don’t mind waiting.”

    “Don’t you have to go back to work?”

    “I have time.”

    “I could just walk home, you know.”

    “Carol.” Zosia looks at her with a serious, no-bullshit demeanor. “That’s just dumb.”

    Carol sighs, but in the end she heads to the entrance of the hospital while Zosia remains in the car. She turns around before walking in, and finds Zosia’s gaze still glued to her.

     

    *

     

    When Carol finally places her cast-less foot on the ground, it feels like she might break it again. It’s swollen, stiff and overall gross and itchy, with flaky skin clinging there as if she were a moulting snake, and it doesn’t give her any sense of trust in the fact that she won’t fall face first on the floor once she starts walking. Dr. Martinez gives her all the needed reassurance, but she’s still uncertain as she moves her first steps without aid in weeks.

    She leaves the hospital holding onto a crutch for dear life, her grip so tight she’s afraid it might break, because the last thing she needs is to hug the floor right in front of Zosia. She’s already made herself look ridiculous enough.

    And Zosia, as expected, is still there waiting for her. Outside, leaning on the hood of her car, she’s absentmindedly bringing a cigarette to her mouth, basking in the warm May sun. Carol has never seen skin glow under it the way Zosia’s does. And she shouldn’t even be noticing how tall she is, yet her eyes trail up her legs, covered in the most unflattering piece of clothing ever, but that does very little to not make her look like she’s off to walk the runaway.

    The simmering ache in her chest– the reminder that none of this has to mean something, that she doesn’t need nor deserves it to be – grows sharper and tighter, and feeling it recoil around her heart is a relief. But then Zosia notices her and makes a face – concern in the rise of her eyebrows, sympathy in the curve of her lips. She puts out her cigarette (she doesn’t even toss it on the ground, and Carol thinks of Helen doing exactly the same thing), and heads towards her, dropping the stub in a trashcan on the curb.

    “Hey,” her voice is soft, though there’s a tiny rasp there, and it catches Carol’s full attention. “Easy, easy.”

    Her grip on the crutch eases up, her palm feels sweaty. Zosia gets close, too close, a hand grazing gently Carol’s elbow, offering support, and guiding her back to her car. Carol can smell the smoke on her, mixed with tangerine and jasmine, just like the first time. She hates that she remembered.

    Still, she moves with the current that takes them to Zosia’s Toyota, her interior turmoil too exhausting to give heed to, now. She just wants to go home and pass out drunk on the couch.

    When Zosia opens the door for her, Carol wishes she could just whack her on the shins with her crutch, which is a cruel thought, considering young Zosia’s unfortunate experience with broken limbs. The gesture makes her unreasonably mad, but the hold anger has on her stomach softens with something akin to tenderness the minute Carol meets Zosia’s eyes. Something stirs in her chest, but she’d rather die right now than acknowledge the way her heart picks up at the maintained contact.

    Zosia’s eyes are scanning, seeing into her, and Carol is afraid that she might just be reading her thoughts, by the way they travel across her face. She’s about to say something when a car honking startles them.

    “Hey! Are you moving or what?” A bald man shouts, his head poking out of his car window. His voice is way too loud, but he looks too ridiculous to be intimidating.

    Zosia looks at him for a split second, like one would do to a fly before swatting it away, and turns back to Carol. She doesn’t pay him any more attention – even when he keeps honking – and stays there, holding the door open for Carol to comfortably get in the car, and closes it. Then, she circles her Toyota, opens the driver’s side door, flips the man off and gets in, spewing an insult under her breath.

    Carol is surprised at Zosia’s switch in demeanor. The first thought she has is one of mortification, in the sense that if she’d been the one on the receiving end of Zosia’s dismissive look, she would’ve wished to disappear on the spot. The second is one of deeper shame, one that she’ll have to scrub away from her brain later, by dint of drinking until her liver gives out – because reflecting on the attractiveness of it all would be a second admission of faults, and that can’t be allowed.

     

    The ride back doesn’t feel so quiet anymore. Carol’s riddled with thoughts of guilt, but at the same time she’s dreading the pit she’ll fall into once she gets home. It’s hard to explain, because that’s something she actively chooses to do every day to the point that it’s become inevitable. Being alone, drinking, ignoring the rest of the world moving on as if the most important person for her hasn’t disappeared from it, those are the only things she knows how to do, now. And yet, today, something about it scares her, because there are new thoughts and feelings in her mind that are best kept dormant, and she doubts that getting hammered will do that.

    For the first time in a while, Carol isn’t so sure any more about downing half a bottle of vodka.

    Zosia turns on the radio, and it fills the charged silence. Duran Duran are singing about girls on film, and she hums the tune, drumming on the steering wheel with her fingers. Carol looks at them for a minute too long.

    “So,” Zosia says, dragging the “o” with uncertainty that clashes against the assertiveness from minutes ago, “any plans now? I mean, now that your leg’s healed.”

    Carol doesn’t think about her answer.

    “Not really. I’ll probably just get groceries at Sprouts.”

    “Oh. Cool.”

    Carol narrows her eyes and scans Zosia’s adamant face for clues that she might think she’s pathetic. Before she finds any, she decides to give them freely.

    “Nah. It’s just, it doesn’t really change anything. I just get to walk around without these things.” She points to the crutches, but Zosia doesn’t look away from the road, offering a curt nod.

    “Well, that’s something, at least.”

    “I guess.”

    Zosia stops at a red light. As she does, the phone in her bag starts ringing, and she lunges towards the backseat to reach for it, her head almost touching Carol’s shoulder. She gives Carol an apologetic look, as if that call is somehow disrupting their … whatever, then answers the phone.

    “Hey, Manny. What’s up?”

    Carol can hear the faint sounds of someone speaking English mixed with some Spanish, but can’t really make out the words. She rests her forehead against the window and focuses on the cat trying to cross the road in order to avoid accidental eavesdropping.

    “No, it’s fine. Yeah. I mean, sounds fair. You take mine today, I’ll cover yours tomorrow. Sure, it’s no problem at all. Just make sure to put it in the schedule. Alright. Bye.”

    Zosia ends the call just in time for the light to turn green. She leaves the phone on her thigh.

    “Everything alright?” Carol asks. It seems like the obvious thing to do.

    “Yeah. Actually, looks like I just got the rest of the day off.”

    “Oh, great.”

    Zosia nods, her lips sealed in a tight line. It brings out dimples Carol had tried not to notice before. She suddenly regrets replying with a less-than-thrilled inflection, wishes she could take it back and say it better. She hates the back and forth her mind’s pushing her into – trying to kill a conversation, but wanting it to keep going.

    “Carol?” Zosia breaks the silence after a couple minutes, her voice thinly veiled in nervousness.

    “Yeah?”

    “Do you want to go grab a coffee?”

     

     

     

    They end up sitting opposite each other in a diner booth, and Carol can’t help but fidget in her seat, her hands reaching for the salt and pepper shakers on the table, moving them around as if they were the most interesting objects in the world. She doesn’t look at Zosia, but she can feel the weight of her attention anyways, patient, unhurried. Even slightly amused.

    Instead, she prefers glancing around to avoid meeting Zosia’s gaze. It’s a regular diner, uncomplicated neutral land where they can finally sever whatever connection they have, drink some watered-down coffee, and then say goodbye, never to see each other again.

    There’s a cop sitting at the counter, having a very late breakfast. In the corner behind Zosia there’s a couple, a bald man and a grey-haired woman, and they’re sitting side by side with the ease and confidence of people that have spent half a century basking in each other’s company. They laugh, a childlike amusement on their faces when the waitress serves them a single milkshake with cream on top and two straws.

    Carol looks away and feels her throat close up, the way it does when she chokes down a sob. She focuses so hard on pushing it all back down, trying to erase the casual intimacy she’s just intruded into, that she doesn’t notice the waitress pouring coffee in the mug she’s gripping. Zosia thanks the woman, and her voice brings her back into the room, grounding her to the moment.

    She finally glances at Zosia, who’s sipping her coffee with a slight frown, as if she’s not really enjoying it.

    “We look like we just broke out of a mental hospital.” Carol says, lifting the steaming mug to her lips.

    “No, you do,” Zosia quirks an eyebrow, “I just look like someone who helped you out.”

    “Yeah, you’re right,” Carol nods, then gestures vaguely at Zosia’s clothes. “So, are you, like, a nurse?” She hesitates, then adds, “Because I remember yelling at you at the hospital when you told me you weren’t a doctor, so… yeah.”

    Zosia chuckles softly at the memory, then shrugs as she answers.

    “Sort of. I’m a care assistant in a nursing home.”

    “Oh. That’s nice.”

    “Yeah, it is. I like it. It’s… rewarding, being there for people. And also, incredibly exhausting.” She lets out a breathy laugh, as if even the thought of her job tires her out.

    “I can imagine,” Carol nods, sympathetic, although it’s not true. She can’t imagine what it’s like to take care of other people to the point of exhaustion, because she’s always been on the other side of that relationship. Always the one being cared for, the one to worry about.

    “What about you, Carol? What do you do?”

    “Oh, me? Uh. I’m a writer.”

    Zosia’s eyebrows lift, just slightly – not in disbelief, but in quiet curiosity.

    “Really?”

    Carol swears she hears some sort of admiration in the question, and she suddenly feels incredibly aware of how pompous it sounds when she says it out loud. She hides half of her face behind the mug.

    “Yeah. Books. I write books.”

    “That’s cool,” Zosia says easily, then takes another sip of her coffee and grimaces. “This is terrible.”

    Carol hums in agreement, and there’s a beat of silence where they both focus on downing most of the contents of their mugs.

    “So, do you like it?” Zosia says, after a few minutes.

    The question catches Carol off guard, because normally she would say that yes, she loves it. She doesn’t think she’d be capable of doing anything else, really. And yet, she’s not sure what to say. She would’ve preferred being asked about what she writes, the classic “anything I might’ve read?”, to which she’d usually respond by downplaying her actually successful series, making it sound accidental, like something that had happened to her rather than something she’d built.

    Carol exhales through her nose and lowers the mug.

    “I don’t know,” she says finally. “I did. I think I still do. It’s just… complicated.”

    Zosia nods, like that makes perfect sense.

    “Most things worth doing are,” she says.

    Carol studies the way Zosia’s fingers curl around the chipped ceramic, steady and unhurried, as if time is inconsequential for her. She finds that sitting in front of someone so undemanding isn’t the worst feeling in the world. It actually makes it easier to talk. Like there’s no judgement because there’s no expectation.

    “But I get that,” Zosia continues. “As much as I love my job, some days I get home and just stare at the wall for, like, an hour.”

    Carol huffs a quiet laugh before she can stop herself. She feels some of the tension that had been locked into her bones ease up, giving way to a quiet but persistent curiosity about this woman in front of her.

    They end up talking more about Zosia’s job, and she tells Carol all about her favorite residents – like Fanny, a scrawny eighty-year-old lady who hides desserts in her robes and then forgets about them, or Miguel, who used to be a fisherman and likes to teach to whoever’s willing to listen how to tie knots that will never be of any use. Which is why Zosia knows how to tie a perfect palomar knot, a bimini twist, and a dropper loop.

    “I’m telling you! He gets really upset if I don’t sit there and listen.”

    “At least he’s teaching you good survival skills. Better than, I don’t know, crocheting.”

    “Excuse me,” Zosia feigns offence, her hand dramatically landing on her chest. “Crocheting is very relaxing, thank you.”

    Carol chuckles. “Right. But fishing is definitely more useful. Like, think about it – apocalypse-wise, fishing beats crocheting.”

    Zosia snorts softly. “Unless it’s really cold and you need a very nice handmade cap.”

    “Fair point,” they smile at each other, and Carol doesn’t feel bad about it, for now. She decides to bask in this bubble for as long as it doesn’t pop, which will eventually happen.

    The coffee has gone lukewarm, but she doesn’t care. The old couple in the corner has left, now replaced by teenagers biting into giant burgers and scrolling on their phones, loud and careless. Outside, people move on with their lives, that’s what’s supposed to happen. And today, Carol feels like she’s one of those functional, normal human beings; which is why, when Zosia clears her throat to get her attention, Carol dreads what she’s going to say.

    “I should probably get you home, now.”

    Carol feels the familiar dip in her stomach, a pebble in a pond.

    “Yeah,” she says. “Yeah, okay.”

    They stand, and Carol reaches for her crutch. She appreciates that Zosia doesn’t help this time, but now she also knows that doing so is in her nature. Still, she prefers being given a sliver of independence, and quietly slides out of the booth after leaving some money on the table and placing her mug on top of it.

    As they walk towards the door, the bubble pops, and Carol knows that today hasn’t fixed anything, except maybe her foot. The house waiting for her is still an empty house, devoid of love, affection, care, because they’re all buried deep underground, now. She’ll never get to share a milkshake with her eighty-year-old wife, they’ll never laugh like mischievous kids, they’ll never have more dates and anniversaries. Sudden guilt punches her in the guts, and she feels like the entire day – the fact that she has enjoyed it, that she has even laughed – is a betrayal. It shouldn’t have happened.

    The ride home is silent, and Carol makes sure it stays that way, giving Zosia curt answers and looking out of the window for the entirety of the ride. She knows it’s like whiplash, and probably Zosia’s weirded out by Carol’s constant swings in attitude, but it’s not her place to care about that.

    When Zosia pulls into her driveway, she’s still smiling, albeit this time it’s more tentative, almost nervous.

    “I had a great time,” she says, her dark, fawny eyes scanning Carol’s face for signs that echo the sentiment. They drop to her own hands when she doesn’t find any.

    Carol purses her lips, starts picking at her cuticles with her nails. They’re too short to do any damage, so it doesn’t really ground her.

    “Yeah,” she says. “Thanks for driving me around.”

    “Anytime.”

    Carol opens the door, and gets out of the car. Before she can place her good foot on the pavement, Zosia grabs her wrist. She doesn’t hold it, doesn’t squeeze. Her fingers are just there, yet they burn into Carol’s skin as if they were made of pure magma.

    “I mean it – if you need anything, just call.”

    Carol nods, but it takes a lot of willpower to bite her tongue and hold back the “I won’t” that threatens to leave her lips.

     

    *

     

    Once she’s back into the emptiness of her house, Carol drops the crutches and her bag on the floor and limps straight to the liquor cabinet. She grabs a bottle – she doesn’t even bother to choose her poison – but no glass, then heads upstairs to the bedroom.

    She ends up sitting in front of their dresser. Helen’s stuff is in the bottom drawer, and she hasn’t had the courage to open it since that evening. She’s afraid that if she does, Helen’s scent will slip away, and she’ll lose that too, forever.

    Still, she breathes deep and steadies herself to be as quick as possible when she throws it open and grabs a sweater, then shuts it again.

    As soon as she brings it to her face, inhaling deeply, her eyes well up with tears and a raw cry breaks out of her.

     

    She doesn’t know how long she remains there, on the cold hard wood floor. At some point, when the bottle is almost empty and she can’t smell anything but the alcohol in her breath, she takes off her shirt and wears her wife’s sweater.

    Then, she curls up on Helen’s side of the bed, and cries herself to sleep.

It hits her like icicles falling from a canopy, and she’s glad the shock gives her something else to focus on.

When she steps out of the shower, she stands in front of the mirror and stares at her reflection. Bags and dark circles under her eyes. Sad, pathetic hair falling limply above her slouched shoulders. Arms, lean but less defined than they used to be. Faintly bloated stomach. She pinches at the skin there, pulling it as if she could tear it off.

     

    *

     

    Carol leaves the house with no actual plan on how to spend the day. She just knows she needs to stay out of those four walls for a while before she does something stupid like looking up how many benzos she’d need to take to sleep for the entirety of the year.

    Predictably, she winds up wandering the aisles of her favorite grocery store. She’s not looking for anything in particular, but the way she glances around does give her a suspicious aura. Some clerks eye her attentively, and she brushes their stares off with awkward and tight smiles. She walks past the pet care section and, two seconds later, retraces her steps and stops in front of those bags with happy canine faces that she kind of envies.

    Yeah, fuck it, she thinks, and grabs one of those bags, plus a few cans of wet food. She hastily checks out before she gets a chance to reconsider.

    In her car, she looks up the address of the closest shelter around and, twenty minutes later, she’s looking at puppies excitedly wagging their tails to get her attention, sneezing and standing on their hind legs to sniff her hands and let her pet them. Some stay behind in their crates, uninterested and lazily keeping tabs on the situation. Those are the ones that draw Carol’s attention the most.

    She walks to the crate where a light brown, short-haired dog lays curled up on a quilted blanket. Carol glances at the kennel card on the side: Rusty – 10 years old – Personal traits: goofy, happy, high-energy, sweet – I like long walks and belly rubs and car rides! Oh, and frisbees! And kids! And treats!

    She crouches and stares at Rusty, narrowing her eyes. He blinks back slowly. “Hi,” she puts the back of her hand against the crate. “I’m Carol.”

    Rusty stretches his neck to sniff her for a few seconds, then goes back to staring. Carol studies his face. His eyes look weirdly human – sad and disappointed – and some hairs are turning white around them. His floppy ears don’t perk up when she speaks next.

    “You don’t seem very happy to me.”

    If she were drunk, she’d imagine Rusty talking back to her, saying something like thanks, you look miserable yourself. He doesn’t, of course. But his snout gets closer to Carol’s hand once again, sheepishly evaluating her smell. His nose feels damp against her dry skin.

    “Aw, look at you two!”

    A voice from behind her startles both Carol and Rusty, and as Carol retracts her hand, Rusty does the same and buries his face in the blanket. A very short, very energetic and smiley young woman skips towards them with her hands clasped in front of her face. Carol wonders whether the kennel card should sport her name and face instead of Rusty’s.

    “You’re besties already!”

    Carol stands up and scratches her head. “I’m sorry?”

    “You and Rusty! See, he’s one of our old-timers. And he’s a very good boy, aren’t you, Rusty?”

    The dog doesn’t pay her any attention, and lets out one of those deep, heavy sighs that make humans wonder whether dogs have some ancestral anguish to deal with or bills to pay. Or it might just be that high-pitched baby voices don’t work on him anymore.

    “Oh. Yeah. Apparently we have a lot in common,” she points at the kennel card. “Happy, high-energy, and all that.”

    The girl doesn’t seem to catch the sarcasm in her voice. Instead, her smile grows bigger and brighter. Carol stares at her colorful braces as she speaks.

    “Oh my god! Then it’s the perfect match! Don’t you think?”

    Carol doesn’t really know what to say. Sure, she’d bought food and bowls on a whim, but that’s how she does a lot of things. She doesn’t think about outcomes. Rusty paws at the crate bars, a timid motion to get Carol’s attention, followed by a whine that surprises both the shelter worker and Carol.

    “See, he really likes you! Don’t you, Rusty boy?”

    Fuck.

     

    *

     

    Carol drives back home thinking about that sad dog movie she and Helen watched when they lived in a cluttered one-bedroom apartment in New York. Helen had declared that she’d never get attached to a dog, and between sobs made Carol swear that they would never get one, for her heart and sanity, because their lives were too short, and she couldn’t love something so fleeting without being crushed by its very nature.

    In the passenger seat, Rusty sits with his tongue out and a renewed glint in his eyes. Carol can feel his breath on her arm, and she can’t help but smile, even though she knows that she has just broken an old oath. Of all the dogs she could’ve chosen, she got one of the oldest, yet once out, he started looking like an overly excitable puppy.

    “Should we get you a frisbee?”

    Rusty wags his tail, tilting his head to the side in lively curiosity.

    “Oh, so you actually like them,” she glances at him for a second, then her eyes are back on the road, but she decides to test it again. “Frisbee.”

    Rusty lets out a timid bark, and Carol imagines he’s trying to say yes, I’ve heard you the first time, lady, get me a damn frisbee.

    They stop at a sports store along the way, and Carol ends up buying two frisbees and a couple of tennis balls.

    As she enters her house, Rusty trails behind her with uncertainty. Carol observes as he looks around, sniffing the air and the floor, going from one room to the other, and waiting for her to go upstairs. She gives him a tour, and Rusty seems to like her studio more than the rest of the house, because he doesn’t immediately follow her when she moves to the living room.

    But he sticks close as she puts his food away in the pantry and then decides for a spot to place a tiny mat with his bowls. She fills one with water and stands there, hands on her hips, as Rusty laps at it.

    “Alright, welcome home, buddy.”

    They spend the rest of the afternoon lounging about in front of the TV, and Carol feels her heart melt a little when Rusty cuddles up as physically close as he can and puts his head on her lap, waiting for a few scritches behind his ears.

    They’re both about to doze off when Carol feels her phone buzz between her thigh and the couch armrest. She doesn’t like the way her heart jumps when she reads Zosia’s name on the display.

    “I can’t believe I didn’t put two and two together.”

    Then, a second text, and it’s a picture of a copy of Stormshadow of Wycaro, with Carol’s name written in large letters at the top of the cover. Bestselling author and all. Her immediate reaction is the same as someone who’s done nothing wrong but gets anxious as if being accused of something. She realizes it’s not about her books, but more about being perceived by someone else – and it’s not the same as the hundreds of fans she meets at book signing events or conventions. Zosia makes her nervous, clammy palms and jittery legs nervous, because as much as she tries to push her away (and it’s not like she’s made a huge effort to) she never seems to succeed. And it isn’t even Zosia’s fault, truthfully. It’s Carol who does all the work.

    Just like she’s doing now, typing a reply without even thinking about it.

    “Oh, so you’re a fan.”

    She sends the message, then turns to Rusty, who’s blissfully snoring on her leg.

    “Hey, Rusty,” the old fella looks up at her and sighs. It’s kind of humbling, because he already looks tired of Carol and he has known her for less than ten hours. “Could you please bite my hands off?”

    Zosia’s reply is quick and even more humbling than her dog’s antics.

    “Oh, no. It’s not mine. I found it in Fanny’s room. Apparently she borrowed it from Miss Rosa, who’s reading the sequel.”

    “Wow. They should start a book club.”

    “That’s actually a good idea. I’ll tell them. But you know what’s funny? Fanny said I look like the guy on the cover.”

    Carol furrows her brow, then zooms on the photo Zosia sent her and stares at Raban’s face. It’s… uncanny. And it’s also incredibly weird that a grandma in a nursing home has basically unveiled her biggest authorial secret – that the heartthrob that fueled the fantasies of many readers of her series was actually supposed to be a woman. And now, thanks to Fanny the dessert thief, Carol can’t unsee the resemblance with Zosia.

    “Huh, weird. I don’t see it.”

    “That’s what I said. But then she called Miss Rosa and she agreed with her, and you can’t really argue with stubborn grandmas.”

    “I guess.”

    Carol wants to desperately get a drink. But Rusty seems so at peace there, on her leg, that she doesn’t have the heart to move his head and leave him alone. So, she remains on the couch, clutching her phone, waiting for more texts from Zosia, though they never come. She ends up watching five more episodes of Golden Girls before she falls asleep.

     

    *

     

    Once Rusty gets comfortable in his new home, Carol understands why his kennel card called him happy, high-energy and playful. He seems to never get tired of running around, jumping and catching anything she throws at him and then placing it at her feet for her to redo it all again.

    And as tiring as it is, after a few weeks Carol finds that her mind has stopped drifting to dark places as soon as she opens her eyes. Now, she wakes up to a waggly tail and paws digging in her stomach, and she doesn’t hate it. She eats breakfast almost every morning, then showers, puts on sporty clothes and goes on long walks which sometimes turn into a jog, then finds a spot to play with Rusty until he pants so heavily that she worries his heart might give out.

    She starts taking lots of pictures. Of sunrises – Helen’s favorite moment of the day –, when they go out early because it’s starting to get too hot, and sunsets when the air is crisp enough to allow a short walk around the cul-de-sac. And as she settles into this newfound rhythm, she finds that it’s a little easier to breathe.

    She has a few drinks in the evenings. Nothing to make her pass out on the floor, but just enough to lighten the load of burdening feelings that threaten to spill when she glances at pictures of Helen – especially the one where she’s doing bunny ears behind Carol’s head – and when Zosia sends her casual texts, which are becoming a common occurrence that’s not entirely unwelcomed. Still, it’s not always an effective counteraction to those gnawing thoughts that remind her she shouldn’t be enjoying anything, not even a walk in the park. Whenever she gets to the evening without having had a single thought of her wife, that’s when Carol feels herself slip into the all too familiar spiral of despair. Not thinking about her sounds like forgetting, and forgetting sounds like a second death. And she can’t be responsible for that.

    What she feels responsible for, though, is the way her fingers itch to text back whenever Zosia tells her something that happened at work, or simply some random thoughts that occur to her during the day. Sometimes, they watch the same channel on TV, and they text Jeopardy! answers back and forth until the show’s over and she finds herself smiling at a static screen. Some other nights, Zosia seems to be too tired to even text and resorts to sending her voice notes, which she doesn’t usually like, but hers are not that annoying to listen to. When she’s had a particularly intense shift, Zosia drags vowels as if they’re heavy themselves, and at times, Carol can hear the smoke she holds in her lungs as she says something, then the breath she exhales to release it. But the softness, the warmth – coiled around her endearing accent – those stick to Carol more insistently day by day, until it feels like this is part of a routine. It’s just something they do, and she doesn’t know how to explain it.

    Tonight, with Rusty snoring softly in her lap, Carol’s zapping through channels and nothing seems to catch her interest. She sips at the scotch in her tumbler glass and pulls out her phone, then taps on the messaging app.

    “What are we watching tonight? There’s Grey’s Anatomy reruns on abc.”

    She doesn’t get a reply for another twenty minutes, and tries not to think too much of it. But her jaw is tense, and she wishes she hadn’t texted first. She focuses on the screen in front of her, forcing herself to ignore the one that remains unlit on the coffee table.

    Just as Christina Yang starts yelling about a lost shoe amidst a plane wreck, her phone buzzes. The vibration against the wood startles Rusty awake.

    “Sorry, buddy,” Carol gives him a few scratches on the head, then grabs her phone.

    “Which season? I’m going out tonight, but some early grey’s sounds tempting”

    “I think it’s season 8”

    “Damn. Hope it’s not the finale at least”

    “Well…”

    Carol wonders what kind of people Zosia hangs out with. She doesn’t like to be nosy, so she refrains from asking questions that are too personal, and that also allows her to set and respect boundaries to relationships that never evolve into friendship or even just acquaintance status. That’s mostly convenient, always has been, but now she really wants to know what sorts of places Zosia goes to, what she does to have a fun night out. She would never ask, of course. But still, she can’t shake off that curiosity.

    “Nooo :(”

    “I’ll watch it for both of us, don’t worry. It’s not like I can give you spoilers anyway”

    “I have a better idea.”

    Carol hesitates, staring at those words a little longer than necessary. On the TV screen, someone is banging a rock against the plane fuselage. After a few more minutes, Zosia sends her an address. Carol’s frown deepens when she taps on it and reads the name of a bowling alley that isn’t too far, but definitely not close enough for a night walk.

    “What’s this?”

    “An underground fight club.”

    Carol rolls her eyes.

    “Very funny.”

    Her chest tightens as Zosia’s caller ID suddenly pops up on her screen and she almost drops her phone. After a few rings, she swipes to answer.

    “Hey,” Carol clears her throat, muting the TV.

    “I’m hopping in the car, so I thought I’d just call.” Zosia sounds slightly out of breath.

    “Sure.”

    “So, what do you say, are you coming?”

    “Huh…”

    “I’ll just assume you suck at bowling if you don’t.” Zosia teases. There’s a laugh in her voice – soft, coaxing, and oddly comforting.

    “I do not suck at bowling,” Carol shoots back, even as her stomach flutters.

    “Ok, then prove it.”

    “I would, but –” Carol scratches the back of her neck, glancing at the empty glass on the coffee table. “I can’t drive.” She exhales, a mix of embarrassment and frustration.

    “Oh? The ankle? I thought it wasn’t bothering you anymore.”

    “No, no, I’m fine. It’s just… huh. I’ve had a few drinks. Can’t start my car.”

    “I see,” Zosia hums. Carol hears the clinking of her car keys against the ignition lock, then the tired sound of her Toyota starting up. “I could… pick you up? If you want.”

    Carol blinks slowly, surprised by the way she’s not entirely against the idea. “I feel like you enjoy being my chauffeur way too much.”

    Zosia chuckles softly. “Maybe.”

    There’s a pause, and it feels charged with expectation from the other side of the line. Carol breathes deep and glances at Rusty, who’s now moved to the opposite side of the couch and is still snoring soundly.

    “Alright. At least we’re not going to the hospital this time.”

    “That’s the spirit. I’ll be there in ten.”

     

     

    Carol realizes what she has agreed to only a few minutes later, when she’s standing in front of the bathroom mirror and she’s looking at her hair – which she should definitely cut and style better – and wonders if Rusty could make a good excuse for her to not leave the house. I’m sorry, he doesn’t like to be alone, I don’t want him chewing my couch off, he’d wail and bark and bother the neighbors all night long. Except he doesn’t do any of these things, because when they’re home, he likes his space and his sleep, and in fifteen days, she’s heard him bark a grand total of four times. Also, he doesn’t seem like an overly attached or anxious dog and, if anything, all those excuses fit her better than they fit the dog.

    She ends up throwing on a pair of washed-out jeans and an old off-white Fleetwood Mac T-shirt under a black leather jacket, because it’s just a bowling alley, not the Met Gala. Still, she puts on a bit of makeup, mostly to cover up the sempiternal dark circles under her eyes. Then, she sprays a bit of perfume on her neck and wrists and hastily puts the bottle away before she can question why the fuck she’s wearing the one Helen had gifted her and asked her to wear to dinner on her birthday.

    But the thought is there now, and it makes her shoulders shake and lips curl downwards, and she has to look away from her own reflection before she punches the mirror.  

    Ten minutes seem to stretch to an hour, and while she waits sitting on a kitchen chair, she feels her legs tremble and her palms get clammy. She wipes them on her thighs, then stands to grab another glass of scotch and downs it in a quick motion.

    Before she can pour some more, she hears Zosia’s car in her driveway, so she places the glass in the sink and puts the bottle back in the liquor cabinet.

    “Please, don’t chew off my couch,” she says to Rusty as she gives him a few scritches behind his ears, and then heads out.

     

    Zosia is waiting for her with her window rolled down. It’s a typical warm mid-June evening, though there’s a gentle breeze that ruffles Carol’s hair as soon as she takes a few steps towards the car.

    “Hey,” Zosia calls, and Carol wishes she would just stop looking at her as she circles the front of her car and gets in the passenger seat. She smells tangerine, jasmine and smoke, as usual, and it’s starting to become an incredibly specific smell, as if she’s collected it in an imaginary vial with Zosia’s name on it.

    “Hi,” she replies while she fumbles with the seatbelt. “Thanks for picking me up. Again.”

    Zosia smiles as she reverses out of the driveway and into the quiet and poorly lit road that leads them out of Carol’s cul-de-sac.

    “Ready to get your ass kicked?” She says, never taking her eyes off the road. Carol allows herself a few seconds to study her. From her profile to her posture, Zosia seems to embody a peaceful relaxation that slips into everything she does. The way her hand shifts gears while the other steers the wheel, the confident smirk on her lips, it all exudes a certain resolve that Carol wishes she could absorb, at least a little. Then her eyes find the exposed skin of her thighs, and she has to look away, she really does. Her heart picks up a bit when she looks up and finds Zosia glancing at her. She doesn’t say anything.

    “You tell me,” Carol tries her best at sounding just as confident. When Zosia laughs, she feels relieved.

    “Don’t get cocky. You’re up against some tough players.”

    “Oh, I’m sorry. I didn’t know I was in the presence of bowling royalty here.”

    “No, not me. Koumba is kind of a show-off. You’ll see.”

     

     

    Turns out that Carol is actually crashing a birthday party, though no one seems particularly bothered by it. In fact, a tall gentleman with a peculiar French accent greets her as if she were an old friend, enthusiastically shaking her hand while beaming at the attention everyone else seems to be giving him.

    “Koumba, are you trying to rip her arm off?” Zosia cuts in with a laugh, placing her hand on Carol’s back. She must feel the way Carol’s spine stiffens, because she retracts it immediately.

    “Oh, ma chère, I don’t need these dirty tricks to beat you all,” Koumba says with a grin. “Like every year, after all.”

    “Well, don’t get too comfortable this time. Right, Carol?” Zosia winks at her.

    “Wha – uh. Yeah. I – yeah,” she sighs, wishing the floor would open and swallow her whole. “Happy birthday, by the way.”

     

    Zosia introduces her around with an ease that makes Carol quite comfortable, although she trips on her own words and worries that her hand might be too clammy for more handshakes. Fifteen nice to meet yous later, Carol inhales deeply to steady herself. The smell of old shoes and fryer oil hits her nostrils and clings to her clothes, but she doesn’t mind the warm bustling atmosphere. If anything, it helps her stay out of her own head.

    She ends up sitting on a sticky plastic chair on her own while Zosia gets bowling shoes for both of them. Koumba slides in the seat next to her with two cups, holding one out for Carol and offering her what he must think is a charming smile.

    “Thanks,” she says as she grabs the cup, though she lifts it to her mouth with uncertainty. When she realizes that it’s just soda, she takes a sip.  

    “So, Carole,” he starts, rolling the r and leaning into his accent when pronouncing Carol’s name. “How do you know Zosia?”

    “Oh,” Carol tries to hide her face behind the cup. “Well. I yelled at her at the hospital.”

    Koumba nods for a second, then he suddenly snaps his fingers and points one at her. “Ah, oui, then you must be the woman she’s been talking about at –”

    “Hey, you two,” Zosia sits in the chair to Carol’s left, handing her a pair of shoes. “Is he trying to intimidate you, Carol?” Her voice sounds playful, but she gives Koumba a somewhat charged glare.

    “Oh, no,” Carol gestures at the cup he’s kindly offered her. “Unless he has a habit of poisoning his opponents.”

    “Ah!” Koumba dramatically places his hand over his chest. “Another accusation! Let’s let the bowling balls do the talking!” He stands up and adjusts his blazer and tie. Carol takes the time to wonder why he’s dressed like that in a bowling alley.

    Once Koumba leaves to go speak to other friends, Carol turns to Zosia with a raised eyebrow. She wants to know what he was going to say before Zosia interrupted them. But she’s also scared to find out anything that could give their uncertain relationship a more defined outline. She goes for some casual questions while she ties her left shoe.

    “So, how do you know him? He seems like… a lot.”

    “Oh, yes, he is. But we go way back,” Zosia shrugs, like that should be explanation enough. She seems to realize that it isn’t when Carol doesn’t say anything back. She hesitates, then clears her throat and continues. “He’s the first friend I made when I started going to meetings.”

    Carol presses her lips together and nods. Those meetings. The word settles heavy in the back of her head as she wonders how many of these people here share the same background. Judging by the cups full of soda, most of them, for sure. Not to mention the fact that they seem to talk to each other with the deep familiarity of people who share more than birthdays and bowling nights.

    Suddenly, she feels a pang of disappointment in her chest, and it burns up to her neck, and she doesn’t know where to place the feeling that she’s been lured into something all along.

    “You’re not –” She starts, then stops, exhales through her nose. “You’re not trying to… I don’t know. Make a point?”

    Zosia frowns, caught off guard by the edge in Carol’s voice. “Make a point? About what?”

    “I don’t know, about me?” Carol gestures vaguely around them, then points to her cup. “About this? I mean, I didn’t realize today came with a theme.”

    Zosia sighs and leans back in her chair, her expression tight and uncertain.

    “I’m not trying to get you into anything,” she says carefully. “I just wanted to hang out with you.”

    Carol swallows the lump in her throat. Heat creeps up her face and she feels incredibly stupid.

    “Okay. Nevermind, then,” She focuses on tying her other shoe, then looks up at Zosia again. “Sorry.”

    Zosia doesn’t acknowledge her apology. Instead, she watches Carol for a second longer than necessary. “Is it that hard to believe,” she says, quieter now, “that some people might just enjoy your company?”

    The lane lights flicker as someone keys in the next round, and the mechanical clatter of pins being reset fills the brief silence between them. Carol doesn’t answer right away. She presses her lips together, nods once, and focuses very deliberately on tightening the laces of her bowling shoes, even though they’re already snug.

    Zosia doesn’t push and, after a few seconds, she turns her attention to the lane just as Koumba strides up rolling his shoulders and cracking his neck like he’s about to step on a ring instead of a polished strip of wood.

    “Alright, mes amis,” he announces, clapping his hands together, “Let us begin the massacre.”

    Carol exhales a quiet breath. There’s a turmoil of feelings in her stomach threatening to gain the upper hand on her sanity. Zosia wanting to hang out because she feels like it, not because she’s on some sort of savior complex high, sounds like the best worst revelation she could get tonight. It does something to her chest, warms it up and softly engulfs her beaten muscle, but the way it comforts her is also terribly frightening, and she wishes she’d just stayed home watching those damn Grey’s Anatomy reruns.

    Outside of her own head, people are gathering near the lanes, waiting for their turn. They laugh, throw their balls and watch them thud and skid and hit the pins, then cheer or groan in equal measure.

    Koumba lives up to his reputation, throwing with unnecessary flair and reacting to every strike like it’s proof of divine favor. Zosia plays well too, steady and unshowy, but with each strike she grows more confident and teases Koumba about stealing his crown. He waves her off with a smirk on his face.

    When it’s Carol’s turn, she hesitates at the edge of the lane, and her first throw is clumsy and angled wrong. The ball wobbles and hits one pin before ending up in the gutter.

    “That’s exactly what I wanted to do,” Carol says as she turns around and grabs the second ball.

    “Hey,” Zosia calls. “First throw doesn’t count. It’s the rule.”

    A few people nod in agreement and cheer for Carol, but she finds that the one smile she gives out is directed to Zosia, who’s doing a thumbs up gesture with both her hands.

    The second throw is better. Not great, but better. She gets a few enthusiastic claps on her shoulders as she moves to the back of the line and watches other people strike with precision.

    Between frames, everybody settles into a rhythm. Someone orders fries and slices of pizza, and Carol feels like she’s at a thirteen-year-old birthday party, but it’s not necessarily a bad feeling. She blends into background laughter, listens to Zosia tell a story about the time she bragged about being very good at bowling and ended up with the ball slipping from her hands and rolling backwards straight onto Koumba’s feet.

    He laughs, nodding as if recalling a fond memory. “Ah, yes, the reverse strike.”

    Zosia pops a fry into her mouth and shrugs. “That was a few years ago. If I ever reverse strike into your feet again, Koumba, it will be intentional.”

    “And probably deserved,” he adds, stealing one fry for himself and getting up to play his turn.

    “You know, I went bowling once on a date,” Carol says, and she doesn’t know what compels her to share. Maybe it’s the comfort of laughing with someone who thinks of her as a friend, maybe it’s the clarity of being sober and present among normal people. Or maybe it’s the invitation behind Zosia’s eyes, the spark of curiosity and patience, as if she’s uncovering ancient ruins and bringing them back to their former glory. So, she speaks before she even realizes the magnitude of her unveiling.

    “She was very competitive. Like, stretching beforehand and bringing your own bowling ball competitive.” She tries her best to keep her voice steady, dodging the cracks that always come with bringing up anything too personal.

    Zosia raises an eyebrow, leaning forward and propping her chin in her palm. Her eyes follow Carol’s words attentively.

    “I was shit, obviously. Gutter ball after gutter ball. And I spent the entire night apologizing for ruining her average,” Carol grabs a fry, dips it in ketchup, pops it in her mouth. She chews slowly to give herself time to avoid breaking down. “But I guess I did something right on that date, because she ended up marrying me anyway.”

    There’s a beat in which she’s sure Zosia wants to say something, but she doesn’t give her the chance.

    “Bowling was still a dealbreaker though. We never went again,” she grabs another fry, then glances up at the screen. “It’s your turn.”

    Zosia seems surprised by the abrupt self-interruption, her mouth hanging half open as if ready to ask some more questions, but Carol is glad that she just nods and walks to the ball return.

    She throws two gutter balls and goes back to her seat with a thoughtful expression. Koumba teases her, but she seems distracted.

    As Carol walks up to the lane, she catches Zosia’s attentive gaze on her. She feels it linger as she throws her first strike of the game and is flooded with cheers and laughter and clapping as if she’s just overthrown Koumba.

    In the end, when the final scores flash on the screen, Koumba still wins by a comfortable margin. He raises his arm as if this had ever been in doubt.

    “Same time next year,” he declares. “You all may train in the meantime.”

    As people begin to disperse and gather their things, Carol realizes she’s incredibly tired, but she can’t point whether this is just pure physical strain or it has something to do with the bit of oversharing she’s fallen into. People come up to her to say goodbye, some are as optimistic as to say see you soon, and Koumba walks up to her to thank her and give her two kisses on the cheeks.

    She puts her shoes and her jacket back on and waits for Zosia before heading out in the cooler midnight air.

     

     

    In the parking lot, Zosia leans on the hood of her car, fumbling with something in her denim jacket’s pocket. She pulls out a lighter and a pack of cigarettes, takes one for herself and offers one to Carol. Sure, she says, and then slips one out of the light blue carton.

    Zosia gets closer to light it up for her and Carol forces herself to avoid eye contact. It’s futile, and she falls victim to the magnetic pull that anchors her gaze.

    “I – I had a wonderful time,” she says then, her voice hoarse as the smoke burns her lungs and stings at the back of her throat.

    “Me too,” Zosia puffs out the smoke to the side, never taking her eyes off of Carol.

    It’s starting to get too intense, the way those dark eyes follow her fingers to her mouth with each drag, and Carol can’t stand it anymore. She turns her back to Zosia as she exhales the smoke up in the air and watches it dissipate.

She gets closer and reads the shaky handwriting.

    Help yourself :), it says. She doesn’t take any and keeps looking around. It feels less like an A.A. meeting and more like a bonding session for a new theatre company.

    Then, a short woman walks out from behind the stage. She’s holding paper plates and cups and doesn’t immediately notice Carol because she’s too focused on keeping everything balanced under her chin.

    “Hey,” Carol says, raising her hand in a guarded greeting.

    The woman startles, but manages to keep everything off the floor. “Oh. Hi, hi! Hello,” she scrambles to place the items on the table and looks back at Carol. “I didn’t see you there.”

    “Sorry, didn’t mean to startle you,” Carol scratches the back of her neck. She was hoping it wouldn’t feel this awkward. But this woman – her curly red hair, tiny watery eyes and prominent front teeth – reminds her of an anthropomorphic mouse, and it’s unsettling. Like she’s just walked into a parallel universe of weird creatures.

    But then, she wonders if mouse lady feels the same about her. Maybe, from where she stands, she looks like a sad chihuahua trying to steal donuts.

    “No, no, it’s fine. We don’t usually start right on time, so I wasn’t expecting to find someone here already.”

    “Right,” Carol puts her hands in her jeans pockets, hooking her thumbs in the belt loops, and looks around. “Are you, uhm, the one in charge?”

    Mouse lady chuckles. “Oh, I like the sound of that,” she strides closer to Carol with her hand held out for her. “I guess you could say that! My name’s Misty.”

    Carol takes the offered hand and shakes it politely. “Carol.”

    “Nice to meet you, Carol. Is this your first meeting?”

    “Yeah. Can you tell? My hand’s really sweaty.”

    “Don’t worry about that. I’ve shaken sweatier.”

    Carol chuckles without even realizing, but, as she’s about to say something else, like maybe asking for advice for a newbie, three people arrive at the same time. They crowd the small table, drink some coffee, grab a donut and then take their seats with seasoned experience.

    Chairs scrape softly against the stage floor as people drift into the circle. No one rushes amongst quick nods and quiet greetings. Carol takes a seat slightly off-center, not quite at the edge, but close enough to leave without making a scene.

    Misty settles last, folding her legs and glancing around the circle.

    “Alright,” she says, rubbing her hands together lightly. “Looks like we’re seven today. That’s a good number. Cozy.” She smiles. “If you’re new, you don’t have to share. You can just listen. No pressure.”

    Carol stares at her own shoes as Misty continues, voice steady, like she’s rehearsing a well-loved script.

    “I’m Misty,” she says, with a soft wave of her hand. “I’m an alcoholic.”

    A quiet chorus follows, gentle and automatic. “Hi, Misty.”

    They go around in a loose circle, name after name and addiction after addiction. There’s Tom, alcoholic, then Jade, alcoholic, and Eileen, alcoholic. Marcus gives his name and struggles a bit before he manages to say it out loud. Rina, alcoholic, comes in last, and she looks so young, yet so experienced with this, that Carol feels slightly intimidated.

    She doesn’t realize everyone’s waiting for her. She stammers, looks at Misty for a second, and she gives her an encouraging nod. It doesn’t do much, because she doesn’t know her, there’s nothing in her that warrants her trust.

    She wonders if that’s how Zosia felt the first time.

    “I -  I’m Carol,” she says, trying to avoid the sets of eyes that seem to battle for her attention. “I’m an alcoholic.”

    No one reacts or flinches, and Carol feels like the glaive has been lifted from behind her neck.

    “Hi, Carol.”

    Misty nods once. “Anyone want to start?”

    And just like that, Carol finds herself pulled into stories of strangers who aren’t afraid to share. They speak as if it’s only for themselves, like they’re talking to a mirror, but without the pressure of looking at their own faces.

    Eileen talks briefly about gardening instead of drinking in the mornings. Marcus mentions switching from vodka to sparkling water and feeling ridiculous about it. Rina admits she still avoids work events because she doesn’t trust herself around open bars.

    It all flows like it’s second nature for them, to be this open, and Carol starts to feel like there really is nothing to be scared of.

    Then, Tom clears his throat. He doesn’t look at anyone in particular as he speaks, but Carol notices his tense shoulders, buried under a heavy reflective vest smeared in paint.

    “I hit six months last week,” he says. “Didn’t tell anyone about it. My wife doesn’t care, and my kids, well, they don’t trust it yet.” He shrugs, like he’s just talking about the weather. “I used to think I was the kind of guy who just liked a few beers after work, you know what I mean?”

    There are a few understanding nods before he keeps going.

    “Yeah, well. Beers turned into whiskey and that turned into bottles hidden in the garage. But I always thought I had it under control. I never missed a day at work, never got a DUI. See, I thought I was doing it all exactly how you’re supposed to. Never crossing certain lines means you’re still in charge, right? But then I started saying things, cruel things. Sometimes I forgot about the words, but remembered my wife’s face after I said them. And I know that it was always the worst thing I could say.”

    Carol’s jaw tightens as Tom talks about himself, but he might as well be talking about her – minus the never getting a DUI. She grinds her teeth together so hard it makes her head hurt.

    “Anyway, six months,” people smile softly as he repeats his milestone without an ounce of pride, just matter-of-factly, as if he’s not even halfway done. “I still don’t know who I am at night. After dinner, you know, those hours. They’re the hardest. That’s it.”

    There’s a beat of silence waiting to be filled. Misty nods. “Thank you for sharing, Tom.”

    Jade speaks next without being invited. She looks like she’s in her mid-twenties, messy bun and oversize hoodie with sleeves pulled over her hands.

    “I’m at day twelve,” she says. “Again.” A short laugh escapes her lips, but doesn’t quite land. It gets her a few sympathetic smiles from Rina and Eileen. “I always make it to two weeks, like that’s enough to prove something. Maybe that I’m not that bad. And some days it’s easy to be fooled. Like, I don’t wake up shaking, I have a good job, a nice boyfriend… and I don’t even drink every day. But then I just binge, hard. I feel like my chances are thinning out. So yeah. I’m back to day twelve, and trying to avoid fucking up.”

    “Glad you’re here, Jade,” Misty says softly.

    The room settles again, and this time the silence feels heavier. Like it’s Carol’s turn to fill it, now. She rubs her hands on her thighs, then picks at a nail while glancing around the room.

    “You’re welcome to share,” Misty’s eyes land on her anyways. “But you don’t have to. No pressure.”

    Carol knows she means well. But sharing or not, the pressure is there already. At this point, she might as well try and fight it back.

    “Uh. I don’t know how this works,” she looks to Eileen, whose gentle eyes feel the warmest in the room. Her lips curve up in the understanding smile of a mother, and it does feel safe enough that she can picture talking to her, and her only.

    “I’m at two months,” she starts, her voice surprisingly steadier than how she feels. “It feels like a lifetime, and sometimes I wish I had someone to be proud of me, but I don’t, because the people who could care are – uhm, well, they’re either gone or I pushed them away. But yeah, uhm. Two months. I thought it’d be easier the further I got, but the truth is that it’s even harder now. It’s like you said.” She points to Jade, who’s sitting there with pursed lips and her feet up on the chair, head propped on her knees. “I didn’t drink every day,  but when I did… fuck, it was slash and burn all around me.”

    Carol shifts in her seat, unsure of what to say next. She should’ve rehearsed this in the car.

    “I guess I’m here to learn how to be in control of that part of myself again.”

    Misty smiles, and chimes in with a warm “We’re glad you came, Carol. Thanks for sharing.”

    There’s a murmur of agreement, a closing ceremony for a ritualistic baring of the soul. Misty talks about the 12 steps program, in a secular approach that thankfully doesn’t involve god as much as other programs. Carol made sure of it when she looked up all the information she could gather about this particular organization here.

    After twenty more minutes, the meeting is over and Carol has survived it. She feels relief, surprise, and something warm she wants to call satisfaction, but is more leaning to the side of optimism. It wasn’t bad. No one told her to get over herself. No one even dared to glance at her with nothing but understanding in their eyes.

    As she moves to the table to grab a coffee and chat some more with Eileen and Rina, she thinks about Zosia doing this thing over and over for ten whole years. Her chest swells with fondness, though it’s the nostalgic kind, because she can’t reach out now, she can’t show up or wait for her to show up and embrace her. She can’t, but maybe this is exactly where she needs to be to make sure that that isn’t the final ruling.

     

    *

     

    Before she knows it, October is almost over and she’s been to three more meetings. It’s Halloween night and Carol is sitting on her couch snacking on candies she got just in case the neighbor’s kids come knocking, a horror movie in the background and Rusty to her side.

    As Drew Barrymore gets gutted on screen, she hears – or rather, sees – movement outside her window. There are a few voices, and then some timid knocks on her door. She’s surprised those overbearing parents even let their kids near her property. Rusty’s head shoots up, ears alert and tail tense. Carol pets him once, then gets up with the basket of candy and walks to the door. Before she opens, though, she pops in a set of vampire teeth she bought for the occasion, and wears a black cape that’s basically a blanket, but it sells a pretty convincing vampiric image, with the circles around her eyes and apparent aversion to daylight.

    As she opens, the kids seem startled for a second, then they smile and yell Trick or treat, before lunging for the jack-o-lantern shaped basket.

    The tiny Ghostface drops his rubber knife as he tries to grab the best candies in the bunch, while the girl with the long purple braid and her Spiderman brother settle for a fistful of casual sweets. When she lets out a disappointed oh at the selection, Carol offers her another pick, and she smiles as she dives in again.

    They leave her with licorice rolls and Runts that she knows will end up in the back of her pantry, because she hates them.

    She spits out the fake teeth and settles back on the couch with the blanket around her shoulders, letting Rusty cuddle up on her lap. Then, she grabs her phone, absentmindedly scrolling her Instagram feed. Most days, she tries to resist the urge to tap on Zosia’s profile, but tonight she can’t. She posted a few stories of her, Koumba, and a crowded group of people dressed up in various costumes. Zosia has touched up her face with some white foundation, which makes her look too pale to be alive, and has accentuated dark circles around her eyes. Then, she notices trickles of fake blood on her lips, pointy teeth peeking out, and she has to hold in a chuckle at the fact that they chose the same costume. Except Zosia’s out to party and has done more than the bare minimum – she looks like, fuck, a sexy vampire. When the timer on the story runs out, Carol taps back to look at it again. If she hadn’t fucked things up, she’d probably be in the picture as well.

    She types a message. I miss you. Deletes it, types  it back, then deletes it again. With a sigh, she locks her phone and tosses it to the other side of the couch.

     

    *

     

    October gives way to an uncharacteristically cold November. She’s out to buy new sweaters, today, and of all people, she runs into Eileen while shopping.

    “Carol, hi!” She waves, and Carol catches her eyeing up price tags and grimacing at the numbers as she walks up to her.

    “Eileen, hey,” she says once she’s close enough. She can’t remember the last time she’s had to make small talk with someone, but she doesn’t feel uncomfortable. It’s like she’s finally understanding what Zosia meant when she talked about community. Once you’re in it, it’s everywhere. “Find anything good?”

    She picks up a white sweater with colorful zig zag lines, inspects it like she’s interested, even though she’s just doing it to keep her hands busy.

    “Not really,” she shrugs, then glances at Carol’s pick. “Oh, that’s cute. You should try it on.”

    Carol nods, then drapes the sweater on her forearm and browses some more. “You know, I’ve been meaning to tell you… I liked what you said the other day. About not overcomplicating things. It’s, uhm, been helpful.”

    “Oh, thanks. I’m glad to hear that. You know, when you get to my age you learn that there’s nothing better than being blunt and forward. I’ve had a bunch of years to dwell on things that should’ve been easy as cake. You young people should make the most of it.”

    Carol frowns. “Eileen, we’re really not that far apart in age.”

    “I know,” she smiles, and Carol notices the wrinkles around her eyes, which make them softer, somehow, kinder and welcoming. “But you remind me of my daughter sometimes, so I can’t help it.”

    Those words make Carol’s eye sting, all of a sudden. She fake coughs to focus on something else and ends up picking up a neutral turtleneck she doesn’t even like.

    “Well, uhm. I’m going to try these on.”

    “Oh, of course. I’ll see you Monday?”

    “Yeah,” Carol nods. “I’ll be there.”

    She ends up buying both sweaters without trying them on.

     

    *

     

    Carol shifts in the leather chair in Dr. Brooks’ studio, uncrossing her legs and leaning forward with her elbows on her knees.

    “God, it makes me nauseous just thinking about it,” she says. “It’s – uhm. I feel like I’m a chicken at a crossroad.”

    “Well, even the chicken gets to the other side, eventually.” Dr. Brooks gives in to something akin to a smirk.

    “Ah. So you agree, I’m the chicken.”

    “Could be any other animal, really. Let’s focus on the rest of the image. What’s stopping you from getting there?”

    Carol sits back and hums, listening to the ticking clock behind her. She looks to the side, eyes some new fish in the huge exotic tank by the window and feels sad for them.

    “Uhm, I don’t know. Well, no, I know exactly what I’ll say. I even wrote it down the other day. But what if she doesn’t want to hear it? That’s – I mean. I wouldn’t blame her. It’s just… I don’t know if things can ever go back to the way they were.”

    “Isn’t that the point? You’re not in the same place as three months ago, now.”

    “Yeah. Well, same goes for her.”

    “If it weren’t for the program, would you still try to reach out?”

    Carol bites her lip. There’s a beat of silence that Dr. Brooks doesn’t rush to fill. Truth be told, Carol has been on the verge of just calling Zosia more than once in the past few weeks. She tapped on her contact info and let her finger hover over the phone icon, almost hoping to accidentally start the call.

    But she never did. So, now that she’s at that point of the program – make a list of the people you’ve harmed to make direct amends, when possible – she thinks it might at least be a good excuse. Or, well, maybe not an excuse. Just a more direct push.

    And then, she’s also afraid that Zosia will be willing to listen just because she knows what it’s like to work through the steps. That she’ll take it as closure and move on with her life, if she hasn’t already.

    “Yeah,” she says eventually. “I would. I almost did a few times.” It was more than a few, really, but Dr. Brooks doesn’t need to know.

    “Well, then it sounds like you’ve made up your mind already.”

     

     

    Carol turns up the radio as she drives back home. She never does it, but it reminds her of Zosia and how she seemed to always find the most remote radio stations to listen to classic rock music or 80s pop.

    She ends up on one of Zosia’s favorites, playing Everywhere by Fleetwood Mac, and wonders if she’s listening to the same song, humming along to the tune as she drives to or back from work.

    When she pulls into her driveway, she turns off the engine and sits in the car for a few more songs. As Dolores O’Riordan sings about not finding the right words to say that she loved or needed someone, she grabs her phone and scrolls down in the message app to get to Zosia’s thread. It’s at the bottom of the list, now, buried under a bunch of spam texts that have piled up over these months.

    “Hey,” she types. “I’ve been thinking about you. A lot. Can we talk?”

    She hits send before she can overthink it and swallows the panic down by locking her phone, putting it on silent mode and shoving it in her pocket.

    Carol ignores it for the rest of the day. Sometimes, when she acts like this, it feels like she’s undoing months of therapy by allowing herself to fall back into old ways that comfort her in their familiarity.

    She’s sitting at the kitchen table, peeling an apple and trying to get all of it in one piece, but anxiety makes her stomach drop and turn with every passing minute, until she can’t take it anymore.  She drops the half peeled fruit back on the plate and wipes her hands on a ragged cloth.

    When she pulls her phone out of her back pocket, there is a reply. Her heart skips a few beats and then thumps louder than ever.

    God, please, please, please.

    She takes a deep breath and taps on the notification from three hours before.

    “Hey. I’m really busy with work these days, but you can come by lunch break tomorrow.”

    Carol doesn’t know what she was expecting, really, but it wasn’t this. She knows better, now, than to obsess over something she has absolutely no control over, but the way she feels dismissed makes her almost flinch in her seat.

    “Serves me right,” she mumbles as she gets up and tosses the browning apple in the trash.

    Still, dismissive or not, Zosia is willing to give her a chance, which is neither obvious nor due, so she’ll take what she can get and be ready to make the most of it.

     Later, while she brushes her teeth, she starts thinking of the realm of possibilities fifteen minutes with a clear head could open up for her. Option A, Zosia forgives her and tells her to just move on. Option B, Zosia has agreed to meet her to tell her to fuck off. Option C, Zosia has missed her just as much as Carol has. That’s the best case scenario, the only one she doesn’t indulge in and washes away from her mind with cold water splashed over her face.

     

    That night she feels restless, her bare legs brushing against the soft duvet as she tosses and turns, bothering Rusty to the point that he flips his tail and lets out an annoyed huff before he leaves the room.

    Now, as sleep eludes her and the bed feels cold, she can’t help but picture Zosia besides her. Bare shoulders, smooth skin glowing under rays filtering through the blinds, a made up memory of how it could’ve been. She imagines the soft breaths escaping from lips facing away from her, the curve of her hip, where her fingers could’ve left sweet, sweet marks to run her tongue over.

    “Fuck,” she sighs, slamming a hand to her forehead and dragging it down her face, hoping to eradicate the fantasy – and the real thing as well. The taste of Zosia’s lips, the feel of her fingers. Why the fuck would she even think about that, now, of all days? When she’s supposed to be thinking about how to make up for her fuckups instead?

    Yet the more she forces herself to stop conjuring up those thoughts, the more insistent they get, so she sits up and leans her head back against the bedpost. With her eyes closed, she tries counting sheep hoping to drift off into sleep, but the only thing she keeps seeing even behind her eyelids is Zosia. She’s everywhere.

     

     

    When morning comes, Carol struggles to get into the groove. Her eyes sting with the lack of sleep, and her body feels sluggish, half-broken. For a second, she feels sorry for what she’s put it through for so long. Then, she slips out of bed and pads into the kitchen, getting ready for a quick breakfast, a jog with Rusty trotting behind her, and a cold shower to shake the rest of her drowsiness off once she’s back.

    Every time she glances at the clock, her heart skips and beats louder, and as it ticks towards the hour, it gets to an incessant commotion in her ribcage.

    It’s weird that she hasn’t seen Zosia in three months, which is not that long, really, but it feels like ages. And it’s like coming back home, in a way, even though she had sworn to herself that home would be one person, and that person only, when she put a slim golden band on her finger and sealed a promise that now dwells in the ground.

    But Carol is coming to understand that letting someone else in isn’t foul betrayal, and it isn’t even pure survival. It’s a chance she can take once she feels comfortable and safe enough, knowing that love – present or past and gone – doesn’t fade and doesn’t rot. That’s all the encouragement she needs, for now.

    As she stands in front of the mirror, fingers combing through her wet hair, she realizes just how long it had been since she had liked her own reflection. Not just her appearance, but the overall demeanor. She does look good, more rested despite the sleepless night. She flexes her arm, pokes at her bicep and smirks as she feels it firm, solid. But then scrunches up her nose and flinches because why is she even doing this?

    This lunch break, these fifteen minutes she’s being spared, might be a total fiasco. Yet the woman in the mirror is getting her hopes up, and dares to feel a juvenile excitement, warm, pleasant and stupid, at the thought of seeing Zosia, of finally speaking to her. Again, she’s getting lost in possibilities.

    By the time she’s dressed and ready, she realizes that Zosia has never even seen her like this. Helen had a few good years of Carol – she took care of herself, wasn’t quite a mess yet – and now she’s the closest she’s ever been to that version of her she hardly remembered.

    Carol wants Zosia to notice. She wants her to say something, maybe look at her for a few minutes too long, unable to mask her surprise. Or, better yet, Carol needs her to notice, because if she didn’t, it would be a blow too heavy to miss. It would mean indifference, and she’s not – nor will she ever be – ready to be on the receiving end of Zosia’s cold stare. Even though her recent behavior calls for it, loud and clear.

    Still, she gently shakes her head and pushes those thoughts away, grabs her car keys, kisses Rusty on the head and all but skips out of the house.

     

     

    The drive isn’t short, but Carol has left one hour earlier than she should have, so she gets to the Golden Meadows Wellness and Rehabilitation Center well before lunch time. She remembers Zosia telling her that when she moved to Albuquerque, she wasn’t sure about her career, but she knew she wanted to be of assistance to the community. After a couple years of being clean, she managed to get a CNA certificate and started working in this facility. She always spoke highly of her job, said she felt the need to help and give back, and up until recently Carol wasn’t sure she understood why.

    Now, having experienced a community, and mostly having gotten the help she so clearly needed, she gets the reasoning behind wanting to be someone who helps. Because Zosia, too, has been in a place where no one was willing to, and she’s too selfless to watch it happen to someone else.

    Carol hates that she’s the one who probably, for a split second, made Zosia regret it.

    As she circles the structure and looks for a spot in the back, where the employees’ cars are, she notices a familiar blue Toyota. She smiles at the sight and ends up parking her car not too far from it. She wonders whether she should call Zosia, or text her to let her know that she can wait here, in her car, for as long as she wants her to, then decides against it and listens to a few songs on the radio.

    But the more she looks at the time, the slower it seems to go, and at some point she can’t take it anymore. She pulls her phone out and types a quick message to Zosia.

    “Hey, I’m here. I know I’m probably super early, but I can wait.”

    Zosia doesn’t reply immediately, obviously. She knows she’s busy. Carol wants to delete the message, but as she presses on it, three dots flicker and a new text pops up.

    “Can’t come out rn”

    Then two more.

    “Sorry to keep u waiting. I should be done in 10”

    “Oh. Don’t come out front. Your fans might see you.”

    Carol giggles thinking about the Wycaro elders.

    “I could do an autograph session.”

    “That’s really nice of you, but I’d rather hear what you have to say to me.”

    Carol’s heart drops to her stomach. She bites her lip, admonishes herself for getting ahead of anything practical and jumping to thoughts and fantasies in which they’re fine and everything’s good.

    “Of course”

     

    Once midday strikes, Carol gets out of the car and decides to wait by the employee’s entrance. Zosia should be out any minute now. She glances at the recycling bins lined up to the side and taps her foot against one, whistling a low note as she feels her mind emptying by the second.

    Then, the grey door opens, and Carol turns her head so quickly she’s afraid her neck might snap. She makes eye contact with a man who looks at her like she’s a raccoon rifling through the trash.

    “Hostia, qué susto,” he mumbles while he keeps the door open with his foot and places a hand on his chest. A cigarette hangs from the side of his mouth, almost falling off. “What are you doing here?”

    The man taps the Employees Only sign on the door and raises an eyebrow. Carol blinks slowly, but after a second of disconnection, she shakes her head and scrambles to explain her presence.

    “Oh. Uhm. Sorry, I – I was waiting for –”

    “She’s fine, Manny.” Zosia’s voice reaches her before she can see her, and she slides out, leaving the door half open and reaching up to light Manny’s cigarette and then her own. “Déjala en paz.”

    She puffs out the smoke and gets closer to Carol, gazing at her mercilessly. “Carol.”

    “Zosia, hey,” she breathes. Her mouth is too dry to say anything else, but her eyes roam all over Zosia, taking her in, drinking the image to quench her self-inflicted thirst. She looks beautiful, as always. But she looks tired, too, like she hasn’t had a second to sit down in ages. And the way she stares at her feels distant, like she’s waiting for Carol to earn it.

    Zosia glances back at her coworker and gestures to the parking lot behind them. “I’m taking a walk with her. When you’re done, can you make sure Mr. Ferguson takes his pills?”

    “Sí, sí. But you owe me.”

    Zosia scoffs and flips him off, turning her attention back to Carol. “Well, shall we?”

    “Sure,” Carol says. They walk side by side in the scarce shade of a few yellowed trees. “So, you’re having a cigarette for lunch?”

    Zosia rolls her eyes. She kicks a crunchy leaf and then crushes it with the heel of her ugly nurse shoes. “I had some coffee.”

    “Okay, so a liquid and some smoke. Where’s the solid?”

    “Oh, I’m sorry,” Zosia turns to look at Carol with disbelief plastered on her face, though there’s the faintest hint of a smirk threatening to come out right underneath it all. “I didn’t know my mom was trying to get herself admitted here.”

    “Alright, alright.” Carol raises her hands in defeat. “You got me.”

    Zosia takes another drag, keeps the smoke in for a second and lets it out, all while looking at Carol with an evident inquiry in the shine of her eyes.

    “So, how have –” Carol tries, but Zosia speaks at the same time and doesn’t stop, pushes the boulder up to the top of the mountain and lets it roll on the other side, straight onto Carol’s head.

    “What are you doing here, Carol?”

    The question feels like a slap to the cheek. Obviously deserved, but that doesn’t make it sting any less. Still, Carol doesn’t miss the way Zosia recoils at the sound of her own voice, the way she studies her like she’s assessing all the differences that she isn’t naming yet.

    “I wanted to apologize. Like, properly.” Carol puts her hands in her jacket pockets and leans against the wall, head softly thumping on the concrete surface as she swallows her next uncertain words. “You deserve it, Zosia. You deserve so much more than what I – yeah.”

    “Okay, let’s start off slow,” Zosia replies, settling beside Carol and mirroring her position with a groan. There’s a beat of silence. Then, she speaks again. “You’re here to apologize. After what, three months? Why now?”

    “I – uhm. I needed time.” Carol scratches the back of her neck. “To get my shit together. I don’t think anything I could’ve said three months ago would’ve been as sincere as what I’m trying to say now.”

    “And what’s different, now?” Zosia turns her head towards her. Dark eyes melt into her own, and Carol can see that there’s still some hurt buried under the curiosity – but it’s not all there is.
