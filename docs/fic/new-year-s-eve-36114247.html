    Chapter 1

    The stars were much brighter in Missouri than they were in New York.

    Kate hadn’t expected snow in the midwest, either. Maybe it was ignorance, or a testament to the shittiness of the American school system, but she’d always imagined Missouri to be hot and dry year round - not quite like the Texan deserts of her middle school textbook, but like dry grass that crunched when you stepped in it, and cicadas that sang through the day. But instead, it had been cold since Christmas, a permanent blanket of snow over the Barton homestead.

    Kate rested her chin on her knees, her breaths billowing in frozen little clouds. Her fingertips ached, frigid and numb, and she pulled her hands into her sleeves to try and preserve heat. Lucky pranced through the Bartons’ huge yard, clumps of snow clinging to his soaked golden fur.

    Without turning around, she could guess that at least one of the Bartons had eyes on her. Clint had been checking in on her basically every day since everything that had happened on Christmas Eve, always gentle and respectful, but worried as if she were one of his kids. Laura had also welcomed her with open arms, going out of her way to make her feel comfortable and accepted. Even Lila, clearly the most perceptive of the Barton kids, had pulled her aside once or twice asking how she was doing and if she needed to talk about anything. Each time, Kate would swallow and smile sweetly and reassure everyone that she was okay, really she was, she promised .

    Of course, they saw right through her, but no one ever said a word - they’d just nod and remind her that she could talk to them if she needed to, and they’d linger for a moment more, eyes soft and kind and melancholic, before letting it go. Kate was grateful, of course, but she’d already put the Barton family through enough - after all, Clint had just barely made it home for Christmas and it had been all her fault, and she was lucky he was still willing to take her under his wing and help her hone her skills as an archer even further. Not to mention the fact that they had opened up their home at a moment’s notice for a college kid with nowhere to stay and a hungry Retriever in tow.

    It was just that Kate wasn’t even quite sure what she was feeling, and she definitely wasn’t ready to dump it all over the Bartons, either. She knew whatever she felt wasn’t the same as when her father died. Similar, sure, but deeper, more hollow, more painful. But it also hadn’t quite hit yet either. It was almost like she was an alien in her own skin, going through the motions half-asleep, smiling on cue and laughing at the kids’ jokes, all the while feeling nothing at all.

    Lucky trotted back to the deck wagging his tail with his goofy little dog grin. The corners of Kate’s lips twitched and she pulled her hands back out of her sleeves to scratch behind his ears, her frosty fingers working numbly through his fur.

    “I know,” she murmured. “Just a few more minutes, okay?” Lucky only panted in response. Though it was freezing out, the idea of going back inside made Kate’s stomach churn. She just needed a few more minutes alone, that was all. She just needed to collect herself. Lucky flopped down on the step next to her, his tail thumping against the weathered deck. Absently, Kate continued stroking his ears, focusing on the softness of his fur, the way it felt under her fingertips. It was the best way she had found to ground herself. Focus on those who needed her, stamp out whatever she was feeling. It was easier that way. She wasn’t sure how long she had been sitting outside in the snow - maybe a minute or two, maybe ten - before Lucky suddenly growled, jumping to his feet, ears perked and alert. Kate grabbed his collar, a shiver running down her spine, and before she could say anything or pull herself back to earth, she heard a familiar voice.

    “Hello, Kate Bishop.”

    Her eyes had been sightlessly glued to Lucky’s paws, and when she dragged her gaze up, a few tears managed to slip down her cheeks without her consent. Blinking to refocus them and swiftly wiping her tears away, she was both shocked yet somewhat unsurprised to see Yelena standing in the Bartons’ yard, a huge fluffy dog sat next to her on the right and a large canvas tote bag in the crook of her other arm.

    “Jesus Christ Yelena- how long have you been…? You're literally trespassing, how did you even-” The words tumbled out of Kate’s mouth as she attempted to collect herself and steady her racing heart. Yelena snorted.

    “I saw you out here trying to freeze to death and you didn’t seem to have an issue with it when I came to your apartment in New York so I figured what the hell?” When Kate just stared at her blankly she added, “Besides, it’s New Year’s Eve and you’re spending it out here alone knowing damn well you promised me a drink.”

    “So?” Kate asked stiffly. “Maybe I like it out here. Maybe I’m just enjoying the snow.” Yelena laughed. “Oh Kate Bishop, nobody actually likes the snow.” After a beat she asked, “Is Lucky friendly? This is Fanny.” She gestured down at the fluffy dog by her side, who was sitting very still, watching her owner carefully.

    A little off-guard, Kate glanced back down at Lucky, who, despite the rumble rising in his throat, thumped his tail against her back with excitement. “Yeah,” she responded. Yelena uttered something Russian to Fanny, who, on command, stood and trotted towards Lucky, sniffing gently and wagging her tail. As soon as Kate let go of Lucky’s collar he took off, bounding through the snow with Fanny right behind him, both dogs barking playfully. Kate watched silently, a lump forming in her throat.

    “Well? Can I sit?”

    Kate blinked, shaking her head and meeting Yelena’s expectant gaze once more, before realizing with a jolt that she was waiting for an answer. “Oh, uh… yeah, yeah, yeah, of course.” She shifted over and patted the spot next to her, casting a swift glance over her shoulder at the window. Of course, Clint had a watchful, wary eye on the two of them. Without wasting any time Yelena made herself comfortable, digging through her tote bag and pulling out a quilt. “Here, Kate Bishop, before you get hypothermia.”

    “It’s not even that cold out- I’m from New York Yelena, I-” Yelena just raised an eyebrow and handed the quilt over. Begrudgingly, Kate wrapped it around her shoulders tightly and grumbled, “Can you please stop using my full name?”

    “I’m not using your full name,” Yelena said innocently. “If I were, I’d call you Katherine Elizabeth Bishop, or Katherine with an i not a y .”

    “Just Kate is fine.”

    “You’re not underage, right?” Kate shook her head, biting back a snark rising in the back of her throat that she didn’t have the energy to deliver. Yelena pulled a can of beer out of her tote and handed it to her, cracking open her own. “Good. I hear New Year’s is boring sober.” “Yeah?” Kate let out a dry chuckle, tracing the lip of the can with her finger absently. Yelena nodded, taking a small sip.

    “Well, I spent a majority of them mind controlled by a homicidal misogynist. Red Room stuff. It’s kind of my first official ‘New Year,’ and I thought we’d spend it bar hopping and watching the big shiny ball drop, but then I found out that you left New York.” Yelena almost sounded a bit hurt. Maybe . It was always hard to tell with her. Kate wasn’t even sure if they were friends, or why she was even here. Yelena went quiet for a moment, staring straight ahead at the dogs, before adding softly, “I was hoping to ring in the new year with Natasha.” A silence fell over them once more. Maybe this was Yelena’s olive branch of sorts, or an invitation to listen if Kate needed to talk. She frowned, pulling the blanket a little tighter and letting her chin rest on her knees again. That strange mix of emotions she kept pushing down was rising up in her throat once more, filling her chest with a near-painful emptiness. “My mom used to throw this huge New Year’s Eve party, and I was always forced to go.” Unsure of why she was telling Yelena this, she shifted uncomfortably. Yelena didn’t say anything, just glanced in her direction to signal that she was listening. “Before my dad died, every year we made this pig out of a lemon and some toothpicks, and we’d cut a slit on one of the pointy ends for a mouth and put a penny in it for good luck.” Kate let out an involuntary, watery chuckle.

    “You did what ?” Yelena snorted. “I have never heard of that. Must be some silly American tradition.” Gently, she asked after a beat, “Why did you stop doing it?” Kate shrugged, her chest tightening. “I guess it was one of those little things that got buried with him.”

    Yelena nodded. “Yeah.” Her voice sounded worlds away, and they both stared sightlessly out at the yard for a moment. Kate dropped her gaze back to the unopened can in her hands, tracing her finger over the pull tab, the aluminum icy cold. The longer she sat outside, the more alone she felt, despite Yelena’s presence. Her chest was hollow, a bitter and burning emptiness eating her from the inside out.

    “It’s weird,” Kate murmured. “I thought I’d be more upset about my mom.” She stilled, taking a deep breath. “I don’t know how I feel, I guess. I miss her, and I’m so… I’m so sad, and I think I’m really angry. But it’s like…” She swallowed, her voice dropping to a whisper. “It’s like… the cops came, and they took her, and once that cruiser drove away I… I was… relieved .” Yelena didn’t say anything. A strained laugh worked its way out of Kate’s throat. “I’ve got to be the worst daughter in the world.”

    “Why do you say that?” Yelena asked gently.

    Kate looked at her incredulously. “Because I had everything,” she told her. “I had a home, and a mother who loved me. I had everything I needed and every opportunity handed to me. My biggest worry was making her proud.” She shook her head, her gaze drifting back to the dogs. “And yet, when it came down to it, I was glad to see her gone.” When Yelena remained quiet, she suddenly felt very uncomfortable. “Sorry,” she whispered. “I- I know I’m being ungrateful. I know I have nothing to complain about.”

    Yelena thought for a moment, then shook her head. “You’re allowed to feel any way you want. It’s none of my business what your relationship with your mother was but it couldn't have been very good.”

    Kate sighed. “When my dad died, I wanted nothing more than to protect her. To be everything she needed.” She stopped, thinking. “She changed, I think. When he died. She was more distant. Cold. But… not always. Like, there were days where I was convinced she was my best friend, and of course days where I could swear she hated my guts. And… and through all of it I never felt good enough. I always felt like I was doing something wrong, no matter what stage we were in.” She thought for a moment. “I always felt like she saw my achievements. It was like an expectation that I would perform. She saw the trophies I brought home, but not me.”

    “For what it’s worth, Kate Bishop, I think you’re more than good enough.” Yelena nudged her, and when Kate looked up and met her gaze she saw nothing but kindness. “And I mean that sincerely. You don’t need to prove yourself to me. Or Clint, for that matter. We see you.” Kate stilled, her vision going blurry. Her hand flew to her mouth as her breaths started to shudder, her cheeks flushing with embarrassment. “S-sor-sorry-” she tried, her voice wet and crackling as she fought the tears flooding her eyes.

    “It’s okay,” Yelena murmured, opening her arms. Without thinking, Kate leaned into her, letting herself melt into Yelena’s arms and burying her face into the Russian’s mustard yellow trench coat. Yelena held her close, rubbing soothing circles on her back as she cried. It was foreign, but not uncomfortable. “Let it out. My Mama says sometimes that’s all you need.” Kate couldn’t remember the last time she had cried. After holding it back all week, it admittedly felt good to let it out, though as she hiccuped one last time her cheeks burned with embarrassment. “Sorry,” she murmured, her voice a thin whisper. Her throat felt raw. “And… and thank you.”

    “Why don’t we call the dogs and go inside?” Yelena suggested gently. “It’s freezing out.” Kate nodded, wiping the last of her tears away. “Yeah,” she conceded. “I guess so.” They had barely stepped foot in the house before Clint was throwing towels at them. “Jesus Christ Kate, it’s 25 degrees outside!” As Yelena kicked off her boots, Clint shut the door behind them, helping Kate out of her jacket and starting to usher the two to the couch. Lucky and Fanny both shook off, sending clumps of snow flying through the room. “Hey Coop, can you grab some towels for the dogs before they flood the living room?”

    “The kids are making some hot cocoa if you girls want some,” Laura offered as they sat down. “Or we have coffee. Or I can make you some tea. Just let me know, okay?” “I’m okay, thank you.” Kate’s voice was still a thin rasp, but she flashed a gracious smile Laura’s way.

    “Hey Clint, do you have any lemons?” Yelena asked. “Kate was telling me about how you Americans make pigs out of them for luck.”

    Clint laughed. “Is that so?”

    “Lemon pig?” Nathaniel laughed. Lila’s face lit up.

    “That sounds fun!” She turned her attention to Kate. “Could you show us?” A small smile curled the corners of Kate’s lips. “Sure,” she agreed softly. “Yeah, I can show you.”
