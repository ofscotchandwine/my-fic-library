“You’re going to need to take the east route.”

Kara’s heart sank in her chest, a heavy pull that settled awkwardly in her gut. It should have been the extra hours that would be added to her journey that ate at her, but it was the mechanical notes of Alex’s voice that she couldn’t shake. She had been desperate to catch a glimpse of her sister, if only across the lines of cars trying to get out of the city.

“We got a group through, but the DEO is bulking up here, pulling over practically every other car,” came the warning. “Sloppy. These fuckers would be better off relying on their dogs,” she grumbled.

Kara shook her head. “Don’t tell them that,” she chuckled under her breath, but Alex would not hear her. Her earpiece had been stripped of the ability to transmit anything—a measure of Lena’s design, making it that much harder to track her.

Swallowing down her disappointment, Kara pulled the leader of her little band aside, her three eyes unblinking, wide as they stared, searching for any scrap of hope where there was none to be had.

“We can’t get through to the south,” Kara relayed.

The woman nodded, but they both knew the toll that would take. The desert was unforgiving to the most prepared traveler, but they were far from that. “We should go,” she urged, a frantic edge in her words.

Kara glanced about their company, and even in the dim lighting of their dank basement hideaway, there was no mistaking the care they would need to take. Among the members of their group was a juvenile white martian, but as he sat against one of the drier stretches of the cement wall, he was still nearly as tall as Kara.

The overlarge leather jacket he wore soothed his frame, but the sleeves were not even long enough to cover his wrists. Kara’s jaw tensed at the raw blue flesh that wreathed the cuff that kept him from shifting, and she could only assume it had been there since the first wave. He would stand out in any crowd, and that limited their options, forced them to go to further lengths to avoid searching eyes.

The martian felt her eyes on him, and shifted lower on the wall, while his shoulders lifted to hide his neck. Kara clicked her tongue at herself as she looked away. She had not meant to draw attention to the challenge he presented for their chances, but most eyes were pointed down, none among them having the energy to do anything but follow orders.

Footsteps above them had a rush of panic circling the group, the range of different heart types all spiking in a cacophony Kara wished she wasn’t growing used to. “It’s just Charles,” she whispered, despite her certainty that they were still safe to talk at a normal volume.

And, true to her word, Charles came down the sagging wooden steps, stooping to poke his head beneath the ridge of the ceiling. “Patrols are light, but I’d wager they’ll be swarming back this way soon enough.”

Kara nodded to acknowledge his report. “It seems they all headed to the south roads.”

Charles’s shallow smile sank into a frown. “You best be heading out, then,” he noted, echoing the group’s leader, and Kara could only sigh and nod again. Their chances would not get any better if they lingered.

With a soft clearing of her throat and a small gesture with her head, the group rose to their feet. They followed Charles up into his tiny kitchen, the old man pushing his table against his fridge to make a bit more room. He imparted a gentle sentiment, a wish of luck, and that was the best they could hope for. The tiny auexian who never parted from the martian’s side was the only one to spare a word of thanks, their webbed fingers flattening against his outstretched palm before he gently shooed them out the door.

“Reach out in a few days,” Charles reminded Kara, but that was far from their first parting and it was always left on the same note.

Kara joined their group in a waiting van, instructing the driver to head east. “Last stop.”

The highway took them miles out of their way before they reached a dirt road that took them into the shelter of a cluster of trees on the edge of the desert. It was a daunting sight, the hill giving them a preview of just how far the unforgiving landscape stretched.

“We need to be quick getting over that hill,” Kara nodded into the distance, “but then we’re mostly in the clear.” And apart from the hollowness of that hope, they ran into no trouble entering the desert. Silence was not necessary, but no one had reason to talk, and perhaps that was the most draining part of the trek.

Walking was agonizing in its own way, the hours stretching like the miles that lay before them, and still Kara knew that the caravan would have a far more grueling march after she left them, when they headed for the Mexican border. As it was, the camp nestled in the hills was only a temporary haven, too small to support more than a handful of companies, and far from truly safe.

The shadows elongated as evening began to overtake the day, the sun low in the sky, when a tug at the shoulder of her cloak stopped Kara short. “Ms. Supergirl,” a small voice drew her attention, her eyes turning up to the white martian. It was rare that anyone used her moniker anymore, but many children still clung to it. If anyone needed the hope, it was them, and Kara couldn’t bring herself to correct them.

The martian shifted nervously, his other hand supporting the sagging auexian child—their usually vibrant aqua coloring dulled to a sickening pale. “They can’t breathe,” he noted, his rasping voice hardly breaking over the howling winds that kicked up dust all around them.

There were no other auexians within the company, and no martians for that matter. Kara’s heart ached for the children—stranded refugees, younger than she had been when she had landed on Earth, and just as alone, but faced with a far harsher future. Her choice had always been to blend in or take up the mantle of her cousin before her, while they had to choose between the unspecified fate of those in DEO custody or fleeing the only home they had known since being forced from their own worlds.

Kara turned her head southwest, before looking back towards the city, calculating the risk of being spotted, knowing she had little choice. She grimaced as she bundled the wheezing little child into her arms. Their gills were shivering weakly, straining in the oppressively arid air. “You should be safe,” she assured the leader, who acknowledged her with a tired nod. “I’m not seeing any patrols around,” she added, more for her own conscience than anything else. “Just follow this range,” she indicated a series of rocky ridges.

With the tiny head cradled in the crook of her neck, Kara touched the arm of the martian. “I’ll get them to water,” she promised, wishing she didn’t have to separate the children.

With a last sigh, Kara took to the sky, traveling ahead of the small caravan, staying low to avoid any scans, but above the clouds of dust that billowed in the wind. The auexian child was almost entirely out of it when she landed, about half a mile from the camp, but still she jogged the final leg to assure that small measure of secrecy.

The group that made it through the south roads had arrived just before her, the camp in full commotion trying to arrange bunks and get nourishment for each of the refugees. A haggard woman who had become a permanent face at the camp, and who Kara recognized as the motherly sort, was the first to acknowledge her arrival. “What does the little one need?”

There was no breath to pause, straight to business, no one able to afford anything else. “Water. They’re an auexian,” Kara explained, handing the child off to the human with the barest relief that care would be given. “There is a martian in the group behind me; the two are close.”

That was all she managed to impart before she was pulled away, another familiar face pointing her north. “The truck ran out of gas a ways back, but we can’t spare any.”

Kara nodded, already starting back out into the desert, a scrappy teenager on her heels.

“How are things?” The girl—Xan—was one of the few to regularly ask her questions. Kara was glad to have something to do with her hands, being a glorified alarm system for the refugees never truly feeling like enough, but it was harder still to never have an answer to offer.

“Not much has changed.”

The words rang hollow. Things rarely changed drastically, not in the way that was easy to point to. Outrage on the behalf of aliens had died down—forgotten soon after the initial legislation that stripped their status as a protected class and resulted in every known alien being documented and cuffed. There had not been a protest in nearly a year, but society did not wake up one day and just decide as one to turn their gaze away from the horrors aliens faced.

It had been a gradual shift—a slow poison that took time to choke the life out of them, the insidiousness not immediately apparent to anyone not directly affected. There had been quiet efforts to narrow the number of species that qualified for sponsors, decided behind closed doors, while pressure was indirectly applied to news outlets to cover ‘both sides’, saturating the conversation with misinformation. Uncertainty fostered fear, which in turn bred mistrust, many preferring to stay out of the controversy.

The changes weren’t so much what was happening—raids in broad daylight, videos circulating of an alien being dragged into vans with the DEO insignia boldly painted on the sides, assurances that the streets were safer for it—but the frequency ticking slower upward, the displays of violent tactics the most appalling thing on the rise.

“The bastards are getting comfortable roughing people up,” Xan grumbled as she jogged to catch up.

“Language,” Kara shot her a scrunched little look, but the girl just rolled her eyes. “They certainly aren’t being subtle.” And the world was growing increasingly numbed to them.

“I wish you could give them a good thrashing, make them scared to show their faces,” the girl admitted, kicking a small stone down their path, and as Kara watched it skip off into the shadow of the hollow trunk of a tree, she could not deny she wished the same.

“If only it were that simple,” she exhaled.

Xan was uncharacteristically quiet after that, but soon enough they located the truck, the canvas over the bed tied down hastily when it had been abandoned. “I’m relying on you to steer,” Kara reminded, and the girl gave her a dramatic salute.

With the girl at the wheel, Kara pushed the truck back along the path they had taken. The group Kara had been accompanying was reaching the camp just as she pushed the vehicle up over the ridge into the perimeter, but the first chirp of her alarm sounded in her ear, a soft curse passing her lips as she checked the time. She had let the day get too late, and couldn’t linger to say any goodbyes.

Kara knew she would have to fly back, groaning inwardly at her own carelessness, her desire to do as much as she could for the fleeing aliens overshadowing the precautions she always intended to keep in place. She flew to the west, circling the city to enter through the bay side, tacking on more and more time to her trip.

The chime in Kara’s ear was growing more incessant, the intervals of silence shrinking until the gentleness of the warning note could not cushion the way it grated against the inside of her skull. She flew faster, but even the roar of the wind could not dampen that anxiety that rose with the frequency, until her mind was buzzing as much as her earpiece.

Kara skidded to a sloppy halt, just inches from the glass door of the LCorp balcony, the door yanked near clear of its hinges as she threw herself into the otherwise quiet space, Lena’s typing a dull tap beneath her own heart. Finally her alarm went silent, just in time for her earpiece to announce: curfew now in place.

“Cutting it close,” came the short comment, Lena’s head not lifting away from her work, and Kara moved to stand behind her, over one shoulder, postured as if engaged in the same task.

“We’re getting a visit,” she grumbled, the too familiar troop of footsteps approaching from the elevator.

“Put your hood up, love,” Lena spared her the briefest glance, hardly a greeting. Her smile was tight—thin and exhausted, unable to compete with the shadows that deepened in the sunkenness of her eyes. “Your hair is dusted.”

Kara acknowledged the advice with a nod and patted down her front before carefully lifting her hood up. “Still the cutest hero you know?” The question earned her a genuine little grin, an exhaled puff of a chuckle worth the corniness she held on to. It was a relic from a time when she was considered a hero—when one of her greatest fears was that she and Lena would not last—and yet it was one of the few things to survive.

“Always,” Lena echoed the line she always offered.

They were not given the chance to appreciate the breath of old comfort, the unceremonious impact of the doors to Lena’s office hitting the stoppers meant to protect the walls jarred them to attention. “This is the DEO,” the first agent announced, far louder than was necessary, his voice overfilling the office, smothering any pretense of comfort. They weren’t there to be pleasant. “Present your documentation, Alien 111181.”

Kara’s jaw tightened. “Is that really necessary? We’ve done this dance before,” she reminded.

“It’s procedure, Alien 111181.”

That agent’s face was not one Kara knew, but her eyes were on the commanding officer among the group, his twisted smirk visible beneath his scruff, the gleam in his eyes delighting in the insistence of his underlying, and the inconvenience it imposed on their mark. Or perhaps the humiliation.

Kara fished the identification card she had been granted from her back pocket and handed it to the nameless agent, the only markings on his uniform the DEO insignia, the letters printed on his chest and either shoulder.

“Care to explain why you have barged into my office?” Lena addressed the commanding officer.

“We apologize for the intrusion, Ms. Luthor,” the agent handed Kara back her card as he turned to answer, leaving the commander to smirk in the background. “There were reports of unauthorized use of flight entering the city border.”

“There are a lot of people with the ability to fly,” Kara huffed.

“I did not address you, Alien 111181,” the agent intoned. It got under Kara’s skin more than even the sadistic enjoyment the commander brazenly displayed, the calm neutrality of the words quietly insidious. The agent felt entirely too comfortable with the casual belittlement of aliens. He felt entirely justified in his actions. “We have good reason to believe it,” his hand lifted to indicate Kara, “was the alien in the reports.”

“Excuse me?” Kara’s cheeks flamed hot, her chest tight around the words, her mind reeling. She hadn’t even realized the fire reached her eyes, blazing outward, shadows dancing as the air around her spat and hissed.

Weapons were drawn and pointed without hesitation, provoking a snarl to tug at her lip, her hackles high, her fingers itching to tighten into fists.

Lena’s hand moved to Kara’s wrist, a show of restraining her. What the agents wouldn’t see was the way Lena’s fingers trailed lower to sit against the inside of her palm, the smallest gesture of comfort, reinforcing the tether that was moments from snapping.

Some days it seemed that Lena was the only reason her anger—a beast that raged in her chest, her ribs a crumbling cage—didn’t devour her from the inside out. The fire that licked across her cheeks was just what spilled outward, what she could not swallow down.

The heat retreated inward, into the dark hollow she hid away in, emptiness a necessary toll, but one she paid only because Lena stood beside her.

“We have first-hand accounts of it landing on your balcony within minutes of the reports,” the agent stated simply.

“That doesn’t answer my question,” Lena sneered, and Kara could feel the disdain in her gaze without being the subject of it. “I shouldn’t have to remind you that Kara has provisional permissions to use flight, one of those being in an effort to meet her curfew.”

“Any suspicious alien activity is subject to—”

Lena was not in the mood to let the man finish. “Surely you recall that your raids require sufficient evidence, and stalking is in direct violation of the DEO statutes when gathering evidence against a lawfully sponsored alien, as defined by National City v Kara Zor-El.”

The man faltered, but as Lena lifted a remote to turn on a series of monitors, all the agents began to shift uncomfortably. There would be no breath of relief, and the rules of engagement were more likely to tip in the DEO’s favor the next time a raid brought them face to face.

Afterall, the point was never to land the charge of unauthorized flight. The point was only ever to wear them down, to remind them that they were always being watched, to exhaust them until they handed the agents the leverage they needed. But at least they had the night.

Kara stalked the length of Lena’s office, as much a caged animal as she was a shell of herself. She was hardly a person, but neither was she a symbol. She was a battleground. By handing over her identity, she had become the center of disputes over the rights aliens could have. She could hear the commotion all around her, calls for help she no longer had the freedom to answer, and the struggle to ignore each one rang with echoes of her early years on Earth, when she had just as little control.

For all her power, Kara was helpless. Time budged inch by inch in a seemingly endless game of waiting, without her knowing if a shoe would drop to finally push the needle towards some tangible fight, or if her efforts to stem the bleeding would ultimately make her witness to the carnage—fated to be the last to feel the sting of the blade, but the one to drown in all that was spilled before her.

“Kara?”

She was pushed to walk faster, prodded by a looming panic, with no sense that she could outrun it. At every turn she questioned if there was more she could spare, more of herself that she could give, and yet she was cornered, trapped within her own existence, the walls just an extension of her ribs, squeezing down around her lungs, her heart, her hope.

Her vision narrowed until she wasn’t registering what she saw, only watching the scene blur past her as she pivoted to retrace her steps, again and again, nausea rising in her wake, gaining on her despite her efforts. There was no escape. Her blood roared in her ears, and it was not enough to silence the howls and pleas and suffering that was not hers, but for all it split her skull and burrowed deeper, it might as well have been.

Hands gingerly rose, first to push back her hood and then to hold her steady, drawing her focus to the pleasant warmth of palms against her cheeks. Lena’s answer came as she sat beside Kara, the gentle shake of her head dismissing the need for concern over leaving her work for later. Fingers trailed along Kara’s shoulder and up the back of her neck, the touch reminding her muscles that they could relax, and her head tipped back sharply into the cradle of Lena’s hand.

The fuzzy darkness that clawed at her vision was blanketed by heavy lids, drooping low, not quite closing until she felt lips against her jaw.

Kara drifted in a sea of sickness, dark, turbulent waters lapping all around her, but she was held afloat by Lena’s hand. She let her mind be pulled towards Lena’s lips, the gentle press a welcome distraction, while soft coos encouraged her heart to settle.

When Kara lifted her head, the world was quieter, but far from silent. Lena’s hand stayed at the base of her neck, kept them tethered to each other. It was not the first time they had found themselves in those positions, but that made it no easier for Kara to swallow.

“Do you ever regret signing up to be my babysitter?” She chuckled the question like it was nothing more than a joke, but as it was allowed to hang in the air, the ring of it distorted into something that stole the breath from her lungs, left her empty and paralyzed, helpless to fill the hollowness.

Kara almost didn’t want to look at Lena, not wanting to know how terribly her own hurt stung when it spilled outward, but when she finally turned her head, she was met with only a soft frown and that unwavering warmth that was too often hidden beneath a piercing green stare.

“You know that isn’t how I see things.”

And she did know.

Kara had never asked for Lena to be her sponsor; it had been offered freely; readily; fought for against her lawyers’ insistence that it would be better to cut her ties to an alien during such uncertain times. Lena was always there for her, with her—not as a babysitter, but as a partner—holding her hand as her world collapsed around them.

Lena was there when Kara weighed her responsibility to the world, and ultimately relinquished her life as Kara Danvers, to be thrust into the center of public discourse about aliens; Lena was there when Kara was barred from the courtroom as they awaited the ruling on Alex’s application to be a sponsor, and she was the one whole held Kara together when the call came to let her know that Alex was denied; Lena was there when their lawyers advised that Kara never be seen with her sister, in case it gave any illusion of her subverting the law she positioned herself to be adhering to.

Kara had not heard from J’onn or M’gann since they escaped down to Mexico, working to establish a network for receiving fleeing aliens. Kara’s communication was heavily monitored, and they could not risk exposing her involvement in any effort to aid refugees, not when so much rode on her image as a ‘model’ alien.

Nia had thrown herself into her work at CatCo, even as the neutralizing cuffs she was forced to wear in exchange for claiming her human heritage weighed more than either of them could have predicted. Kara only saw her when a piece she was chasing brought her to Lena’s door, and each time she looked more exhausted than the last, sleep no longer offering any relief.

No one had heard directly from Brainy since the first wave, but his cryptic messages were a hollow replacement for his presence. He had predicted that his capture would spell the death of all hope, and none of them could deny the fear that if the DEO was given the chance, he would be used as a weapon against them all.

But even as the Superfriends were rent from each other’s lives, Lena was there with Kara. Always.

“Sometimes I regret it,” Kara murmured. “Not the part where I’m with you,” she rushed to clarify, her brow crinkling at the poor segue. “Just, you know, coming out… I know it was right, and I know that it helps so, so many people,” she sighed, “but I—”

Kara swallowed. She couldn’t truly give voice to all that she had given up, all that she mourned, not when it paled against what was forcefully stripped from others. The shackles that she wore were one of the last defenses against far more suffering.

“It feels selfish.”

Lena exhaled a sad chuckle and leaned her forehead against Kara’s temple. “I know.” Fingers ghosted along her forearm, over her bare wrist, and slipped between her own where they rested on her thigh. “But there is nothing more you can do tonight,” she reminded, “and you can’t burn yourself out chasing a fix that isn’t there. You need to rest, or you’re no help to anyone.”

Compelled to hold Lena, Kara’s hand lifted and turned, taking those fingers in her gentle grip, her thumb brushing absently over knuckles. Her head was not so chaotic when Lena’s voice guided her thoughts.

“I— um, what?”

The rarity of Lena’s confusion tickled Kara enough to almost laugh, if only the heaviness of her ribs would allow it, but the hint of a smile withered before it had fully taken form on her lips. “It’s a kryptonian custom.” Her eyes never left Lena’s hand, but she had sunk into her own head, swallowing down the memory of the box she put away, buried with the plans she had made to present it to Lena. “Our version of rings.”

“Rings,” Lena echoed in a breathy whisper.

“I was going to ask— I,” the words caught in Kara’s throat. She had tried to forget it, to put it aside so she could focus on the duty she had to the world, but more than a year had been devoted entirely to the cause, and it wasn’t just time that slipped through her fingers. Her hope for the future had begun to erode. She had so little to look forward to, and less every day. “I was going to ask you to marry me,” she choked out with a soft sob, “but now, I— I don’t know if it will ever be possible.”

“Oh, Kara,” Lena breathed, “yes.” Kara’s chest nearly lurched with how tightly her heart squeezed. “Of course I will marry you.”

Elation clashed with the nauseous recognition of the lost opportunity, and all Kara could do was fight the urge to blubber. She wasn’t wholly attached to being married under the law, but she could no longer imagine everyone she loved gathering in the same room to celebrate—and to her, that did matter.

“Kara?” A little hummed note of acknowledgement was all Kara could muster. “Hey,” Lena’s hand slipped out of hers and lifted to her cheek to guide her head to turn. “You’re going to be my wife.” The slight uptick of Lena’s heart—the little dance of excitement—called Kara’s to mimic it. She allowed herself to smile and to meet Lena’s teary-eyed gaze fully.

“Your wife,” Kara teased the words across her tongue, delighted by the sound of them.

When Lena smiled—the gentle crack in the mask Kara was so used to seeing which ushered the rise of a blush to melt the rest away—nothing else mattered. The creases at the corners of Lena’s eyes reminded her of the streaks of light that wreathed the yellow sun when she floated above the clouds, and her smile was just as radiant, captivating enough to forget that there was a world at her back.

“Let’s make tonight about us,” Lena proposed. “Whatever the future brings, we deserve one night to be selfish, don’t you think?”

Lena had never been easy to say no to, and that was before Kara had starved herself of all thought of indulgence. They shared a bed every night, curled into each other for comfort through the relentless nightmares, but hands never wandered. Not in a long time. They were too busy holding on to the last of what they were afraid to lose.

But they could have one night. One night to pretend. One night to look no further than the space they occupied. One night to forget what awaited them.

“Well, I’m not allowed to leave your side until morning,” Kara exhaled the words before they had fully formed in her head and followed them with a breathless chuckle. It was far from her grandest display of wit, but she was ready to abandon it completely—to not think, to not worry, to only exist, with Lena.

“That’s right; you’re all mine.”

The confirmation—with its heavy notes that sank low in her belly and stirred up a dormant warmth—sent a shiver down Kara’s spine, and Lena’s fingers chased lower until the back of the couch intercepted her.

Distantly, Kara recalled the fire she once housed, the one that licked at her ribs and scorched through her veins, the one she loosed--with tongue and lips--to devour. Her dreams still wandered through the moments that formed the kindling of what they shared, the time she had told herself she never took for granted--after they had built back the foundation that had crumbled under the weight of the secret of her identity. There were no longer lies between them to keep them from enjoying each other; instead there was Kara’s exhaustion, distraction, and the numbness she used to combat the rage that she harbored.

Kara hadn’t fully realized how much she missed Lena, yet another ache in her life she had grown numb to. It was never more apparent than when Lena was so close, within her reach. Right there.

She spent every day in Lena’s company, but the tether between them was not one they chose for themselves. It didn’t feel like it was theirs. Not the way Kara wanted.
