    AGNES THE VAMPIRE SLAYER A Woss Jhedon Production Agnes is almost done with the evening’s patrol when she spots her slouched against a moss-covered gravestone: clad in skintight leather, the most fearsome vampire ever to darken Westview’s monster-laden streets. (The most irritating, too.)

    “Vidal,” Agnes says, twirling her stake between her fingers. Her voice carries over the empty cemetery, disturbing the chorus of crickets. “Don’t tell me you’re visiting your own grave. That’s just sad.”

    Vidal grins. Her pointed teeth catch the moonlight. “Oh, please,” she says, brushing a bit of dirt from the name carved into the gravestone. “I wouldn’t be caught dead dying in this town. I’m just checking up on one of the locals.”

    V - I, read the first two letters of the gravestone. Vidal, obviously. What a stupid thing to lie about, when Agnes is staring the grave right in the face. Maybe Vidal got dumber when the vampirization process stopped the flow of blood to her brain.

    But as more dirt falls from name, the full breadth of letters comes into view: V - I - S - I - O -

    “I heard this one has a tendency to come back,” Vidal says, tracing the indents in the stone with a sharpened nail. “Might want to work him into your patrolling route, Winters.”

    “You know who else has a tendency to come back?” Agnes says. “Half the freaking town, since the Hellmouth opened up. This guy ain’t special.”

    “Don’t let his wife hear you say that.” Vidal takes a casual step forward, eyes flicking between Agnes and the stake. “Where’s the rest of the Scooby Gang? Or do they actually get invited to stuff on a Saturday night, unlike you?”

    “I work alone,” Agnes says, tapping the stake against her chin. “Unless I’ve got a group project for English. In which case, I’m a reluctant team player.” “Right, high school. You …are supposed to be in high school. The oldest sophomore that Westview High’s ever seen.” Vidal makes a grab for the stake; Agnes hoists it out of reach. “You don’t work alone, Agnes. You’ve got two quirky friends that follow you everywhere you go. That’s a staple of the genre.”

    Agnes shouldn’t have skipped her usual pre-patrol coffee. Her head swims with indistinct memories. Two friends, yes; a boy and a girl. Perpetually flanking her as she rids Westview of its many supernatural baddies. There’s the boy, so generic that he’s hardly worth remembering. But the girl – “Aw, I love that face,” Vidal says, reaching again for the stake. Agnes retreats back a step to elude her grasp, nearly tripping over that mystery grave. “That’s your I’ve almost got it face. It’s adorable.”

    The girl. A redhead, she thinks – scarlet hair, falling just past her shoulders. Her name starts with a W. Agnes is sure of it.

    “Maybe this’ll jog your memory,” Vidal says, her face rippling into its true vampire form as she shoves Agnes square in the chest.

    It’s a rookie move, losing herself in memory while an enemy stands within stabbing distance. It’s the sort of thing that Agnes used to do during her freshman year, which was – last – was it last year? Is she a sophomore?

    Diving back into her brain, Agnes can’t recall when she started high school. Her memories are all small episodic adventures: clearing zombies from the cafeteria, evicting a den of werewolves who had nested in the boys’ locker room. Running into Vidal in the spookiest hours of the night. Those might be her favorite memories out of the bunch.

    But there’s no time to get sentimental. Vidal’s knee is digging into her ribs, her hands doing their best to pin Agnes’s arms to the overgrown grass, but the stake is still clutched tight in Agnes’s fist – with a strategic burst of strength, Agnes manages to flip Vidal onto her back, driving the stake toward her heart.

    (Toward the place where her heart should be, if she had one.)

    Vidal catches Agnes’s wrist in the nick of time; the stake buries a centimeter into her skin, drawing a thin stream of blood. “Crazy bitch,” she huffs, her arms shaking with the effort of keeping the stake from driving any deeper. “Even without your powers, you’re still – so – fucking – annoying – ”

    She punctuates each word with an attempt to buck Agnes off. Agnes holds steady, keeping the stake hovering just over Vidal’s chest.

    “‘Without my powers?’” Agnes repeats. One moment of weakness, one miscalculation on the vampire’s part, and the wood will find its mark. Vidal will be reduced to dust and memory, and Agnes can finally get some peace and quiet during her nightly strolls. “I’m the slayer, sweetheart. The chosen one. Look up ‘powerful’ in your nearest dictionary and all you’ll see is my magnificent mug.”

    “Then why can’t you kill me?” Vidal asks. She releases Agnes’s wrist.

    Talk about amateur hour. Agnes brings the stake down in an elegant arc.

    “Oh, I very much can kill – ”

    But something stills her hand mid-swing, the stake’s point stopping a hair’s breadth from Vidal’s chest. A gut feeling, churning and rancid; a flashing red light from the depths of her psyche. Don’t, the feeling says. You’re not allowed.

    “There,” Vidal breathes, tracing a sharpened nail up Agnes’s forearm.

    “You’ve almost got it, babe. Who are you?”

    She’s Agnes Winters. Snarky, sexy vampire slayer. Forty-something high school sophomore. Westview’s her home; Westview’s always been her home. In fact, she hasn’t left the town since – she hasn’t – “The girl,” Agnes says, letting the stake fall to the ground. “The redhead. Who – ”

    “Now, she’s the chosen one,” Vidal says, threading a hand through Agnes’s hair to pull her closer. Her breath smells of petrichor. “Kind of bold to make yourself Buffy, honestly. Feels like that’s more the Scarlet Witch’s style, being the tortured heroine and all. But we’re not talking about her. Who are you?”

    Don’t hurt her, the feeling says. Agnes doesn’t want to. With Vidal’s hand in her hair, Vidal’s body so un-vampirically warm under hers, Agnes doesn’t want to hurt her at all.

    “I’m a girl who’s up way past my bedtime,” Agnes says, and rock music blares as the credits start to roll.

As much as Agnes hates to admit it, Rio’s been a good addition to the show: she’s funny, mean, and willing to beef with anything that moves. Their ratings hit a tasty spike after Rio accused Mrs. Hart of trying to steal her man at Agnes’s last party.

Being scream-at- a-waiter crazy is fine, if not preferable; being talk-nonsense-and-scare- people crazy is not. “Maybe you should call it a night, hun,” Agnes says. “You’re sounding even less coherent than usual.”

    “Not that this isn’t fun,” Rio says, delicately plucking another glass from a passing waiter’s tray. It’s like she’s not listening to Agnes at all. “It’s a real hoot, watching you humiliate yourself in front of these suburban simpletons. But it’s not a fair fight if your brain is permanently scrambled eggs, is it?”

Even without the cameras around, she’ll never pass up an opportunity to practice her hair-toss to perfection. “Yours seems like it’s on the fritz, considering you’re insulting your de facto boss. This is my show, Rio. If I don’t like you, you’re out.”

    “Right,” Rio says. Her eyes flick over Agnes, head to toe. Agnes hates it when she does that. It feels like some inscrutable power move, meant to throw Agnes off her game. “It’s always your show, Agnes. And yet you keep making me a main character.”

    A VERY WESTVIEW CHRISTMAS An Original Made-For-TV Movie Only on the Hallmark Channel “Hey there, neighbor!” Agnes beams, pausing the battle against her garden’s weeds to greet the new face on the block.

    The woman pauses on the second step of her stoop, her arms overflowing with cardboard boxes. “Hey there,” she says. “You must be Agnes.”

    “I guess I must!” Agnes stands, brushing the soil from her pants. Compared to the woman’s outfit – a crisp suit, tailored and creaseless – Agnes’s gardening getup might as well be rags. “Boy, am I glad to see somebody parking their keister in that house again. I haven’t had a next-door buddy on the ol’ street since – ”

    Since – “Consider me parked,” the woman says, depositing her boxes on the stoop.

    Adjusting her suit collar, she extends her hand over the fence. “I’m Rio.” “Last name de Janeiro, I hope,” Agnes says, shaking Rio’s hand enthusiastically. “Kidding. But if you happen to make a mean caipirinha, I wouldn’t turn one down.”

The sound that emerges from Agnes’s throat is unbefitting of her dignified station as the head of Westview’s Christmas Cheer Committee.

    Agnes retracts her hand. The nicks and cuts have disappeared; all that adorns her knuckles is a smattering of dirt. “Something tells me you’re gonna be a real firecracker at our block parties,” Agnes says, clearing her throat. “What brings you to Westview, anyhow?”

    “Work,” Rio says. “I’m a lawyer over in New York. Our next case is representing a union of overworked mall Santas, so my boss sent me to this podunk town to learn about the true meaning of Christmas.”

    “Well, you came to the right place, sister. I’ve lived in Westview all my life, and you’ve never seen a town more bitten by the Christmas spirit bug. We’ll have you ho-ho-ho-ing in no time.”

    “I don’t know,” Rio says, crossing her arms. “What could I, a big-city lawyer, learn from you, a Christmas-loving townie with a heart of gold?”

    “Why don’t we find out over that drink?” Agnes says, flexing her oddly cut- free hand.

    Westview is at its most lovely, strewn in multicolored string lights – all the colors of the rainbow, twinkling from each house’s trees and bushes. Plastic reindeer perch atop the roofs, blown-up Frosty the Snowmen waving from the front yards. It truly is, Agnes thinks, the most wonderful time of the year.

    (It’s also June, but that’s besides the point. In Westview, it’s essentially Christmas year-round.)

    “Don’t you see, Rio?” Agnes says as the pair meanders down the avenue, holding hands. The smell of gingerbread wafts from nowhere. “All those big-city lawyer problems are distracting you from what’s really important: Christmas. And family. And love.”

    “I see that now, Agnes,” Rio says. “I guess I could tell my lawyer boss that I need a little extra time on this assignment. Maybe you can teach me that Christmas carol you’re always humming.”

    Agnes knows the one she means. Despite humming that pesky earworm nearly 24/7, Agnes can’t seem to remember the words past the first line: Seekest thou the road…

    “Anything.”

    “These movies usually have villains,” Rio says. She’s doing that thing again – looking at Agnes so intensely that it feels like a physical probe. “You could’ve made me the corporate businessman who wants to bulldoze the Christmas tree farm to put up a bank, or whatever. Her silly lawyer, always spouting some sort of nonsense. “You blew in from your fancy New York life and stole my heart, and there wasn’t a darn thing I could do about it.     Every day is Christmas.

When Agnes has those strange night terrors, dreaming of being shut up in a cage made of horrible red light, Rio holds her until she calms down. When Agnes starts crying at the nativity scene, looking down at the little baby Jesus, Rio makes her laugh with a joke about preferring the pagan holidays, anyway. It all becomes a little more bearable, with Rio there.

You should’ve changed the channel by now.”

    “There she is,” Rio purrs, spidering a hand up Agnes’s arm to cup her cheek. “There’s my girl. I knew you were still in there.”

    Agnes shakes off the touch. An ancient rage yowls in her chest, longing to execute deeply un-Hallmark acts. To tear Rio limb from lithe limb; to raze the rest of the planet so Rio has nowhere else to go. Agnes wants to suck the marrow from this worthless town and then leave it to its swift decay.     “I know,” Rio says, both hands cradling Agnes’s cheeks. (Agnes might’ve said that last part out loud.) “You will. There’s just one mystery to sort through first. And I don’t think a Hallmark backlot is the place to do it.” A mystery. Agnes feels the ground shift beneath her feet, the walls growing HBO-gray. The Christmas decorations fade, replaced by a police badge and a pile of unsolved cases.

    Her vision muddles, a powerful headache racking her skull. She could really use a quick nap before she meets Herb at the crime scene out near Eastview.

    “Crime drama,” Rio says with a smirk. “You’re so fucking literal. Alright, detective. I’ll see you at the station.”

    “Wait,” Agnes says, catching Rio’s arm as her consciousness ebbs. “Don’t – don’t leave me, Rio. Please.”

    “Not planning on it, my love,” Rio says, kissing Agnes in a way that briefly makes the whole horrible ever-shifting world make sense.

    And then Agnes is alone with her gritty past and her crime-ridden present. According to her wristwatch, she’s really got to get a move on. That Jane Doe isn’t going to identify herself.
